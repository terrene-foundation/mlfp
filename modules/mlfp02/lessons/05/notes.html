Speaker Notes — Lesson 2.5 Linear Regression

Total time: ~60 minutes  ·  17 slides  ·  ~3.5 min per slide average

The first modelling lesson — and the one where statistics becomes machine learning. The derivation is worth doing properly: squared loss, gradient, normal equations, and the closed form. Everything in M3's supervised learning is this lesson with a bigger hammer. Budget extra time; this is a 60-minute slot.

Slide 1~1 minTitle

Linear Regression

Open with the bridge from 2.4: "A/B tests compare two groups. But HDB prices don't come in two groups — floor area varies continuously, and so does price. Today: the model that predicts a number from other numbers. OLS, the closed form, $t$-stats, $R^2$, the $F$-stat, and dummy encoding."

→ "The model itself."
Slide 2~3 minThe model

The model

Read the equation aloud, then the three bullets. $\beta_0$: expected $y$ when all predictors are zero — often not interpretable on its own. $\beta_j$: change in $y$ per unit change in $x_j$, holding the other predictors constant — underline that clause; it's the ceteris paribus that comes back at the end of the lesson. $\varepsilon_i$: everything the linear combination can't capture.

"This is a strong claim: the world is a weighted sum plus noise. Sometimes it's approximately right, and approximate is incredibly useful."

→ "The picture that explains the name."
Slide 3~3 minThe picture

The picture

Walk the figure: teal points are the data, the amber line is the best-fit line, and the dashed red segment is one residual — the vertical gap between a point and the line.

"OLS — ordinary least squares — chooses the line that minimises the sum of squared vertical distances. Why squared? It punishes big misses quadratically, it makes the maths solvable in closed form, and under Normal errors it's exactly MLE — which connects today to 2.2."

If beginners ask why not absolute distance: "That's a real method — least absolute deviations, robust to outliers — but it has no closed form. Squared error gives us the formula on the next slides."
→ "Let's derive it."
Slide 4~3 minThe loss

Deriving OLS — the loss

Stack the equations: $y = X\beta + \varepsilon$, where each row of $X$ is one observation. The loss is the sum of squared residuals, $S(\beta) = (y - X\beta)^\top(y - X\beta)$.

Expand the product slowly: $y^\top y - 2\beta^\top X^\top y + \beta^\top X^\top X \beta$. "Three terms: a constant, a linear term in $\beta$, and a quadratic term. A quadratic bowl — which is why there's exactly one minimum."

→ "Set the gradient to zero."
Slide 5~4 minNormal equations

Deriving OLS — setting the gradient to zero

Differentiate term by term: the constant vanishes, the linear term gives $-2X^\top y$, the quadratic gives $2X^\top X\beta$. Set to zero and you have the normal equations: $X^\top X \beta = X^\top y$.

Then the box: if $X^\top X$ is invertible, $\hat\beta = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top y$. "This is arguably the most important formula in applied statistics. Say the caveat out loud: if $X^\top X$ is invertible — that's the multicollinearity failure we'll meet in the mistakes slide."

For experts: nobody inverts the matrix in practice — $QR$ or SVD factorisations are numerically stable. numpy's solve uses LU. Same answer, better arithmetic.
→ "Point estimates aren't enough — how certain are we?"
Slide 6~3 minStandard errors

Standard errors of $\hat\beta$

Under Normal errors, $\hat\beta$ is itself a random vector with covariance $\sigma^2 (X^\top X)^{-1}$. We don't know $\sigma^2$, so estimate it from the residuals: $\hat\sigma^2 = e^\top e / (n - k - 1)$.

Pause on the denominator: "$n - k - 1$ — the general Bessel correction from 2.2. We spent $k+1$ degrees of freedom estimating the coefficients, so the residual sum of squares gets divided by what's left. The diagonal of $\hat\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}$ gives the squared standard errors."

→ "One $t$-stat per coefficient."
Slide 7~3 mint-statistics

The $t$-statistic for each coefficient

Walk the formula: $t_j = \hat\beta_j / \text{SE}(\hat\beta_j)$, distributed $t_{n-k-1}$ under the null that the true coefficient is zero. "Large $|t_j|$ means the estimate is many standard errors from zero — strong evidence this predictor matters."

Give the rule of thumb with its warning: "$|t| > 2$ corresponds roughly to $p < 0.05$. Fine for a quick scan of a regression table; but always report the exact p-value."

→ "How much of the variance does the model explain?"
Slide 8~3 minR²

$R^2$: proportion of variance explained

Define the two sums of squares: $\text{SS}_{\text{tot}}$ is the variance of $y$ around its own mean; $\text{SS}_{\text{res}}$ is what's left after the model. $R^2$ is the fraction explained. "An $R^2$ of 0.83 means the model explains 83% of the price variation — the rest is noise, omitted variables, and non-linearity."

Then the trap, stated on the slide: "$R^2$ only goes up as you add predictors — even useless ones, because the model can always fit a little more noise. Adjusted $R^2$ penalises complexity with the $(n-1)/(n-k-1)$ factor. Always report adjusted."

→ "One number for the whole model."
Slide 9~2 minF-statistic

$F$-statistic: model vs intercept-only

"The $t$-stats test coefficients one at a time. The $F$-stat tests the whole model at once: is this better than just predicting $\bar{y}$ for everyone?" Walk the formula — regression variance per predictor over residual variance per remaining degree of freedom. Under the null that all slopes are zero, $F \sim F_{k,\,n-k-1}$.

Read the green box: "Large $F$ → small p-value → the model is doing something beyond guessing the mean."

→ "How do towns — categories — enter a linear model?"
Slide 10~3 minDummy encoding

Categorical variables — dummy encoding

Walk the figure. The flat_type column — 3-room, 4-room, 5-room, executive — becomes three indicator columns. Each row lights up exactly one. Notice 3-room has no column: it's the base level, absorbed into the intercept.

Read the caption carefully — this is the interpretation students get wrong: "The coefficient on is_4room is the price difference between 4-room and 3-room — the base — not some absolute 4-room effect. And we drop one level deliberately: with all four columns plus an intercept, the columns would sum to the intercept and $X^\top X$ would be singular."

→ "Worked example on real HDB data."
Slide 11~4 minWorked load

Worked: HDB load and design matrix

Walk the code. Load the HDB resale parquet. First the filter: drop the sentinel prices — the planted 10-dollar and 9-million-dollar rows you met in M1 — keeping sales between 100K and 2M. Then feature engineering: flat age from the sale year minus lease commencement, and log_price as the target.

"Why log price? Raw price is right-skewed with multiplicative errors — a 5% error on a million-dollar flat dwarfs a 5% error on a small flat. Logging makes errors additive and coefficients become proportional effects." Then to_dummies on town with drop_first=True, assemble $X$ and $y$, and add the intercept column of ones.

→ "Solve it — four lines."
Slide 12~3 minWorked solve

Worked: solve the normal equations

Walk the code. $X^\top X$, $X^\top y$, one np.linalg.solve — that's $\hat\beta$. Then standard errors: residuals, $\hat\sigma^2$ with the $n - p$ denominator, square root of the diagonal of $\hat\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}$, and the $t$-stats as coefficient over SE.

Read the footer with pride: "That's the core of OLS — four lines. Everything else in every regression textbook is interpretation and assumption-checking."

→ "How good is the fit?"
Slide 13~3 minWorked R² and F

Worked: R² and F

Walk the code: total and residual sums of squares, $R^2$, adjusted $R^2$, and $F$ built directly from $R^2$.

Report the numbers on the slide: this fit — 49,899 sales after cleaning — gives $R^2 \approx 0.83$ and $F \approx 48{,}000$ with a p-value effectively zero. Then the caveat that matters at this sample size: "With $n$ this large, everything is statistically significant. Read $R^2$ and effect sizes, not just p-values. Significance is cheap; importance is what you're after."

→ "Now read the coefficients."
Slide 14~4 minInterpretation

Interpreting coefficients (log-price)

Walk the table. Area: $\hat\beta = 0.0112$ with $t \approx 490$ — each additional square metre adds about 1.1% to price. Age: $\hat\beta \approx 0.00002$, $t \approx 0.6$ — no detectable age effect once area is held fixed. Bishan: $\hat\beta = 0.119$, $t \approx 33$ — and since 0.119 isn't tiny, use the exact transform: $e^{0.119} - 1 \approx 13\%$ premium over the base, which is every town not in the model.

Then the two footnotes. "With a log outcome, small $\beta$ is approximately the proportional change; use $e^\beta - 1$ when it's not small. And ceteris paribus: every coefficient means 'holding the other predictors constant'. Drop any predictor and every remaining coefficient can change."

If beginners are surprised age doesn't matter: "Age is correlated with area and town — older estates have smaller flats in certain towns. Once those are in the model, age's independent contribution is tiny. That's ceteris paribus in action, not a claim that old flats sell like new ones."
→ "Straight lines aren't the whole story."
Slide 15~2 minNon-linearity

Non-linearity without leaving OLS

Walk the three tricks. Polynomial: add $x^2$ for curvature — diminishing returns of floor area. Interaction: $x_1 \cdot x_2$ — the effect of area depends on town. Log transform: $\log y$ when $y$ is skewed — you just did this.

Land the punchline from the slide: "'Linear' means linear in the coefficients, not in the predictors. A quadratic in $x$ is still a linear model — and OLS still solves it in closed form."

→ "Three ways regression goes wrong."
Slide 16~2 minRookie mistakes

Three rookie mistakes

Walk the three red boxes. One: interpreting coefficients in isolation — they depend on the whole model; add or drop a predictor and they move. Two: chasing $R^2$ — it only goes up; use adjusted $R^2$ and cross-validation. Three: multicollinearity — correlated predictors make $X^\top X$ nearly singular, and standard errors explode. "Check VIF when predictors are correlated — if two columns carry the same information, OLS can't decide how to split the credit."

→ "Recap and bridge."
Slide 17~2 minRecap

What you can now do

Walk the bullets: derive OLS from squared loss, solve the normal equations in numpy, compute SEs and $t$-stats from $(X^\top X)^{-1}$, evaluate with $R^2$/adjusted/$F$, dummy-encode with ceteris paribus interpretation, and add polynomial and interaction terms. Tease 2.6: "Today the outcome was a number. Tomorrow it's a yes/no — will this flat sell above median? Logistic regression is OLS's other half."
