Speaker Notes — Lesson 2.6 Logistic Regression and Classification

Total time: ~60 minutes  ·  19 slides  ·  ~3 min per slide average

OLS's other half: the outcome is now yes/no. The lesson has three parts — the model (sigmoid, log-odds, Bernoulli MLE), evaluation (confusion matrix, ROC, AUC), and the ANOVA + Tukey bridge back to group comparisons. The worked example is the same HDB above-median target as Exercise 6, so students can compare notes with their own runs: odds ratio ≈ 54 per SD of floor area, accuracy 0.81, AUC 0.92.

Slide 1~1 minTitle

Logistic Regression and Classification

Open with the bridge from 2.5: "Yesterday the outcome was a number — price. Today it's a yes/no: will this flat sell above the median? Same linear machinery inside, one new twist outside." Agenda: sigmoid, log-odds, odds ratios, then ANOVA and Tukey HSD for comparing 3+ groups.

→ "Why not just use linear regression?"
Slide 2~3 minWhy not linear

Binary outcomes — why not linear?

Walk the three bullets. One: linear predictions can exceed 1 or drop below 0 — no probability interpretation, and you can't take logs of the impossible. Two: constant variance is wrong for binary data — the variance of a Bernoulli is $p(1-p)$, which changes with $p$. Three: MLE under the Bernoulli likelihood is cleaner and more efficient.

"We need a model that bounds the output in $(0,1)$ and uses the right likelihood. Both fixes arrive together."

→ "The bounding function."
Slide 3~2 minSigmoid

The sigmoid

Walk the S-curve. As $z \to -\infty$, $\sigma(z) \to 0$; as $z \to +\infty$, $\sigma(z) \to 1$; at $z = 0$ it crosses exactly 0.5. "Any real number goes in; a probability comes out. The slope is steepest at 0.5 — most sensitive where the decision is hardest."

→ "Where does $z$ come from?"
Slide 4~3 minLog-odds link

From log-odds to sigmoid

This is the conceptual heart of the lesson. "We assume the log-odds — not the probability — is linear in the predictors." Remind them from 2.4's odds aside: odds $= p/(1-p)$. Log-odds lives on the whole real line, so a linear model fits it legally.

Then solve for $p$ step by step: exponentiate both sides, rearrange, and the sigmoid appears. "The sigmoid isn't an arbitrary choice — it's what you get when log-odds is linear. Log-odds in $(-\infty, \infty)$, sigmoid maps back to $(0,1)$."

If beginners glaze over: "Probability is a weird ruler — squashed at both ends. Log-odds is the unstretched ruler. We do the linear fit on the unstretched ruler, then re-squash to read the answer."
→ "What the fitted model looks like."
Slide 5~2 minBoundary picture

Logistic regression draws a probability boundary

Walk the figure. Two features, two classes — blue circles, rose squares. The dashed line is $p = 0.5$: the decision boundary. The shading deepens away from the line — darker means more confident. "Logistic regression is still a linear model — the boundary is a straight line. What changed is that we now read off a probability of class membership, not just a side."

→ "Fit it by MLE."
Slide 6~2 minBernoulli likelihood

Bernoulli likelihood

Walk the one-line formula: $p_i^{y_i}(1-p_i)^{1-y_i}$. "When $y_i = 1$, this is $p_i$. When $y_i = 0$, it's $1 - p_i$. One compact expression for 'the model should be confident in the right direction'." The product over all observations is the likelihood — same MLE game as 2.2.

→ "Take the log."
Slide 7~3 minCross-entropy

The log-likelihood (cross-entropy)

Sum the logs: $\ell(\beta) = \sum_i [y_i \log p_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)]$. Then the amber box: "No closed form this time — the gradient is non-linear in $\beta$. We optimise numerically: BFGS or IRLS. This is why 2.2's scipy.minimize pattern mattered."

Point at the footer: "Negate $\ell$ and you have the binary cross-entropy loss — the exact function deep learning frameworks minimise. When you meet it in M5, remember you derived it here from a Bernoulli."

→ "Reading the coefficients."
Slide 8~3 minOdds ratios

Odds ratios — the multiplicative story

Derive quickly: increasing $x_j$ by one unit multiplies the odds by $e^{\beta_j}$. Walk the table: $\beta = 0.69$ → $e^\beta \approx 2$, odds double per unit. $\beta = 0.10$ → about 10% more odds. $\beta = 0$ → exactly 1, no effect. $\beta = -0.22$ → odds drop 20% per unit.

"Coefficients add in log-odds space, which means they multiply in odds space. Report $e^\beta$, not $\beta$."

→ "And the warning that saves your credibility."
Slide 9~3 minOdds ≠ probability

Odds ≠ probability

Read the red box verbatim: "Odds ratio of 2 does NOT mean 'twice as likely'. For a baseline probability of 10%, doubling the odds gives about 18% — not 20%." Do the arithmetic on the board if needed: odds 0.1/0.9 = 0.111; doubled = 0.222; back to probability: 0.222/1.222 ≈ 0.18.

Read the footer: "Always convert back to probabilities when reporting to non-specialists. Coefficients live in log-odds space; audiences live in probability space. You are the translator."

→ "Evaluating the classifier."
Slide 10~3 minConfusion matrix

Evaluation: confusion matrix

Walk the 2×2 grid: TN, FP, FN, TP. Then the footer definitions: accuracy is everything correct over the total; precision is TP/(TP+FP) — of the ones you flagged, how many were real; recall is TP/(TP+FN) — of the real ones, how many you caught. And AUC is threshold-independent — next slide.

"Precision and recall trade off through the threshold. You can't raise both at once without a better model."

→ "Sweep every threshold at once."
Slide 11~3 minROC

ROC: threshold sweep from strict to lenient

Walk the curve. Every point is one threshold: the strict end (high threshold) sits bottom-left — few false positives, few catches; the lenient end sits top-right. The diagonal is random — AUC 0.5. The shaded area under the teal curve is the AUC; the circled top-left corner is the perfect classifier.

"Higher AUC means better class separation at all thresholds. Report AUC to compare models; then pick the operating threshold from business costs — that's 3.5's topic."

→ "Worked example — the Exercise 6 target."
Slide 12~3 minWorked setup

Worked: is this HDB resale above the median price?

Walk the code. Load the HDB parquet, keep 2020-onwards, build flat age. The target: resale price above the median — the same binary target Exercise 6 uses, so students can compare their own runs. Features: floor area and age, standardised.

"Why standardise? So each odds ratio is 'per one standard deviation' — comparable across features with different units. One SD of floor area is about 27 sqm."

→ "Fit it by hand."
Slide 13~3 minWorked MLE

Worked: MLE by hand

Walk the code. The sigmoid is written branch-wise for numerical stability — the naive form overflows for large $|z|$. The negative log-likelihood adds a tiny epsilon inside the logs so $\log 0$ never fires. BFGS from zeros.

Report the result on the slide: area OR ≈ 54 per SD (one standard deviation, ≈27 sqm, multiplies the odds of an above-median sale by about 54); age OR ≈ 1.0 — no effect once area is in the model, the same ceteris paribus story as 2.5.

→ "Score it."
Slide 14~3 minWorked evaluation

Worked: evaluate the classifier

Walk the code. Probabilities from the fitted model, threshold at 0.5 for the class predictions. Accuracy comes out at 0.81; AUC from the stateless roc_auc_score at 0.92. Then the Kailash visualisation: ModelVisualizer renders the confusion matrix and ROC curve from the polars-native arrays.

Read the footer: "Always report accuracy with the confusion matrix. 0.81 sounds modest until you see where the errors land — and on imbalanced data, accuracy alone lies outright."

→ "Bridge back to group comparisons: ANOVA."
Slide 15~3 minANOVA

ANOVA: generalising the $t$-test

"The $t$-test compares two means. What about three or more — 3-room, 4-room, 5-room, executive prices?" Decompose the variance: total sum of squares splits into between-group and within-group. The $F$-statistic is their ratio, scaled by degrees of freedom. Under the null that all group means are equal, $F \sim F_{k-1,\,n-k}$.

Nice footnote on the slide: with 2 groups, ANOVA's $F$ is exactly the square of the $t$-statistic — the same test, generalised.

→ "ANOVA says 'some differ'. Tukey says which."
Slide 16~3 minTukey HSD

Post-hoc: Tukey's HSD

Walk the formula: HSD uses $q$ from the studentized range distribution times the within-group standard error. Two means differ significantly if their gap exceeds HSD. Family-wise error control is built in.

Read the footer carefully — this is a common misconception: "Tukey's HSD is not Bonferroni-corrected pairwise $t$-tests. That's a separate, more conservative method. Tukey uses the studentized range, which accounts for the distribution of the largest gap among all pairs."

→ "Run it on flat types."
Slide 17~3 minWorked ANOVA

Worked: HDB prices across flat types

Walk the code. Pull the four flat-type groups as arrays, stats.f_oneway gives $F$ in the thousands with $p \approx 0$ — flat types absolutely differ in price. No surprise; the interesting question is which pairs differ, and stats.tukey_hsd answers with pairwise differences, p-values, and confidence intervals.

"The printout indexes groups positionally — 0 is 3 ROOM, 1 is 4 ROOM, and so on. Read the intervals: any CI that excludes zero is a significant pair, with family-wise control baked in."

→ "Three ways classification goes wrong."
Slide 18~2 minRookie mistakes

Three rookie mistakes

Walk the three red boxes. One: the blind 0.5 threshold — it's arbitrary; the right threshold comes from business costs. Two: accuracy on imbalanced data — 95% accuracy sounds great until you learn the base rate is 95%. Three: multiple pairwise $t$-tests — that's 2.3's multiple-testing trap wearing a lab coat; use ANOVA plus Tukey.

→ "Recap and bridge."
