Lesson 5.7 Transfer Learning

Training deep networks from scratch requires millions of images or billions of tokens — data and compute that most teams do not have. Transfer learning sidesteps this: take a model pre-trained on a massive general dataset, freeze the early layers that capture universal features, and fine-tune the later layers on your specific task. Today you fine-tune ResNet-18 for CIFAR‑10 image classification and BERT for news‑topic classification, add adapters that train about 1% of a frozen network, export the image model to ONNX with Kailash's OnnxBridge, and serve it with InferenceServer from the model registry — the complete pipeline from research to production.

Why This Matters

A Singapore hospital wants to classify chest X-rays (normal vs pneumonia). They have 5,000 labelled images — enough to fine-tune, not enough to train from scratch. A Singapore fintech wants to classify customer complaints by department. They have 2,000 labelled examples — enough to fine-tune BERT, not enough to train a language model. In both cases, transfer learning takes a model that learned general patterns from millions of examples and adapts it to the specific task with a fraction of the data.

This is the most practically impactful technique in modern deep learning. The vast majority of production ML systems do not train from scratch — they fine-tune pre-trained models. Understanding when, why, and how to transfer is the difference between a model that takes a week to train and one that takes an hour.

Core Concepts

Feature hierarchy in deep networks

The fundamental insight behind transfer learning: features in deep networks form a hierarchy from universal to specific. In a CNN trained on ImageNet:

  • Layer 1: Gabor filters — edges at various orientations (universal to all images)
  • Layer 2: Texture detectors — combinations of edges (universal)
  • Layer 3-4: Part detectors — shapes like wheels, eyes, petals (domain-specific)
  • Layer 5+: Object detectors — complete objects like cars, cats, flowers (task-specific)

The same hierarchy exists in language models. In BERT:

  • Layers 1-3: Syntax — part-of-speech, dependency relations (universal to all English)
  • Layers 4-8: Semantics — word meaning, coreference (partially transferable)
  • Layers 9-12: The most task‑ and objective‑specific — these change most during fine‑tuning

Three transfer learning strategies

StrategyApproachData neededWhen to use
Feature extractionFreeze entire backbone, train new head onlyHundredsVery small dataset, similar domain
Fine-tuning (top)Freeze early layers, fine-tune top layers + headThousandsModerate dataset, different domain
Full fine-tuningUnfreeze all layers, low learning rateTens of thousandsLarge dataset, very different domain
Foundations

Start with feature extraction (freeze everything, train only the new head). If accuracy is insufficient, unfreeze the top few layers and fine-tune. Only unfreeze all layers if you have enough data to prevent overfitting. This progressive unfreezing strategy is safe — you cannot make things worse by starting frozen and gradually unfreezing.

Math Foundations

Why freezing helps: the learning rate perspective

When you fine-tune, the pre-trained weights are already in a good region of the loss landscape. Large updates would push them out of this region, destroying the learned features. The solution:

$$ \theta_l^{(t+1)} = \theta_l^{(t)} - \eta_l \cdot \nabla_{\theta_l} \mathcal{L} $$
$\eta_l$ — learning rate for layer $l$ $\theta_l$ — parameters of layer $l$ $\nabla_{\theta_l} \mathcal{L}$ — gradient of loss w.r.t. layer $l$

Frozen layers have $\eta_l = 0$ (no updates). Fine-tuned layers use a small $\eta_l$ (typically $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-4}$). The new head uses a normal $\eta_l$ (typically $10^{-3}$). This discriminative learning rate strategy prevents catastrophic forgetting of pre-trained features.

LLRD — Layer-wise Learning Rate Decay

A more sophisticated approach: decay the learning rate exponentially from top layers to bottom layers:

$$ \eta_l = \eta_{\text{top}} \cdot \xi^{L - l} $$
$\eta_{\text{top}}$ — learning rate for the top layer $\xi \in (0, 1)$ — decay factor (typically 0.9) $L$ — total number of layers $l$ — current layer index

With $\xi = 0.9$ and $L = 12$ (BERT), the bottom layer's learning rate is $0.9^{12} \approx 0.28$ times the top layer's rate. This preserves the low-level features that are most universal while allowing the high-level features to adapt to the new task.

Theory

LLRD is connected to the Fisher Information Matrix from statistics. Parameters with high Fisher information (those critical to the pre-trained task) should change slowly. Empirically, lower layers have higher Fisher information for the pre-training objective, which is why LLRD applies lower learning rates to them. This connection is formalised in Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC; Kirkpatrick et al., 2017), which penalises changes to each weight in proportion to its Fisher information.

Transfer learning as regularisation

Initialising from pre-trained weights is equivalent to an implicit prior: the model starts close to a good solution and the fine-tuning objective acts as a regulariser that keeps it close. Formally:

$$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{fine-tune}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{task}} + \lambda \|\theta - \theta_{\text{pretrained}}\|^2 $$

(This explicit form is known as L2‑SP, Li et al., 2018.)

This $L_2$ penalty toward the pre-trained weights is implicit when you use a small learning rate (the model does not move far from the initialisation). Explicit regularisation toward the pre-trained weights is used in more advanced methods like EWC.

NLP Transfer Learning: BERT Fine-Tuning

import polars as pl
from datasets import Dataset
from transformers import (BertForSequenceClassification, BertTokenizer,
                          Trainer, TrainingArguments)

# AG News headlines shipped with the course (the data of Exercise 4 file 04)
train_df = pl.read_parquet("data/mlfp05/ag_news.parquet")        # 5,000 headlines, labels 0-3
eval_df = pl.read_parquet("data/mlfp05/ag_news_test.parquet")    # 1,000 headlines

tokenizer = BertTokenizer.from_pretrained("bert-base-uncased")
model = BertForSequenceClassification.from_pretrained(
    "bert-base-uncased", num_labels=4,               # World, Sports, Business, Sci/Tech
)

def tokenize(batch):
    return tokenizer(batch["text"], padding="max_length", truncation=True, max_length=64)

train_ds = Dataset.from_dict(train_df.to_dict(as_series=False)).map(tokenize, batched=True)
eval_ds = Dataset.from_dict(eval_df.to_dict(as_series=False)).map(tokenize, batched=True)

args = TrainingArguments(
    output_dir="/tmp/bert_agnews",
    num_train_epochs=2,
    per_device_train_batch_size=16,
    learning_rate=2e-5,             # 10-100x lower than training from scratch
    weight_decay=0.01,              # regularisation
    warmup_steps=100,               # gradual LR ramp-up
    eval_strategy="epoch",          # (the old name evaluation_strategy was removed)
    dataloader_pin_memory=False,    # avoids a warning on Apple MPS
    report_to="none",
)
trainer = Trainer(model=model, args=args, train_dataset=train_ds, eval_dataset=eval_ds)
trainer.train()                     # minutes on a GPU; much longer on a laptop CPU
print(trainer.evaluate())           # eval_loss on the 1,000 held-out headlines

The pre‑trained body is kept and a new, randomly initialised 4‑way head is added (transformers warns that the head is untrained — that is expected). Trainer runs the loop, evaluation and checkpointing; Exercise 4 (file 04) writes the same fine‑tuning loop by hand, with layer freezing, so you can see every step. We do not quote a "typical" accuracy here: compare your fine‑tuned BERT against the from‑scratch Transformer and LSTM of Exercise 4 on the same test headlines — that comparison is the transfer advantage.

Using a fine-tuned model: the pipeline API

For inference, Hugging Face's pipeline wraps tokenisation, the forward pass and softmax in one call, and returns readable labels if the model's config has them: 

from transformers import pipeline

model.config.id2label = {0: "World", 1: "Sports", 2: "Business", 3: "Sci/Tech"}
model.config.label2id = {v: k for k, v in model.config.id2label.items()}
clf = pipeline("text-classification", model=model, tokenizer=tokenizer, device="cpu")
print(clf(["Stocks rally as central bank holds rates", "Striker signs four-year deal"]))
# -> [{'label': ..., 'score': ...}, {'label': ..., 'score': ...}]

Before fine‑tuning, the scores are near 0.25 for every class (the head is random); after fine‑tuning they should be confident and correct on headlines like these. A topic classifier only knows its four topics: give it a customer complaint and it will still confidently pick one of World, Sports, Business or Sci/Tech — Exercise 4 file 04 ends on exactly this out‑of‑distribution trap.

Theory

BERT's pre-training on 3.3 billion words of English text gives it a deep understanding of syntax, semantics, and world knowledge. When you fine-tune on 5,000 AG News headlines, it does not need to learn "what English is" — it already knows. It only needs to learn "what makes a business headline different from a sports headline". This is why a few thousand examples are enough for fine‑tuning but not for training a language model from scratch.

The Kailash Engine: OnnxBridge and InferenceServer

Kailash Engine

OnnxBridge exports a PyTorch model to ONNX (Open Neural Network Exchange) — a portable file that runs on any ONNX Runtime (CPU, GPU, edge devices) without PyTorch. InferenceServer serves a model registered in the ModelRegistry: it is created with the coroutine InferenceServer.from_registry(name, registry=..., version=..., runtime="onnx"), loads the version's model.onnx on await server.start(), and answers await server.predict({"records": [...]}) with a plain mapping. There is no predict_batch, warm_cache or result object — one predict call takes a list of records, so batching is just sending more records. 

import asyncio
from pathlib import Path
import numpy as np
import torch
from kailash_ml import OnnxBridge, InferenceServer
from shared.mlfp05.ex_7 import (FlatImageAdapter, N_PIXELS, attach_onnx_artifact,
                                images_to_records, init_engines, register_model)

conn, tracker, exp_name, registry, _ = init_engines()       # Exercise 7's tracker + registry

# 1. Export behind a flat-row adapter: the server feeds ONE flat row per record
serving = FlatImageAdapter(model.cpu()).eval()              # model: the fine-tuned ResNet-18
path = Path("/tmp/transfer_resnet18.onnx")
res = OnnxBridge().export(serving, "torch", output_path=path,
                          sample_input=torch.randn(2, N_PIXELS))   # 2-row trace keeps batch dynamic
assert res.success, res.error_message

# 2. Register the trained model, then attach model.onnx to that version
v = register_model(registry, "cifar10_transfer", model, val_acc, final_loss)
attach_onnx_artifact("cifar10_transfer", v.version, path)

# 3. Serve from the registry (all async)
async def serve(images):
    server = await InferenceServer.from_registry(
        "cifar10_transfer", registry=registry, version=v.version, runtime="onnx")
    await server.start()
    out = await server.predict({"records": images_to_records(images)})
    await server.stop()
    return np.asarray(out["predictions"], dtype=np.float32)  # one logit row per image

logits = asyncio.run(serve(images))      # images: preprocessed (N, 3, 96, 96) tensor

We ran exactly this flow on the ImageNet ResNet‑18 with a fresh 10‑class head and four random 96×96 inputs: the served logits matched PyTorch's to within $2 \times 10^{-6}$ and gave the same classes. (The exporter may print an opset‑conversion traceback before succeeding; that is log noise — res.success is the real signal.) Exercise 7 (file 05) runs it on the trained CIFAR‑10 model and also checks parity with OnnxBridge.validate.

Adapters: Train ~1% Inside a Frozen Backbone

Freezing the backbone and training only a new head is cheap but can be too rigid; full fine‑tuning is flexible but updates (and must store) every weight per task. Adapters sit in between: small bottleneck modules inserted inside the frozen network, trained together with the head.

$$ \text{Adapter}(x) = x + W_{\text{up}}\,\mathrm{ReLU}(W_{\text{down}}\,x), \qquad W_{\text{up}} = 0 \text{ at initialisation} $$
$W_{\text{down}}$ — project $d \to r$ (bottleneck) $W_{\text{up}}$ — project $r \to d$, zero‑initialised skip connection — adapter starts as the identity 
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

class BottleneckAdapter(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim, bottleneck=64):
        super().__init__()
        self.down = nn.Linear(dim, bottleneck)
        self.up = nn.Linear(bottleneck, dim)
        nn.init.zeros_(self.up.weight)            # start as the identity
        nn.init.zeros_(self.up.bias)

    def forward(self, x):
        return x + self.up(F.relu(self.down(x)))

a = BottleneckAdapter(256)
x = torch.randn(2, 256)
print(torch.equal(a(x), x))                                   # True: no change at step 0
print(sum(p.numel() for p in a.parameters()),                 # 33088
      sum(p.numel() for p in BottleneckAdapter(512).parameters()))  # 66112

Exercise 7 (file 04) inserts one adapter after ResNet‑18's layer3 (256 channels) and one after layer4 (512 channels), applied to the channel‑pooled features. Its wrapper adds only the adapter's change (adapted − pooled) back onto the feature map, so the network computes exactly the ImageNet features at step 0. Trainable parameters: 33,088 + 66,112 = 99,200 for the adapters plus 5,130 for the 10‑class head — about 104K of roughly 11.3M, or about 1%. Because the backbone never changes, one copy of it can serve many tasks, each with its own few‑hundred‑KB adapter. This is the same idea as LoRA in Lesson 6.2: a small trainable change on top of a frozen model.

Worked Example: End-to-End Transfer and Deployment

Goal

Follow Exercise 7's path on CIFAR‑10 (60,000 small photos, 10 classes): fine‑tune an ImageNet ResNet‑18 with a frozen backbone, export it to ONNX, register it and serve it with InferenceServer. The exercise downloads CIFAR‑10 (about 170 MB) on first use and trains for several epochs, so this page shows the code and the shape of the results rather than quoting numbers — run Exercise 7 for yours.

Step 1. Data, resized for an ImageNet backbone. 
from shared.mlfp05.ex_7 import load_cifar10

train_set, val_set, train_loader, val_loader = load_cifar10()
# 50,000 train / 10,000 validation images, resized 32x32 -> 96x96, ImageNet-normalised;
# training images also get random flips and crops

Pre‑trained weights expect the statistics they were trained with: the helper normalises with the ImageNet mean and standard deviation (Mistake 5 below). Upsizing to 96×96 gives ResNet‑18's five downsampling stages a 3×3 map at the end instead of 1×1.

Step 2. Freeze the backbone, train a new head. 
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
from torchvision import models
from shared.kailash_helpers import get_device

device = get_device()
model = models.resnet18(weights=models.ResNet18_Weights.DEFAULT)
for param in model.parameters():
    param.requires_grad = False
model.fc = nn.Linear(model.fc.in_features, 10)             # 5,130 trainable parameters
model = model.to(device)
optimizer = torch.optim.Adam(model.fc.parameters(), lr=1e-3)

for epoch in range(3):                                     # Exercise 7 uses 8
    model.train()
    for images, labels in train_loader:
        images, labels = images.to(device), labels.to(device)
        optimizer.zero_grad()
        loss = nn.functional.cross_entropy(model(images), labels)
        loss.backward()
        optimizer.step()
    model.eval()
    with torch.no_grad():
        correct = sum((model(x.to(device)).argmax(1) == y.to(device)).sum().item()
                      for x, y in val_loader)
    val_acc, final_loss = correct / len(val_set), loss.item()
    print(f"epoch {epoch + 1}: val acc {val_acc:.3f}")

Only the head learns, so each epoch is mostly forward passes through the frozen backbone. Expect validation accuracy to be well above the 10% chance level after the first epoch and to rise only slowly after that — the head can only recombine features learned for ImageNet. Unfreezing layer4 with a small learning rate (the progressive‑unfreezing snippet above), or adding adapters, is the next lever.

Step 3. Export, register, serve.

Exactly the Kailash flow above: wrap with FlatImageAdapter, export with a 2‑row sample, register with register_model, attach model.onnx with attach_onnx_artifact, then InferenceServer.from_registry → start → predict. Feed it a few validation images (images, _ = next(iter(val_loader))) and check that the served classes match model(images).argmax(1).

Step 4. Compare with training from scratch. 
scratch = models.resnet18(weights=None)                    # random initialisation
scratch.fc = nn.Linear(512, 10)
n_trainable = sum(p.numel() for p in scratch.parameters() if p.requires_grad)
print(n_trainable)                                          # 11181642: every weight must be learned

Train it with the same loop (optimising all parameters) and compare validation accuracy per epoch and per labelled image. Exercise 7 file 01 trains a from‑scratch CNN baseline and file 03 repeats the transfer model on 10%, 25%, 50% and 100% of the training labels. The pattern to look for: the transfer model reaches useful accuracy with far fewer labels and epochs, and the gap is largest when labels are scarce. How large it is on your run is the number to report.

Common Mistakes

Mistake 1: Training all layers from the start

Unfreezing everything immediately with a normal learning rate destroys pre-trained features. The model "forgets" what it learned on ImageNet and overfits to your small dataset. Always start frozen, evaluate, then selectively unfreeze if needed.

Mistake 2: Too-high learning rate for fine-tuning

Pre-trained weights are in a good region of the loss landscape. A learning rate of $10^{-3}$ (normal for training from scratch) is too aggressive for fine-tuning — it pushes the weights out of the good region. Use $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-4}$ for fine-tuned layers. For the new head, $10^{-3}$ is fine because it has no pre-trained features to preserve.

Mistake 3: Skipping data augmentation

Small datasets benefit enormously from augmentation — random flips, rotations, colour jitter, cropping. Without augmentation, the model memorises the training images and generalises poorly. With augmentation on 1,000 images, the model effectively sees 10,000+ varied views.

Mistake 4: Forgetting ONNX export

A model that only runs in your Jupyter notebook is not deployed. It depends on PyTorch, your Python version, and your exact CUDA version. ONNX export produces a portable file that runs on any ONNX runtime — CPU server, GPU cluster, or edge device — without any Python dependency.

Mistake 5: Wrong normalisation

Pre-trained models expect inputs normalised with the same statistics used during pre-training. ResNet expects ImageNet normalisation: mean=[0.485, 0.456, 0.406], std=[0.229, 0.224, 0.225]. BERT expects its own tokeniser. Using wrong normalisation is a silent error — the model runs but produces degraded predictions.

Reflection

Transfer learning is the single most impactful technique in modern deep learning. Most production systems do not train from scratch — they fine-tune pre-trained models. Today you learned the why (hierarchical features), the how (freeze, fine-tune, new head), and the deployment path (OnnxBridge to InferenceServer).
