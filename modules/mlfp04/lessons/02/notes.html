Speaker Notes — Lesson 4.2 EM Algorithm and Gaussian Mixture Models

Total time: ~75 minutes  ·  19 slides  ·  ~4 min per slide average

Soft clustering done properly. The lesson delivers on a bold promise on the title slide — "implement EM in 20 lines" — so the centre of gravity is the two code slides; everything before them is motivation and derivation, everything after is validation and practice. The verification story is strong: the from-scratch EM matches sklearn's log-likelihood on the worked example. Keep the Mixture-of-Experts aside short — it's a bridge to M6, not a destination.

Slide 1~1 minTitle

EM Algorithm and Gaussian Mixture Models

Open with the bridge from 4.1: "K-means gave every customer a hard label. Today we soften it — and learn the most important iterative algorithm in statistics along the way. Outcome: implement EM in 20 lines."

→ "Why hard labels lie."
Slide 2~3 minHard labels lie

Hard labels lie

Walk the contrast. K-means says "you are cluster 2" — full stop. GMM says "you are 70% cluster 2, 25% cluster 1, 5% cluster 3." "For a point sitting between clusters, the hard answer is a confident lie. The soft answer is the honest one — and honesty is actionable: low-confidence assignments route to review, which we'll exploit at the end of the lesson."

Read the green box: "GMM = K-means with error bars."

→ "See it."
Slide 3~3 minHard vs soft picture

Hard vs soft in one picture

Walk both panels. Left — K-means: the boundary point is forced red, and the label is wrong. Right — GMM: two overlapping ellipses, and the same point reads P(red) = 0.55, P(blue) = 0.45. "The ellipses are the covariances — clusters can be stretched, rotated, overlapping. K-means' circles can't do that."

→ "The story that generates the data."
Slide 4~3 minGenerative story

The generative story

Walk the two-step story: first pick cluster $k$ with probability $\pi_k$, then draw the point from that cluster's Gaussian. The density of any point is the weighted sum over clusters.

"This is a generative model — it claims to know how the data was made. The learning problem: find $(\pi, \mu, \Sigma)$ that maximise this likelihood for the data we actually see."

→ "And why you can't just solve it."
Slide 5~3 minWhy it's hard

Why maximising the likelihood is hard

Point at the log-of-a-sum: "In 2.2 the log turned products into sums. Here the sum is inside the log — no closed form, no clean derivatives. Dead end for direct MLE."

Read the blue box: "The trick is to introduce a hidden variable $z_{nk}$ — which cluster generated point $n$ — then alternate between estimating it and updating the parameters. If we knew the $z$'s, the problem would be easy. We don't — so we guess them softly, refine, repeat."

→ "Watch the alternation converge."
Slide 6~4 minEM picture

EM: ellipses find the clusters

Walk the three panels. Init: three randomly placed, poorly fitting ellipses over three true clusters. Iteration 3: the ellipses have shifted and stretched toward the data, and the points are colour-blended — that blending is the soft assignment, the responsibility weights. Converged: tight ellipses on the three clusters, firm colours.

"Each iteration does two things: repaint the points softly (E-step), then refit the ellipses to the painted points (M-step). The next two slides are those two steps."

→ "E-step."
Slide 7~4 minE-step

E-step: responsibilities via Bayes' rule

Walk the formula: the responsibility $r_{nk}$ is prior × likelihood, normalised by the marginal. "It's Bayes' rule from 2.1, applied to cluster assignment: $P(\text{cluster } k \mid \text{point } n)$. The responsibilities sum to 1 by construction — they're a probability distribution over clusters for every point."

→ "M-step."
Slide 8~4 minM-step

M-step: weighted means

Walk the three updates. $N_k$ is the effective cluster size — the sum of responsibilities. $\pi_k$ is just $N_k / N$. The mean is a responsibility-weighted average of the points; the covariance the same weighted second moment.

Read the footer: "Each update is a weighted MLE. The weights are the responsibilities. When the weights collapse to 0/1, these are exactly K-means' updates — K-means is the hard limit of GMM."

→ "Why it can't go backwards."
Slide 9~3 minConvergence

Convergence guarantee

State the guarantee: every EM iteration satisfies $\log\mathcal{L}(\theta^{t+1}) \geq \log\mathcal{L}(\theta^{t})$, with equality only at a stationary point. "The likelihood never decreases. That's what makes EM trustworthy as an algorithm."

Read the blue box for the mathematically curious: "EM is coordinate ascent on the ELBO — the evidence lower bound; Jensen's inequality does the heavy lifting." Then the amber caveat with equal weight: "EM can get stuck in local maxima. Never-decreasing is not the same as globally best. Restart from several random inits and keep the best run."

→ "Twenty lines. Here's the first half."
Slide 10~5 minEM code 1/2

Twenty lines of EM (1/2): the two steps

Walk e_step: stack the $\pi_k \times \mathcal{N}(x|\mu_k, \Sigma_k)$ per cluster, normalise row-wise — responsibilities out, plus the log-likelihood for the convergence check. Notice scipy.stats.multivariate_normal does the density work.

Walk m_step: $N_k$ as a column-sum, the weighted mean as one matrix product, the weighted covariance per cluster. Point at reg=1e-6: "A tiny ridge on every covariance keeps them invertible — without it, a cluster that collapses onto a few nearly-collinear points blows up the next E-step."

→ "The loop."
Slide 11~4 minEM code 2/2

Twenty lines of EM (2/2): the loop

Walk em_gmm: init the means at $K$ random data points, covariances at identity, weights uniform. Then alternate E and M, stopping when the log-likelihood gain drops below tol.

"Twenty lines, and it's the real algorithm — not a toy version. The stopping rule matters: a fixed iteration count either wastes compute or stops early. Watch $\Delta\log\mathcal{L}$, not the clock."

→ "How many clusters? BIC."
Slide 12~4 minBIC

Picking $K$ with BIC

Walk the formula: $-2\log\hat{\mathcal{L}} + p\log N$ — fit versus a complexity penalty that grows with the parameter count and the log of the sample size. Lower is better; try $K = 2..10$ and take the minimum.

Walk the code: ClusteringEngine().fit(..., algorithm="gmm", covariance_type="full") reports BIC and AIC in its metrics — "the engine does the model-selection bookkeeping for you. BIC is for recovering the true model; AIC leans toward prediction."

→ "One more dial: the covariance shape."
Slide 13~4 minCovariance types

Covariance types: tied, diag, full

Walk the table. Spherical: one parameter per cluster — tiny data, round clusters. Diag: $D$ parameters — the default for high-dimensional data. Tied: a full covariance shared by all clusters. Full: $D(D+1)/2$ each — low-dimensional data with differently oriented clusters.

Read the blue box: "The types nest — spherical ⊂ diag ⊂ full, and tied ⊂ full. At the same $K$ a more flexible type can never fit worse on the training data, so comparing them by raw likelihood is rigged. Compare with BIC — that's exactly what the penalty is for."

→ "The idea's afterlife in modern LLMs."
Slide 14~3 minMixture of Experts

Mixture of Experts — where GMM meets modern LLMs

Walk the three bullets. Replace the fixed mixing weights $\pi_k$ with a gating network $g_k(x)$ — the mixture proportions now depend on the input. Each expert is a sub-network; the gate routes each token. Openly documented examples: Mixtral 8x7B — 8 experts per layer, top-2 routing, about 47B total but only ~13B active parameters — and DeepSeek-V3. GPT-4 is reported to use MoE; its architecture is unpublished, so say "reported", not "is".

Read the purple box: "Classical GMM is MoE with constant gates. We revisit this in M6 when we route between Kaizen agents."

→ "Prove the twenty lines work."
Slide 15~4 minWorked example

Worked example: 3 Gaussians in 2D

Walk the code: three true Gaussians, 200 points each, then our em_gmm from scratch — and sklearn's GaussianMixture as the referee. Read the result: both print log-likelihood ≈ −2324.39.

Read the footer honestly: "Expect close, not bit-identical, agreement in general — sklearn initialises with K-means, adds reg_covar, and stops at a looser tolerance. Matching to this precision on a fresh implementation is what 'verified' looks like."

→ "Now use the softness for something."
Slide 16~3 minUncertain points

The uncertain points are where the money is

Walk the code: select the points whose maximum responsibility is below 0.6 — the ambiguous memberships. "These are the customers worth flagging for human review in a real segmentation pipeline. K-means would have assigned them confidently to someone; hard clustering hides exactly the rows a business should look at. The soft model turns model uncertainty into a work queue."

→ "Four mistakes."
Slide 17~3 minMistakes

Four rookie mistakes

Walk the four. One: zero-initialised covariance — singular matrix on the first E-step; that's what the reg is for. Two: fixed iteration count with no $\Delta\mathcal{L}$ check — waste or premature stop. Three: comparing GMM to K-means by "accuracy vs labels" — there is no ground truth; compare on likelihood or BIC. Four: covariance_type="full" with $D > 20$ — the parameter count explodes and overfits; use diag.

→ "Where this connects."
Slide 18~3 minCross-references

Cross-references

Walk the four links. 4.1: K-means is GMM with shared $\Sigma = \sigma^2 I$ and equal weights, in the limit $\sigma^2 \to 0$ — the hard argmax. M2: the E-step is Bayes' rule. 4.7: ALS in matrix factorisation also alternates — fix one block, solve the other — the same alternating structure, but it is not EM: there is no latent distribution, so don't call it one. M6: Mixture of Experts generalises GMM to input-dependent gates.

→ "Recap and bridge."
Slide 19~2 minRecap

What you can now do

Walk the bullets: the generative story, deriving E and M steps, implementing EM in 20 lines, cross-validating against sklearn, picking $K$ with BIC, and explaining Mixture of Experts. Tease 4.3: "Stop asking 'which group' and start asking 'which axis' — dimensionality reduction."
