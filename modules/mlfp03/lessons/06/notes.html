Speaker Notes — Lesson 3.6 Interpretability and Fairness

Total time: ~55 minutes  ·  12 slides  ·  ~4.5 min per slide average

The governance lesson. The model from 3.5 is accurate and calibrated — today asks whether it should be deployed, and under what conditions. Two halves: explanation (SHAP, LIME, ALE) and fairness (disparate impact, equalised odds, the impossibility theorem). The fairness numbers to use are the measured Exercise 6 ones: race 0.94 and gender 0.96 pass the four-fifths screen; the youngest age band scores 0.02 and fails — and the correct response is escalation to a human decision, not an automatic "fix".

Slide 1~1 minTitle

Interpretability and Fairness

Open with the deployment question: "3.5 ended with a calibrated decision system. Today: explain it, audit it, and decide whether it should go out the door. Outcome: explain and audit any model."

→ "Why this isn't optional."
Slide 2~4 minWhy it matters

Why interpretability matters

Walk the three drivers. Regulatory: MAS's FEAT principles — non-binding guidance — ask firms to make AI-driven decisions such as credit transparent and fair. Debugging: a model using postcode as a proxy for race needs to be caught before deployment, not after. Trust: stakeholders will not act on a prediction they don't understand.

Read the blue box: "Interpretability is not a nice-to-have. It is a deployment prerequisite."

→ "SHAP — the gold standard."
Slide 3~4 minSHAP theory

SHAP: Shapley values for ML

Walk the formula at a high level: each feature's value is its average marginal contribution across every possible coalition of the other features. "It's the only attribution method that satisfies four fairness axioms borrowed from cooperative game theory."

Walk the axiom table — these are what make SHAP trustworthy. Efficiency: contributions sum exactly to the prediction. Symmetry: equal contributions get equal values. Dummy: a useless feature gets zero. Linearity: values add across model sums — which is why SHAP works on ensembles.

→ "See one prediction explained."
Slide 4~4 minSHAP waterfall

SHAP waterfall

Walk the waterfall for this illustrative applicant. Start at the base value — $E[f(x)] = -1.90$ log-odds. debt_to_income pushes up +0.60 (red, raises default risk), num_late_payments +0.45, income_sgd pulls down −0.35 (green). They sum exactly: $f(x) = -1.20$ log-odds, which converts to $p(\text{default}) \approx 0.23$.

Read the caption carefully — it's a technical point students trip on: "For a boosted model, TreeExplainer works in log-odds: base plus contributions equals $f(x)$ exactly — additivity holds in log-odds space, not probability space. Convert to a probability only at the very end."

→ "Run it."
Slide 5~3 minSHAP code

SHAP in code

Walk the code. TreeExplainer on the fitted model, evaluate on the test matrix — the result is an Explanation object in log-odds; attach feature names for readable plots. Note the comment: a binary random forest returns both classes, so slice [:, :, 1] for the positive class.

Two plots, two scopes: beeswarm for global importance across the whole test set, waterfall for one prediction. "Global for the model audit; local for the customer who asks 'why was I declined?'"

→ "The model-agnostic alternative."
Slide 6~3 minLIME

LIME: local explanations

Walk the recipe: perturb the input, fit a local linear model on the perturbed neighbourhood, read its coefficients. The code shows the tabular explainer — training data for the perturbation distribution, then explain_instance on one row with the model's predict_proba.

"LIME works on any model — it only needs a prediction function. The price: no axioms. Two runs can give different explanations for the same row."

→ "SHAP vs LIME vs ALE."
Slide 7~3 minMethod comparison

SHAP vs LIME vs ALE

Walk the table. SHAP: global and local, consistent by axiom, fast on trees — the default for tree models. LIME: local only, approximate, moderate speed — the fallback for any model. ALE: global, and the one that handles correlated features correctly by accumulating effects along the feature's own conditional distribution.

"Correlated features are SHAP and LIME's shared soft spot — credit data is full of them (income, debt, limits all move together). When the correlations are strong, bring ALE."

→ "From explaining to auditing: fairness."
Slide 8~5 minDisparate impact

Fairness: disparate impact

Walk the formula: each group's approval rate divided by the best group's — the four-fifths screen from US employment guidelines requires DI ≥ 0.8. Walk the code: approval is the complement of the default flag, compute per-group rates, normalise by the max, print PASS or FAIL.

Now the measured Exercise 6.5 numbers — read the footer with weight: "Race scores 0.94 and gender 0.96 — both pass. The youngest age band scores 0.02 — a catastrophic fail. And the response matters as much as the number: a gap that large is routed to a human / risk-committee decision, not auto-'fixed' by reweighing until the metric turns green. Metrics inform judgment; they don't replace it."

If students ask why not just fix the model: "A 0.02 says the model behaves very differently for young applicants. Whether that's justified — thin files, short credit histories — or discrimination is a business and legal question. The engineer's job is to surface it with evidence, not bury it."
→ "A second fairness criterion."
Slide 9~4 minEqualised odds

Fairness: equalised odds

Walk the formula: the true positive rate must be equal across groups — and the false positive rate too. "Disparate impact asks 'do groups get approved at similar rates?' Equalised odds asks the sharper question: 'is the model equally accurate for each group?' A model can pass DI while making worse mistakes on one group."

Walk the code: recall computed within each group's slice of the test set. "Same recall formula from 3.5 — evaluated per group instead of overall."

→ "And the theorem that breaks hearts."
Slide 10~4 minImpossibility theorem

The impossibility theorem

Walk the triangle: demographic parity, equalised odds, calibration. The centre text: with unequal base rates, any two already clash — except for a perfect predictor. Chouldechova 2017, Kleinberg et al. 2016.

Read the red box: "Not a limitation of your model — a mathematical fact. If two groups genuinely default at different rates, you cannot have equal approval rates, equal error rates, and equal calibration. You must choose which criterion to prioritise — and say why, in writing, in the audit report."

For experts: this is why fairness audits are governance documents, not unit tests. The choice among criteria encodes values — it belongs to the risk committee, not the training pipeline.
→ "Three mistakes."
Slide 11~3 minMistakes

Three mistakes to avoid

Walk the three red boxes. One: SHAP ≠ causation — SHAP shows what the model uses, not what causes the outcome; a causal claim needs 2.7's machinery, not an explainer. Two: removing protected features ≠ fairness — postcode and school carry the same signal as proxies; you must audit the outcome, not just the input list. Three: optimising one fairness metric blindly — the impossibility theorem guarantees fixing one can break another.

→ "Recap and bridge."
Slide 12~2 minRecap

What you can now do

Walk the bullets: compute and interpret SHAP globally and locally, apply LIME, measure disparate impact and equalised odds, explain the impossibility theorem to stakeholders, and write a fairness audit report. Tease 3.7: "You've been fitting models by hand. Next lesson: the Kailash workflow — WorkflowBuilder, ModelRegistry, and Bayesian hyperparameter search."
