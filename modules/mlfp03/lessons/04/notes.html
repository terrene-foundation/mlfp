Speaker Notes — Lesson 3.4 Gradient Boosting Deep Dive

Total time: ~75 minutes  ·  16 slides  ·  ~4.5 min per slide average

The derivation lesson. The split gain from the second-order Taylor expansion is the deepest math of M3 — budget time for it and don't rush slides 5–7. The payoff framing: by the end, students should be able to read XGBoost's objective and understand every symbol. The practical half (LightGBM speed tricks, CatBoost ordered encoding, early stopping) is what they'll actually use in the exercises on credit scoring.

Slide 1~1 minTitle

Gradient Boosting Deep Dive

Open with the contrast from 3.3: "Random forests average independent trees. Boosting does the opposite — each tree is built to correct the ensemble so far. XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost, and the second-order Taylor derivation that powers them all."

→ "Bagging vs boosting, side by side."
Slide 2~3 minBagging vs boosting

Bagging vs Boosting

Walk the two diagrams. Bagging: trees in parallel, each on a bootstrap sample, averaged — reduces variance. Boosting: trees in sequence, each one fitted against the current ensemble's errors — reduces bias.

"Same weak learners, opposite philosophy. The forest starts from overfitting and averages it away. Boosting starts from underfitting and chases the residual down. 3.2's decomposition again — variance people on the left, bias people on the right."

→ "The correction loop, drawn."
Slide 3~4 minCorrection loop

Each tree corrects the previous ensemble's mistakes

Walk the three panels. Panel 1: $F_1$ is a weak fit — nearly flat against curved data; the dashed amber segments are the residuals. Panel 2: tree 2 is fitted to those residuals — a step function hugging the gaps. Panel 3: $F_1 + F_2$ tracks the data far more closely, with the faded old $F_1$ for contrast.

Read the bottom bar: "$F_m(x) = F_{m-1}(x) + \eta \cdot f_m(x)$ — each new tree reduces the gap between prediction and truth. The ensemble is a sum of corrections."

→ "The recipe, formally."
Slide 4~4 minBoosting recipe

The boosting recipe

Walk the four bullets. $F_0$ is a constant — the mean for regression, the log-odds for classification. Each $f_m$ is fit to the residuals (gradients) of $F_{m-1}$. $\eta$ is the learning rate — the bias-variance dial of boosting.

Land the last bullet: "Small $\eta$ with many trees beats large $\eta$ with few. Shrinkage is regularisation — tiny steps force the ensemble to share credit across many trees instead of letting the first few dominate. This is why lr and n_estimators interact, and why we'll let early stopping pick the tree count."

→ "Now the derivation. XGBoost's objective."
Slide 5~5 minSecond-order objective

XGBoost — second-order objective

Walk the objective: the loss of the existing predictions plus the new tree, plus the regulariser $\Omega(f_t) = \gamma T + \frac{1}{2}\lambda\sum w_j^2$ — $\gamma$ charges per leaf, $\lambda$ shrinks leaf weights.

Then the Taylor move in the blue box: expand the loss to second order around the current prediction. "Each point contributes a gradient $g_i$ and a Hessian $h_i$. First-order methods use only $g_i$; XGBoost uses the curvature too — that's the 'second-order' in the title, and why it's loss-agnostic: any twice-differentiable loss just supplies different $g_i, h_i$."

For experts: Newton's method in function space. The ratio $-G_j/H_j$ we're about to derive is exactly a Newton step per leaf.
→ "Group by leaf and the maths collapses."
Slide 6~5 minClosed form

Group by leaf, solve in closed form

Walk the regrouping: sum the gradients and Hessians within each leaf — $G_j = \sum_{i \in I_j} g_i$, $H_j = \sum_{i \in I_j} h_i$. The objective becomes a sum of independent quadratics in the leaf weights $w_j$.

"A quadratic in one variable has a closed-form minimum: $w_j^* = -G_j / (H_j + \lambda)$. And the best achievable objective value is $-\frac{1}{2}\sum_j G_j^2/(H_j + \lambda) + \gamma T$. No gradient descent, no iteration — the optimal tree, given a structure, is arithmetic."

→ "Which structure? The split gain."
Slide 7~5 minSplit gain

The split gain — the whole point

Walk the amber box slowly — this is the destination of the derivation. The gain of splitting a node into left and right is the objective improvement: the children's scores minus the parent's, minus $\gamma$.

Walk the bullets. Evaluate the gain for every candidate split and keep the largest. $\gamma$ is the pruning cost — if the best gain is below $\gamma$, don't split. $\lambda$ shrinks leaf weights. And it's loss-agnostic: squared loss, logistic loss, Poisson — they all plug in through $g_i, h_i$.

"Every XGBoost fit you've ever run was this formula, evaluated billions of times. You now know what the library is doing."

→ "LightGBM: same maths, faster."
Slide 8~4 minLightGBM tricks

LightGBM — speed tricks

Walk the three tricks. Histogram splits: bin features into ~256 buckets and evaluate splits at bucket boundaries — $O(n + 256)$ instead of $O(n \log n)$ per feature. GOSS: gradient-based subsampling, next slide. Leaf-wise growth: always split the leaf with the largest gain regardless of depth — unbalanced trees, faster convergence, and num_leaves becomes the main complexity dial.

→ "GOSS deserves its own slide."
Slide 9~4 minGOSS

GOSS intuition

Read the opening: "Small-gradient rows are already well-predicted — they carry little signal. Large-gradient rows are where the model is wrong." Walk the five steps: sort by $|g_i|$, keep the top $a \cdot n$ (default 0.2), randomly sample $b \cdot n$ of the rest (default 0.1 of the full data), train on the ≈30% combined, and up-weight the sampled low-gradient rows by $(1-a)/b$ so the gradient sums stay unbiased.

Read the footer: "~3× speedup with minimal accuracy loss. The up-weighting is what makes it honest — without it you'd be training on a skewed sample."

→ "Leaf-wise vs depth-wise, drawn."
Slide 10~4 minLeaf-wise picture

LightGBM grows the most promising leaf first

Walk the two panels. Left — depth-wise (XGBoost default): every node at level $d$ is expanded before level $d+1$ begins; balanced trees; max_depth is the main dial. Right — leaf-wise (LightGBM): only the highest-gain leaf splits next; the tree grows unbalanced, deep where the signal is and shallow elsewhere; num_leaves is the main dial.

Read the bottom bar: "Depth-wise: tune max_depth (3–10). Leaf-wise: tune num_leaves (31–256). Both use early stopping. And the warning in the right panel: leaf-wise converges fastest but can overfit on small data."

→ "CatBoost and the leakage problem you didn't know you had."
Slide 11~4 minCatBoost

CatBoost — ordered boosting

State the problem first: "Mean-target encoding leaks the target into the features — each row's encoding includes its own label." Then the solution: impose an artificial row ordering and encode row $i$ using only rows $j < i$, with a prior-mean smoothing term $a\bar{y}$. Reshuffle the ordering every boosting iteration.

"It's M2's point-in-time discipline applied to encodings: no row may see its own target. No leakage, no hand-engineering — and high-cardinality categoricals become usable directly."

→ "All three, one dataset."
Slide 12~4 minThree boosters

Train all three on credit scoring

Walk the code, starting with the comment: the shared prepare_credit_split() drops customer_id and the planted leak future_default_indicator — 33 features remain. "That leak column correlates with default at about 0.96; Exercise 4 teaches students to catch it with a leak screen. Every credit example in M3 drops it first."

Walk the three configs: XGBoost (400 trees, lr 0.05, depth 6, reg_lambda, gamma, aucpr metric), LightGBM (num_leaves 31), CatBoost (depth 6, l2_leaf_reg 3). All scored with AUC-PR — average precision — because credit default is imbalanced; the traps slide returns to why.

→ "The free tuning knob."
Slide 13~4 minEarly stopping

Early stopping — free tuning

Walk the code. Request 1,000 trees but set early_stopping_rounds=25: halt when the validation metric hasn't improved for 25 rounds and keep the best iteration. "n_estimators stops being a hyperparameter — the validation curve picks it."

Emphasise the split discipline in the comment: "The validation split is carved from train — stratified — and the test set stays untouched. Stopping on the test set would tune on it, and its score would no longer be honest. 3.2's nested-CV lesson, applied to a single fitting run."

→ "Which one do you reach for?"
Slide 14~3 minWhich booster

Which booster?

Walk the table. XGBoost: clean numeric data, small-to-medium size, when reproducibility matters — tune lr, max_depth, reg_lambda first. LightGBM: millions of rows, many features, speed matters — tune num_leaves, lr, min_data_in_leaf. CatBoost: many high-cardinality categoricals — tune iterations, depth, l2_leaf_reg.

"On tabular data, one of these three is usually the right answer. Deep learning earns its keep on text, images, and sequences — Modules 5 and 6. On tables, boost."

→ "Three traps."
Slide 15~3 minTraps

Three traps

Walk the three red boxes. One: ROC-AUC on imbalanced data — the false-positive axis hides the minority class; use AUC-PR (average precision) instead, exactly as the training code did. More in 3.5. Two: no early stopping — you'll under- or overfit; always pass an eval_set carved from train, never the test set. Three: tuning lr and n_estimators independently — they interact through shrinkage; fix a small lr and let early stopping pick the tree count.

→ "Recap and bridge."
Slide 16~2 minRecap

What you can now do

Walk the bullets: derive the split gain from the second-order expansion, explain GOSS and leaf-wise growth, explain CatBoost's ordered encoding, train all three boosters on imbalanced credit data, and use early stopping correctly. Tease 3.5: "AUC-PR was just the beginning. Next lesson: the full metrics story — SMOTE, focal loss, calibration, and cost-based thresholds."
