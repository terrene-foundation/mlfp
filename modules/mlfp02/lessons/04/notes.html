Speaker Notes — Lesson 2.4 A/B Testing and Experiment Design

Total time: ~45 minutes  ·  14 slides  ·  ~3 min per slide average

This is the payoff lesson of M2's first half: everything from 2.2 and 2.3 lands in one end-to-end experiment. The two teaching priorities are discipline (pre-register, check SRM before outcomes) and the correct sample-size number — 11,554 per arm, not the halved value students expect. The worked example runs on the course experiment data with its designed 40/35/15/10 split.

Slide 1~1 minTitle

A/B Testing and Experiment Design

Open: "2.3 gave you the statistics — p-values, power, corrections. Today we wrap them in the process that keeps you honest: randomisation, pre-registration, power analysis, and the SRM check. By the end you'll run the full loop on real experiment data."

→ "Why does randomisation even work?"
Slide 2~3 minRandomisation

Why randomisation works

Walk the figure. The population box is full of confounders — young and old, low- and high-income, mixed together. Random assignment splits them into control and treatment so that every confounder — measured and, crucially, unmeasured — ends up roughly equally in both arms.

Land it: "You don't have to know what the confounders are. Randomisation balances the ones you never thought to measure. That's the entire causal argument of the A/B test — any systematic difference in outcomes must come from the treatment, because nothing else differs between the groups."

→ "Before you touch data, write everything down."
Slide 3~4 minPre-registration

Pre-register before you touch the data

Walk the five items. Unit of randomisation — user, session, or device; mixing units breaks independence. Metrics: exactly one primary, up to three secondary, plus guardrails that must not degrade. Direction of the alternative. Decision rule — $\alpha$, power target, MDE. Stopping criterion.

Then the red box. "Peeking inflates the false-positive rate — in our simulation, checking the p-value daily and stopping at the first $p < 0.05$ turns a 5% false-positive rate into roughly 25%. Decide everything upfront: commit it in writing, or log it as run parameters in ExperimentTracker before you see any outcome."

If beginners ask why one primary metric: "Every extra metric is another lottery ticket. With one primary metric the decision is unambiguous: did the number move, yes or no. Secondary metrics are for learning, not for the ship call."
→ "How many users do you need?"
Slide 4~4 minPower sizing

Power analysis — sizing

Show both forms: the general $z$-test version and the proportion version with baseline $p_0$ and lift $\Delta$. Then run the numbers on the slide: detect a 1-percentage-point lift from an 8% baseline at 80% power, $\alpha = 0.05$ → $n \approx 11{,}600$ per arm, about 23,100 in total.

Emphasise the warning at the bottom of the slide: "The formula already gives per arm. Do not halve it. The most common sizing mistake in industry is quoting 5,800 here — that under-powers the experiment and wastes the whole run."

→ "What does the power curve look like?"
Slide 5~2 minPower curves

Power curves

Walk the two curves. The red curve is the small effect ($\delta = 1\%$) — power crawls toward 0.8 slowly, needing huge $n$. The teal curve is the big effect ($\delta = 5\%$) — power shoots up fast. The dashed line at 0.8 is the conventional target.

Read the footer: "Required $n$ scales with $1/\delta^2$ — halve the effect, quadruple the sample size. This is why the MDE conversation with the business is the most important slide in the deck: 'what is the smallest lift worth shipping?'"

→ "The experiment is live. First check — before any outcome."
Slide 6~4 minSRM

SRM — the first check you run

"Before you look at a single outcome metric, check the allocation." Walk the chi-squared formula and the code: intended 50/50, observed 5,212 vs 4,788 → $\chi^2 = 17.98$, $p = 0.00002$. Sample ratio mismatch.

Read the amber box carefully — three teaching points. "Test against the designed split, not a default 50/50. Use a strict threshold — this course flags SRM at $p < 0.01$, because SRM is a data-quality alarm, not a hypothesis. And if it fires: do not ship. Something in the randomisation or logging pipeline is broken, and no analysis can rescue the data."

For experts: common SRM causes — bot traffic, logging losses concentrated in one arm, hash-collision in the bucketing function, re-randomisation on user properties. The chi-squared test detects the symptom, not the cause.
→ "Zooming out — where does experiment data come from?"
Slide 7~3 minCollection framework

Data Collection Framework

Walk the five rows briskly. Why: hypothesis, business value, the decision it drives — no experiment without a decision attached. What: primary, secondary, guardrail metrics plus budget. Where: internal sources (CRM, POS, app events) versus external (data.gov.sg, APIs). How: at-start activities (review, discover, validate) versus continuous collection. Frequency: collection vs analysis cadence — and duration set by the power calculation, not by the calendar.

→ "And the ways it goes wrong."
Slide 8~2 minData pitfalls

Common data pitfalls

Walk the five quickly, one sentence each. Silos: metrics spread across systems that never get joined. Red tape: three sign-offs before you can touch data — plan for it. Aggregated only: daily totals with no user-level rows mean no proportion tests. Selection bias: only completers get logged, so your sample is the survivors. Temporal leakage: the experiment window overlaps a marketing push — the lift belongs to the campaign, not your variant.

→ "Now the worked example — sizing first."
Slide 9~3 minWorked sizing

Worked: BOGO sample size

Walk the code. Baseline conversion $p_0 = 0.08$, minimum lift worth detecting $\Delta = 0.01$, $\alpha = 0.05$, power 0.80. Pull the two $z$ values from stats.norm.ppf, plug into the proportion formula, ceil up.

The answer: 11,554 per arm — 23,108 total. Write it on the board. "Remember this number. Everyone's gut says a few thousand. A 1pp lift at an 8% baseline is a small signal in a lot of noise — that's what it costs."

→ "Data collected. First check."
Slide 10~4 minWorked SRM

Worked: load and SRM check

Walk the code. Load experiment_data.parquet. The experiment was designed 40/35/15/10 across control, treatment_a, treatment_b, variant_c — so the expected counts come from those weights, never a flat 50/50. The all-arms chi-squared gives $p \approx 0$: SRM — variant_c is over-allocated relative to design.

Then the recovery: "The four-arm design is compromised, but the control vs treatment_a pair passes its own SRM check at the designed 40:35 ratio — $p \approx 0.62$. We analyse that pair and flag the rest. Note the assertion: below 0.01 we stop — do not analyse a mismatched pair."

If beginners ask why 40:35 and not 50:50: "Testing the pair against 50/50 would flag a perfectly healthy split as SRM, because the design intended unequal arms. Always test against what was designed."
→ "Now the outcome."
Slide 11~4 minWorked primary test

Worked: the primary test

Walk the code. First the metric definition — conversion is a qualifying order, metric_value of at least 50 dollars. "The metric definition is a business decision made at pre-registration, not a line of code you improvise."

Compute the two proportions: control ≈ 19.1%, treatment_a ≈ 24.8%. Then the two-proportion $z$-test with the pooled standard error under the null, the two-sided p-value, and the unpooled 95% CI for the difference with 1.96. "Three numbers to report: the lift, its interval, and the p-value. Point estimate alone is not an answer."

→ "Say it to the business."
Slide 12~3 minReporting

Reporting to the business

Read the green box as a template — these are illustrative numbers for the BOGO case. "Ship. Conversion increased 1.3 percentage points, 95% CI 0.4 to 2.2, p = 0.003, exceeding the pre-registered MDE of 1pp. Roughly 240K dollars additional revenue per quarter. No SRM. No guardrail degradation."

Unpack the craft: "Plain language. Uncertainty quoted, not hidden. Connected to money. And never say 'we prove' — you accumulate evidence, you never prove. The whole paragraph is four sentences; if your report needs more, put it in an appendix."

→ "Three ways to blow it all up."
Slide 13~2 minRookie mistakes

Three rookie mistakes

Walk the three red boxes. One: peeking — monitoring p-values and stopping when significant; the simulation from slide 3 says this roughly quintuples your false-positive rate. Two: no power analysis — underpowered experiments either waste the run or ship noise. Three: skipping SRM — "if allocation is broken, nothing else matters. SRM is the cheapest check you'll ever run and the most expensive one to skip."

→ "Recap and bridge."
Slide 14~2 minRecap

What you can now do

Walk the bullets: pre-register with MDE, $\alpha$, power; compute sample size; detect SRM; apply the Why/What/Where/How/Frequency framework; run a proportion $z$-test with CI; report in business language. Tease 2.5: "A/B testing compares two groups. But prices don't come in two groups — they vary continuously. Next lesson: linear regression from first principles."
