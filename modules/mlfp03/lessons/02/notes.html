Speaker Notes — Lesson 3.2 Bias-Variance, Regularisation, and Cross-Validation

Total time: ~75 minutes  ·  15 slides  ·  ~5 min per slide average

The theoretical core of M3 — "the most important equation in applied ML" per the slide subtitle. Two derivations matter: the bias-variance decomposition, and the geometric story of why L1 produces zeros. The worked example is a noisy sine wave, not a business dataset — the point is to watch the U-shape appear live with a known noise floor.

Slide 1~1 minTitle

Bias-Variance, Regularisation, and Cross-Validation

Open with the promise: "3.1 asked which features. Today asks how hard the model should fit them. The answer is one equation, one geometry, and one honest evaluation protocol." Outcome: derive the decomposition and tune a regularised model.

→ "The picture every ML engineer carries in their head."
Slide 2~4 minCanonical curve

The canonical curve

Walk the axes first: model complexity on the x-axis, error on the y-axis. The blue training curve falls forever — a complex enough model memorises anything. The red test curve is U-shaped: it falls while the model learns real structure, then climbs as it starts fitting noise. The dashed grey line is $\sigma^2$ — the irreducible noise floor no model can beat. The green dashed vertical is the sweet spot.

Read the caption and make it the lesson's mantra: "Training error falls forever; test error is U-shaped. The gap between them is variance. A large train/test gap means high variance — the model fits the training set too specifically. That's how you read every learning curve from now on."

→ "Let's prove where the U comes from."
Slide 3~3 minSetup

The setup

State the model of the world: $y = f(x) + \varepsilon$ with mean-zero noise of variance $\sigma^2$. $f$ is the unknown true function; $\hat{f}$ is our fit on a random training sample. The quantity we care about: expected squared error at a test point, averaged over training sets and noise.

"Emphasise the averaging: we're not asking about one unlucky fit. We're asking what error looks like in expectation over the data we could have drawn."

→ "The decomposition."
Slide 4~5 minDecomposition

The decomposition

Walk the three terms of the boxed equation. Bias²: how wrong the average prediction is — the model is too simple to represent $f$. Variance: how much the prediction wobbles across training sets — the model is too flexible and chases each sample's noise. $\sigma^2$: the part of $y$ that $x$ cannot explain — a hard floor.

"Regularisation is the dial between the first two. Stronger penalty: simpler model — bias up, variance down. Weaker penalty: bias down, variance up. You can't minimise both; you can only trade. The sweet spot on the curve is where the sum is smallest."

For experts: the derivation is one add-and-subtract of $\mathbb{E}\hat{f}$ inside the square, plus the vanishing of the cross-term because $\varepsilon$ is independent mean-zero noise. Worth showing on a whiteboard if the room wants it.
→ "The picture that makes it stick."
Slide 5~3 minDart board

The dart-board picture

Walk the four boards. Low bias, low variance: darts clustered on the bullseye — the goal. High bias, low variance: a tight cluster off-centre — consistently wrong, the too-simple model. Low bias, high variance: darts sprayed around the bullseye — right on average, never reliable — the overfit model. High bias, high variance: a scattered mess off-centre.

"Each dart is a model fitted on a different training sample. The centre of the cluster is bias; the spread is variance. When students diagnose a model later, ask them: which board are you looking at?"

→ "The dial: regularisation."
Slide 6~3 minRegularisation

Regularisation — trade variance for bias

Walk the two penalties. Ridge adds $\lambda\sum\beta_j^2$; Lasso adds $\lambda\sum|\beta_j|$. Both shrink coefficients toward zero — deliberately biasing the fit to reduce its variance.

Then the question that drives the next slide: "Both shrink. Only L1 drives coefficients to exactly zero — automatic feature selection. Why? Geometry."

→ "Circle vs diamond."
Slide 7~5 minL1 vs L2 geometry

L2 (circle) vs L1 (diamond)

Walk the two panels. The grey ellipses are contours of the loss — the unconstrained optimum sits at their centre. The coloured shape is the penalty budget: a circle for L2, a diamond for L1. The regularised solution is where the smallest ellipse first touches the shape.

Left panel: the circle is round — the ellipse touches it at a generic point, both coefficients nonzero. Right panel: the diamond has corners on the axes — the ellipses preferentially hit those corners, and a corner means one coefficient is exactly zero. "Sparsity is not magic. It's corners."

If the geometry is hard: "Roll a ball bearing (the loss) against a round bowl versus a square box. Against the bowl it stops anywhere. Against the box it usually ends up wedged in a corner."
→ "The Bayesian reading — connecting back to 2.2."
Slide 8~4 minBayesian reading

Bayesian reading

Walk the two equivalences: Ridge is the MAP estimate under a Gaussian prior on the coefficients; Lasso is MAP under a Laplace prior. Walk the log-posterior: the data term is the squared loss, the prior term is $\sum\beta_j^2 / (2\tau^2)$ — ridge with $\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2$.

"A tighter prior — smaller $\tau^2$, stronger belief that coefficients are near zero — means a bigger penalty. And the Laplace prior has a sharp spike at zero: the Bayesian image of the diamond's corner. This is MAP from 2.2 wearing an ML hat."

→ "The best of both."
Slide 9~3 minElastic Net

Elastic Net

Walk the mixed penalty: $\lambda(\rho\|\beta\|_1 + (1-\rho)\|\beta\|_2^2)$ — a mixed Laplace–Gaussian prior. Flag the sklearn naming trap on the slide: alpha is the overall strength $\lambda$, l1_ratio is the mix $\rho$.

When to use it — walk the three bullets: Lasso oversimplifies correlated features (it picks one of a correlated group arbitrarily); Ridge refuses to zero anything you know is irrelevant; or you simply want a little of both.

→ "How do we measure any of this honestly? Cross-validation."
Slide 10~4 minCV strategies

Cross-validation — five strategies

Walk the table by "what goes wrong without it". KFold for i.i.d. data — protects against simple holdout variance. StratifiedKFold for class imbalance — protects against folds with zero minority class. GroupKFold for repeated subjects — the same patient must never appear in train and test; that's hidden group leakage. TimeSeriesSplit for temporal data — never let the future predict the past. Nested CV for tuning plus evaluation — protects against optimistic best-score bias.

"The strategy is chosen by the data's structure, not by convenience. Ask: what makes two rows of this data related?"

→ "The rotation, drawn."
Slide 11~3 min5-fold CV

5-fold CV

Walk the five rows. Each fold takes a turn as the red validation block while the other four train. "Five fits, five scores. The CV score is their average — a far more stable estimate of test error than any single split. Every row gets validated exactly once, and no row is ever in its own training set."

→ "But if you tuned on those scores, they're no longer honest."
Slide 12~5 minNested CV

Nested CV — honest model selection

Walk the code. The outer loop splits off a test fold. Inside, a GridSearchCV with its own inner CV picks $\alpha$ on the outer-training data only. The chosen model is scored on the outer test fold — data the search never saw. Collect the outer scores; their mean is the honest MSE.

Read the footer: "Inner loop chooses $\alpha$. Outer loop evaluates. Data used for tuning is never used for scoring. This is why best_score_ from a flat grid search is optimistically biased — the score was part of the selection."

→ "Watch the U-shape live."
Slide 13~4 minWorked sine

Worked example — overfitting a noisy sine

Walk the setup: $y = \sin(2\pi x) + \varepsilon$ with $\sigma = 0.2$ — so the noise floor is $\sigma^2 = 0.04$. Forty training points, a thousand test points, polynomial models of degree 1 to 20 via the ex_2 helpers.

Narrate the printed table against the canonical curve: degrees 1–2 underfit (test MSE ≈ 0.25 — high bias). Degrees 4–9 sit just above the noise floor — the best is degree 6 at 0.044, essentially $\sigma^2$. By degree 20 the test error climbs back to 0.060 while training error keeps falling. "The U-shape, live, with a known floor. Degree 20's widening train/test gap is variance in the flesh."

→ "Three ways CV goes wrong."
Slide 14~3 minMistakes

Three expensive mistakes

Walk the three red boxes. One: reporting GridSearchCV.best_score_ as the test score — optimistically biased; use nested CV. Two: plain k-fold on grouped data — the same patient in train and test; use GroupKFold. Three: shuffling a time-series — the model sees the future; use TimeSeriesSplit.

"All three are the same sin as 3.1: evaluation that saw data it shouldn't have. Leakage isn't just a feature problem — it's an evaluation problem."

→ "Recap and bridge."
Slide 15~2 minRecap

What you can now do

Walk the bullets: derive the decomposition, explain L1 sparsity geometrically and Bayesianly, pick the right CV strategy, build a nested CV loop, and spot the three classic mistakes. Tease 3.3: "You now know how hard to fit. Next lesson: what to fit — the complete supervised model zoo: SVM, KNN, Naive Bayes, trees, and forests."
