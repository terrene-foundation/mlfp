Lesson 6.1 LLM Fundamentals, Prompting, and Structured Output

You finished Module 5 with a working transformer. This lesson picks up exactly where that left off: a transformer, scaled up a thousand times, trained on a trillion tokens, and reached through an API. In the next 60 minutes you will learn how large language models are pre-trained, why prompting works at all, how to squeeze quality out of a model without touching its weights, and how to force its output into typed Python objects you can put into a data pipeline.

Why This Matters

Every deployed AI product you will build from this point forward will contain at least one call to a large language model. Not because LLMs are fashionable, but because they are the only general-purpose tool in the ML toolbox: one API, one prompt, and you have a classifier, an extractor, a summariser, a translator, an agent. The cost of trying an idea has dropped to pennies. The skill that separates an engineer who ships from one who burns budget is no longer "can you fine-tune a model" — it is "can you write a prompt that works the first time".

This lesson is the foundation for every other lesson in Module 6. You cannot build a RAG system (6.4) without prompting skill. You cannot align a model with DPO (6.3) without understanding how the base model was pre-trained. You cannot build an agent (6.5) without a Delegate that reliably emits a typed object. So we start here.

From Transformer to Large Language Model

In Lesson 5.4 you built a small transformer block: multi-head attention, a feed-forward layer, residual connections and layer normalisation. A large language model is that same block, repeated 32–96 times, trained on a trillion tokens of text for weeks on thousands of GPUs.

Pre-training: next-token prediction at scale

GPT-style models are trained on exactly one objective: given the previous $t$ tokens, predict token $t+1$. Formally, given a sequence $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_T$ the model learns parameters $\theta$ that minimise the negative log-likelihood of the next token:

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) \;=\; -\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p_\theta\!\left(x_t \,\mid\, x_1, \ldots, x_{t-1}\right)$$
$p_\theta$ — model distribution $T$ — sequence length $x_t$ — token at position $t$

That is it. No labels, no human annotation — just text that already exists on the internet. The model reads "The Singapore monsoon arrived in" and is rewarded for placing high probability on "December". Repeat this across a trillion tokens of Wikipedia, GitHub, books, forums, news and you eventually get something that has absorbed a staggering fraction of human written knowledge as a side-effect of predicting one token at a time.

🔵 Theory — why next-token prediction yields intelligence

Predicting the next token under wide distribution coverage forces the model to implicitly learn grammar, world knowledge, arithmetic, programming, translation, style, and reasoning — because every one of those skills is useful for lowering the cross-entropy loss on some subset of the training data. The task is simple; the data is everything ever written. Scaling laws (Kaplan et al. 2020; Hoffmann et al. 2022) show loss falls as a power law in parameters and data. The parametric form fitted by Hoffmann et al. (the "Chinchilla" paper) is $$\mathcal{L}(N, D) \approx E + \frac{A}{N^\alpha} + \frac{B}{D^\beta}$$ where $N$ is parameters, $D$ is training tokens and $E$ is the irreducible loss — the entropy of text itself, which no model size removes. More parameters and more data shrink the two power-law terms, with diminishing returns; the loss never reaches zero.

Tokenisation

Before text reaches the transformer it is chopped into tokens. Modern LLMs use byte-pair encoding (BPE) or similar subword schemes: common words are a single token ("Singapore" → 1 token), rare words are split ("Jurong" → "Jur" + "ong"; "HDB" → "H" + "DB"), and every character is representable as a fallback. A rule of thumb: 1 English token $\approx$ 0.75 words $\approx$ 4 characters.

This matters for two practical reasons. First, you pay per token, not per word. Second, context windows are measured in tokens. Context windows of 128k tokens are now common on hosted models; a 20-page report is roughly 10–15k tokens (20 pages × ~500 words ÷ 0.75 words per token). Small local models have smaller windows — check yours before stuffing a prompt.

Inference: the KV-cache and why generation is fast

When you ask an LLM to produce 500 tokens, it does not run the full stack 500 times from scratch. At step $t$ it already computed the Key and Value projections for tokens $1, \ldots, t-1$ — and attention at those positions does not change. The KV-cache stores them and reuses them. Generation becomes "compute Q for the new token, attend against the cached K/V, sample, append". This is why the first token takes longer (prompt prefill) than subsequent tokens (decode).

🟣 Advanced — inference optimisations

Production LLM serving stacks like vLLM and TensorRT-LLM add more: continuous batching packs requests of different lengths into one forward pass; speculative decoding uses a small draft model to propose tokens that the big model verifies in parallel; flash attention rewrites softmax to avoid materialising the full attention matrix. You do not need to implement these — but you should know they exist, because they determine your cost-per-token.

Why Prompting Works

Here is the paradox: a model trained to predict the next token on random internet text is somehow able to classify movie reviews, translate legal documents, and write code. How?

A prompt is not a command — it is a context. When you type "Classify this review: <text>. Sentiment:" you are asking the model "what tokens are most likely to follow this exact string?". And because the training corpus contained billions of classification-shaped examples (stack overflow Q&A, form labels, tutorials), the most likely continuation is a label. You are not teaching the model anything — you are priming it with a context in which the desired output is the natural continuation.

🟢 Foundations — the prompting mindset

A prompt is a way of selecting a neighbourhood of the training distribution. "Answer like a senior compliance officer" places the model in the neighbourhood of compliance documents. "Let's think step by step" places it in the neighbourhood of math tutorials where authors actually showed their work. You are not changing the model's knowledge — you are moving a spotlight across it.

Four Prompting Techniques You Must Know

1. Zero-shot

No examples. Just describe the task and ask. Works whenever the task is well-represented in pre-training — classification, simple extraction, summarisation in English. Cheapest, fastest, most brittle.

2. Few-shot (in-context learning)

Include $k$ labelled examples in the prompt. The model pattern-matches: "I have seen four input → output pairs of this shape; the fifth input is probably resolved the same way". Typical $k$ is 3–8. Diminishing returns beyond 10. Use diverse, representative examples — the model learns the pattern, not the specific content.

3. Chain-of-thought

Ask the model to write out its intermediate work before the final answer. There are two flavours. Few-shot CoT (Wei et al. 2022) puts worked reasoning inside the examples. Zero-shot CoT (Kojima et al. 2022) simply appends the five words "Let's think step by step" — with the text-davinci-002 model that lifted MultiArith accuracy from 17.7% to 78.7% and GSM8K from 10.4% to 40.7%. CoT helps most on arithmetic, logic and multi-step decisions; on an easy task like binary sentiment the gain can be small or zero. The cost is more output tokens.

🔵 Theory — why chain-of-thought works

An LLM emits tokens autoregressively. Each next token is conditioned on everything generated so far. When you force the model to emit reasoning first, those reasoning tokens become part of the context for the final answer — effectively giving the model scratchpad space to break a hard problem into easy sub-problems. It also commits the model to a path; without CoT, the model has to "decide" the answer in a single forward pass through its layers, which is harder.

4. Self-consistency

Sample the same CoT prompt $N$ times at high temperature ($T \approx 0.7$). You get $N$ different reasoning chains that may reach different conclusions. Take the majority vote. Wang et al. 2022 showed this beats plain CoT across many benchmarks — it trades compute for accuracy in a principled way. Cost scales linearly with $N$.

The Kailash Engine: Delegate and Signature

⚙ Kailash — Kaizen Delegate

Delegate is Kailash's LLM call primitive. It puts hosted and local model providers behind one streaming interface: every call emits a stream of events — text chunks, tool-call start, tool-call result, turn complete (with token usage). In this course every call runs on a local model served by Ollama, and you never build a Delegate by hand: the course helper make_delegate() wires the Ollama adapter and reads the model name from the OLLAMA_CHAT_MODEL environment variable (default llama3.2:3b). Do not pass model= — an explicit name overrides your environment. 

from shared.mlfp06._ollama_bootstrap import make_delegate

delegate = make_delegate(temperature=0.0)   # model from OLLAMA_CHAT_MODEL

prompt = "Classify the sentiment of this review as positive or negative: ..."
response, usage = "", {}
async for event in delegate.run(prompt):
    kind = getattr(event, "event_type", None)
    if kind == "text_delta":
        response += event.text          # streamed chunk
    elif kind == "turn_complete":
        usage = event.usage or {}       # prompt / completion token counts

print(response, usage)

The async iterator is important. LLM responses come in chunks; Delegate streams each chunk as soon as it arrives, which lets you display partial text in a UI and abort early if the response is going somewhere bad. The course wraps this loop as run_delegate_text(delegate, prompt), which returns (text, usage, seconds).

What about spend? On a priced hosted provider, Delegate accepts a budget_usd= hard cap. A local Ollama model costs nothing per token, so make_delegate() deliberately sets budget_usd=None — a dollar cap against a free model would be meaningless (and mis-priced). The honest load signal locally is the token count in usage, which Exercise 1 records for every technique.

Structured output with Signature

Free-form text is fragile. If you write response.strip().split(":")[-1] to extract a category, one stray colon in the LLM's prose and your pipeline breaks. The production answer is to force the LLM to return a typed object, not prose. In Kaizen, a Signature is a Python class that declares typed input and output fields. You hand it to an agent; the agent renders the schema into the prompt, asks the model for JSON, and unpacks the reply into a dict keyed by your output-field names — no string parsing on your side. 

from kaizen import Signature, InputField, OutputField
from kaizen.core.base_agent import BaseAgent
from shared.mlfp06._ollama_bootstrap import DEFAULT_CHAT_MODEL, OLLAMA_BASE_URL


class ReviewExtraction(Signature):
    """Extract structured information from a movie review snippet."""

    review_text: str = InputField(description="Movie review snippet to analyse")

    sentiment: str = OutputField(description="Exactly one of: positive, negative")
    confidence: float = OutputField(description="Classification confidence 0.0 to 1.0")
    key_phrases: list[str] = OutputField(description="Up to 5 phrases that signal sentiment")
    targets: list[str] = OutputField(description="Aspects evaluated (acting, plot, ...)")
    tone: str = OutputField(description="enthusiastic, measured, disappointed, ...")


agent = BaseAgent(
    config={
        "llm_provider": "ollama",
        "model": DEFAULT_CHAT_MODEL,           # resolved from OLLAMA_CHAT_MODEL
        "base_url": OLLAMA_BASE_URL,
        "use_async_llm": True,                 # required for run_async
        "response_format": {"type": "json_object"},
        "structured_output_mode": "explicit",
    },
    signature=ReviewExtraction(),
)

review = "a sharp, funny script let down by a limp, predictable ending."
result = await agent.run_async(review_text=review)
print(result["sentiment"], result["confidence"], result["key_phrases"][:3])

Notice what this buys you. result["key_phrases"] arrives as a list you can index, iterate and store in a DataFrame column, and result["confidence"] is a number, so if float(result["confidence"]) < 0.6: escalate_to_human(result) is a one-liner. A small local model does not always honour the schema, so check the keys: Exercise 1.6 raises if any output field is missing instead of filling in a placeholder value.

Worked Example: Classifying Movie-Review Sentiment

📝 Goal

Classify short movie-review snippets from the SST-2 dataset (Stanford Sentiment Treebank, public) as positive or negative, comparing zero-shot, few-shot and chain-of-thought prompting on the same inputs. Then upgrade to a Signature for structured output. This is the same task and data as Exercise 1 (ex_1/01–06).

Step 1.

Set up the environment and load the dataset. load_sst2() downloads 200 shuffled SST-2 training rows once and caches them; each row has text, label (positive/negative) and label_id.

import polars as pl
from dotenv import load_dotenv

from shared.mlfp06._ollama_bootstrap import make_delegate, run_delegate_text
from shared.mlfp06.ex_1 import CATEGORIES, get_eval_docs, normalise_label

load_dotenv()

docs = get_eval_docs(10)        # first 10 rows of the cached SST-2 sample
print(docs.select("text", "label"))
Step 2.

Define a zero-shot classifier. CATEGORIES is ["positive", "negative"].

async def ask(prompt: str) -> tuple[str, int]:
    """One LLM call; returns (text, total tokens)."""
    text, usage, _seconds = await run_delegate_text(make_delegate(temperature=0.0), prompt)
    return text, usage["total_tokens"]


async def zero_shot_classify(text: str) -> tuple[str, int]:
    prompt = f"""Classify the sentiment of the following movie review snippet
into exactly one category.

Categories: {', '.join(CATEGORIES)}

Review: "{text[:800]}"

Respond with ONLY the category name, nothing else."""
    response, tokens = await ask(prompt)
    return normalise_label(response), tokens

Read the prompt carefully. The category list is inline so the model sees exactly which labels are allowed. The text is truncated to 800 characters to bound the prompt length. The closing instruction "ONLY the category name" is the format constraint. normalise_label maps whatever comes back onto positive/negative, or unknown if neither word appears.

Step 3.

Add few-shot examples. A good few-shot example set is diverse, clean (unambiguous), balanced across labels and short (token-efficient).

FEW_SHOT = [
    ("an absolute masterpiece of storytelling and visual style.", "positive"),
    ("a tedious and predictable mess from start to finish.", "negative"),
    ("delightfully clever, with performances that elevate every scene.", "positive"),
    ("fails to land a single emotional beat in over two hours.", "negative"),
]


async def few_shot_classify(text: str) -> tuple[str, int]:
    examples = "\n".join(f'Review: "{t}"\nSentiment: {c}\n' for t, c in FEW_SHOT)
    prompt = f"""Classify the sentiment of movie reviews. Examples:

{examples}
Review: "{text[:800]}"
Sentiment:"""
    response, tokens = await ask(prompt)
    return normalise_label(response), tokens
Step 4.

Add chain-of-thought. The prompt asks for reasoning first, then the label on the last line (which is where normalise_label looks first).

async def cot_classify(text: str) -> tuple[str, str, int]:
    prompt = f"""Classify the sentiment of this movie review as positive or negative.

Think step by step:
1. Identify the key opinion words and their valence
2. Assess whether the overall tone is favourable or unfavourable
3. Consider any sarcasm or irony
4. State your final classification as exactly "positive" or "negative"

Review: "{text[:800]}"

Step-by-step reasoning:"""
    reasoning, tokens = await ask(prompt)
    return normalise_label(reasoning), reasoning, tokens
Step 5.

Run all three on the same ten reviews and compare.

rows = []
for row in docs.iter_rows(named=True):
    zero, zero_tok = await zero_shot_classify(row["text"])
    few, few_tok = await few_shot_classify(row["text"])
    cot, _reasoning, cot_tok = await cot_classify(row["text"])
    rows.append({"review": row["text"][:40], "gold": row["label"],
                 "zero": zero, "few": few, "cot": cot,
                 "zero_tok": zero_tok, "few_tok": few_tok, "cot_tok": cot_tok})

comparison = pl.DataFrame(rows)
print(comparison)
for col in ("zero", "few", "cot"):
    acc = (comparison[col] == comparison["gold"]).mean()
    print(f"{col:5s} accuracy {acc:.0%}   mean tokens {comparison[col + '_tok'].mean():.0f}")

Your numbers depend on your local model and will vary from run to run, so read the shape: few-shot and CoT prompts are several times longer than zero-shot, and CoT spends most of its extra tokens on output. On a task as easy as binary sentiment, the accuracy gap between the three is usually small; rows where the strategies disagree are exactly the ones you would send to a human.

Step 6.

Upgrade to Signature-based extraction for production use — the ReviewExtraction agent from the previous section, run over the same rows. 

FIELDS = ["sentiment", "confidence", "key_phrases", "targets", "tone"]

extracted = []
for text in docs["text"].to_list():
    result = await agent.run_async(review_text=text[:800])
    missing = [f for f in FIELDS if f not in result]
    if missing:                                 # no silent placeholders
        raise RuntimeError(f"agent returned no {missing}: {str(result)[:200]}")
    extracted.append({f: result[f] for f in FIELDS})

print(pl.DataFrame(extracted).select("sentiment", "confidence", "tone"))

The agent writes the prompt for you, calls the model and hands you a dict with one key per output field. You never split strings, and a reply that breaks the schema fails loudly instead of slipping a wrong value into your pipeline.

Mathematical Foundations: Cost, Temperature, and Tokens

The cost model

LLM providers charge per token, with different rates for input (prompt) and output (completion). The bill for one call is:

$$\text{cost} \;=\; N_\text{in}\cdot r_\text{in} + N_\text{out}\cdot r_\text{out}$$
$N_\text{in}$ — prompt tokens $N_\text{out}$ — completion tokens $r$ — rate per token

Prices change often, so treat these as illustrative: a hosted small model might charge $r_\text{in} \approx \$0.15$ and $r_\text{out} \approx \$0.60$ per million tokens, a hosted frontier model about \$3 and \$15. Hosted providers typically charge 4–5× more per output token than per input token. That is why chain-of-thought is more expensive than zero-shot: you are asking the model to produce more output tokens (the reasoning) before the final answer. A local model on Ollama — what this course uses — has $r = 0$; the cost you pay is latency and hardware, which is why Exercise 1 records tokens per technique and converts them at one illustrative blended rate (\$0.50 per million) only to compare techniques.

Temperature and sampling

At inference, the model outputs logits $\ell_1, \ldots, \ell_V$ over the vocabulary. The probability of token $i$ under temperature $T$ is:

$$p_i \;=\; \frac{\exp(\ell_i / T)}{\sum_{j=1}^{V} \exp(\ell_j / T)}$$

When $T \to 0$, the softmax concentrates on the arg-max — the model always picks the highest-logit token (greedy decoding, fully deterministic). When $T \to \infty$, the distribution becomes uniform (pure randomness). In practice: $T = 0$ for classification and extraction; $T \approx 0.7$ for creative generation and self-consistency sampling. Use $T = 0$ whenever you want the same input to produce the same output.

🟢 Rules of thumb for $T$

Classification: $T = 0$. Structured extraction: $T = 0$. Summarisation: $T = 0.2$. Creative writing: $T = 0.7$–$1.0$. Self-consistency sampling: $T = 0.7$ so the chains diverge.

Common Mistakes

⚠ Mistake 1: Parsing free-form text in production

"It worked on my three test cases" is not production-ready. The moment a user types a review that triggers a colon, a newline, or an em-dash inside the model's output, your .split(":")[-1] breaks. Use Signature.

⚠ Mistake 2: Asking for N answers at once

"Here are 20 reviews, classify all of them in one prompt" sounds efficient. It usually is not. The model's error rate compounds — if each row is 95% correct, 20 rows in one prompt is $0.95^{20} \approx 36\%$ fully correct. Parallelise with asyncio.gather instead.

⚠ Mistake 3: No spend control on a priced provider

A bug in a loop that accidentally calls a hosted model 10,000 times with a 2,000-token prompt sends 20 million input tokens — a real bill. On a priced provider, give the Delegate a budget_usd= cap and bound agent loops with max_turns. On local Ollama the dollar cap cannot trip (the model is free, and make_delegate() sets it to None), so watch the token counts instead.

⚠ Mistake 4: "Temperature = 0 means deterministic"

With most providers, $T = 0$ is nearly deterministic, not fully. GPU kernel non-determinism, batching effects, and minor provider-side jitter can still produce different outputs across calls. If you need byte-identical reproducibility for audit, log every prompt and response.

Reflection

In one lesson you have gone from "I know what a transformer is" to "I can classify text with four different prompting strategies and pull structured, typed fields out of an LLM". That is enough to build 80% of the LLM products currently on the market — no fine-tuning, no custom training, just prompting and Signatures.
