Lesson 4.5 Association Rules and Market Basket Analysis

Customers don't buy one item — they buy baskets. Patients don't have one symptom — they have syndromes. Web users don't visit one page — they follow paths. Association rule mining is the classical unsupervised technique for discovering these co-occurrence patterns without assuming any particular model. Today you will meet the Apriori and FP-Growth algorithms, the support / confidence / lift triangle, and how the rules you discover become features for supervised models downstream.

Why This Matters

Thirty years ago, a researcher at a supermarket chain found that customers who bought nappies on Thursday evenings also tended to buy beer. The story is probably apocryphal but the technique it inspired — market basket analysis — is very real. It is a standard way to build "frequently bought together" suggestions, bundle promotions and cross-sell lists. It is one of the oldest unsupervised techniques in the book and still one of the most useful.

Core Concepts

Support, confidence, lift

Let $T$ be the set of all transactions and let $X$ and $Y$ be (disjoint) sets of items. The three fundamental metrics are:

$$ \text{support}(X) \;=\; \frac{|\{t \in T : X \subseteq t\}|}{|T|} $$ $$ \text{confidence}(X \to Y) \;=\; \frac{\text{support}(X \cup Y)}{\text{support}(X)} $$ $$ \text{lift}(X \to Y) \;=\; \frac{\text{confidence}(X \to Y)}{\text{support}(Y)} $$

Support is "how often do we see this combination" — a pure frequency. Confidence is "given that we see $X$, how likely are we to also see $Y$" — a conditional probability. Lift is "is $Y$ more likely when $X$ is present than it would be by random chance?" — lift > 1 means positive association, lift < 1 means negative, lift ≈ 1 means independent.

Example: if 20% of baskets contain bread and 10% contain butter, and 5% contain both, then:

  • support(bread ∧ butter) = 0.05
  • confidence(bread → butter) = 0.05 / 0.20 = 0.25 — 25% of bread-buyers also buy butter
  • lift(bread → butter) = 0.25 / 0.10 = 2.5 — bread-buyers are 2.5× more likely to buy butter than a random customer

Lift is the metric you report to a marketing manager. A rule with high confidence but low lift tells you the consequent is common, not that there is a real association.

🔵 Theory

Lift is the ratio of the joint probability to the product of the marginals: $\text{lift}(X \to Y) = P(X \cap Y) / (P(X) P(Y))$. For independent events this is exactly 1. For perfectly correlated events ($X$ present exactly when $Y$ is) it is $1 / P(X)$. Its logarithm, $\log \text{lift}$, is the pointwise mutual information of $X$ and $Y$ — the same quantity Lesson 4.6 uses (normalised) to score topic coherence. Lift is symmetric: $\text{lift}(X \to Y) = \text{lift}(Y \to X)$; confidence is not.

Apriori — the classical algorithm

Association rule mining has two stages: (1) find all itemsets with support above a minimum threshold, and (2) generate rules from those itemsets. Stage (1) is the expensive one — there are $2^d$ possible itemsets over $d$ items, so brute-forcing is impossible. The Apriori property saves you:

🔵 Apriori property

If an itemset $X$ is not frequent (support below threshold), then no superset of $X$ is frequent either. Equivalently: every subset of a frequent itemset is itself frequent.

Apriori uses this property to prune the search space. It proceeds level by level: find all frequent 1-itemsets, use them to generate candidate 2-itemsets, prune those that contain an infrequent subset, count the survivors, repeat. In practice it scales to thousands of items and millions of transactions, but slows dramatically when itemsets get long.

FP-Growth — Apriori's faster cousin

Apriori has one big drawback: it scans the transaction database once per level. For long itemsets that is many scans. FP-Growth (Frequent Pattern Growth) avoids the candidate-generation step entirely. It makes two passes over the data to build a compressed structure called an FP-tree — one to count single items, one to insert each transaction with its items in frequency order — and then mines frequent itemsets by recursively walking the tree. On large, dense transaction logs with low support thresholds it is typically much faster than Apriori, and it is available in mlxtend and Spark MLlib. On small problems the difference disappears and can even reverse: on Exercise 5's 2,500 baskets Apriori is the faster of the two.

You do not need to implement FP-Growth from scratch — understanding that it exists, that it is faster than Apriori, and that it gives the same answer is enough. In the exercise you will use both.

From rules to features

A key design point: association rules are not an end in themselves. The discovered rules become features for supervised models. Example: if you find that "purchased item A AND item B → 75% probability of purchasing item C", you can add a binary feature has_AB_pattern to your feature store and let a classifier learn how to use it. This is the feed-forward from unsupervised discovery into supervised decisions — the same story as using cluster labels as features in Lesson 4.1.

Mathematical Foundations

The combinatorial explosion

With $d$ items there are $2^d - 1$ non-empty itemsets. For $d = 100$ that is $\approx 10^{30}$ — a thousand million million million billion itemsets. Apriori's pruning is what makes the problem tractable: under a minimum support threshold, the number of itemsets you actually examine is typically a few orders of magnitude smaller than the worst case.

Conviction — beyond lift

An alternative to lift is conviction:

$$ \text{conv}(X \to Y) \;=\; \frac{1 - \text{support}(Y)}{1 - \text{confidence}(X \to Y)} $$

Conviction measures how much the rule improves over a model where $X$ and $Y$ are independent: conviction of 1 means the rule is no better than random, higher values mean stronger implication. Conviction is asymmetric (unlike lift) and is useful when direction matters — how reliably $X$ implies $Y$ rather than merely co-occurring with it. A rule with confidence 1 has infinite conviction. Like every metric here it measures association, not causation.

The Kailash Engine: none — rules become features

⚙ Kailash Engine

kailash-ml has no association-rule engine, and you should not go looking for one. The mining itself is a few lines of Python (the Apriori principle above); for larger problems the open-source mlxtend library provides Apriori and FP-Growth. The Kailash value enters after mining: discovered rules become ordinary feature columns (built in polars) for the supervised pipeline you built in Module 3, and Exercise 5 logs every mining run to the ExperimentTracker so you can compare thresholds and algorithms.

Worked Example: Singapore Mini-Mart Baskets

The baskets here are synthetic. Exercise 5's generator (shared.mlfp04.ex_5.generate_transactions) simulates 2,500 transactions at a neighbourhood mini-mart with 25 products. Twelve "bundles" — kaya-toast breakfast (bread, butter, eggs), kopi (coffee, condensed milk, sugar), beer and chips, toiletries, household cleaning and so on — fire with known probabilities, each item in a firing bundle is dropped 15% of the time, and a few random impulse items are added. Because we know the bundles, we can check whether mining recovers them. 

import numpy as np
import polars as pl
from shared.mlfp04.ex_5 import generate_transactions, transactions_to_onehot

transactions = generate_transactions(n=2500, seed=42)   # list of sets of product names
basket = transactions_to_onehot(transactions)           # polars: one boolean column per product

# Pair rules from first principles: one matrix product counts every pair
B = basket.to_numpy().astype(np.float64)                # 2,500 x 25 indicator matrix
items, n = basket.columns, B.shape[0]
support_1 = B.mean(axis=0)                              # supp(X) for every item
support_2 = (B.T @ B) / n                               # supp(X and Y) for every pair

pair_rules = pl.DataFrame(
    [
        (items[i], items[j], support_2[i, j],
         support_2[i, j] / support_1[i],                          # confidence
         support_2[i, j] / (support_1[i] * support_1[j]))         # lift
        for i in range(len(items)) for j in range(len(items))
        if i != j and support_2[i, j] >= 0.02
    ],
    schema=["antecedent", "consequent", "support", "confidence", "lift"],
    orient="row",
).sort("lift", descending=True)
print(pair_rules.head(6))

The top pairs are shampoo ↔ toothpaste (lift 3.02), cooking oil ↔ fish (2.93), coffee ↔ condensed milk (2.92) and detergent ↔ tissue (2.91) — all pairs from bundles in the generator. Each pair appears in both directions with the same lift but different confidence (coffee → condensed milk 0.52, condensed milk → coffee 0.54).

For itemsets larger than pairs we use mlxtend. It accepts only a pandas DataFrame, so we convert at the call boundary only and bring the result straight back into polars; all filtering and sorting stays in polars, as in Exercise 5.2. 

from mlxtend.frequent_patterns import association_rules, fpgrowth

freq_items = fpgrowth(basket.to_pandas(), min_support=0.02, use_colnames=True)
rules_raw = association_rules(freq_items, metric="lift", min_threshold=1.2)

rules = pl.DataFrame({
    "antecedent": [", ".join(sorted(a)) for a in rules_raw["antecedents"]],
    "consequent": [", ".join(sorted(c)) for c in rules_raw["consequents"]],
    "support": rules_raw["support"].to_numpy(),
    "confidence": rules_raw["confidence"].to_numpy(),
    "lift": rules_raw["lift"].to_numpy(),
}).sort("lift", descending=True)
print(f"Frequent itemsets: {len(freq_items)}   rules with lift >= 1.2: {rules.height}")
print(rules.head(6))

At a minimum support of 2%, FP-Growth finds 336 frequent itemsets (25 single items, 226 pairs, 77 triples, 8 four-item sets) and 494 rules with lift of at least 1.2. The strongest are three-item bundles: {soap, tissue} → {detergent} with confidence 0.76 and lift 6.0, {soap, toothpaste} → {shampoo} (0.75, 5.6) and {detergent, soap} → {tissue} (0.72, 5.6).

Read {soap, tissue} → {detergent} as: 3.8% of all baskets contain all three items (support); 76% of baskets with soap and tissue also contain detergent (confidence); and that is 6.0 times the rate at which detergent appears in baskets in general (lift). For a mini-mart this suggests shelving household cleaning together or a "household restock" bundle. The reverse rule {detergent} → {soap, tissue} has the same lift but a confidence of only 0.30 — most detergent buyers do not buy the other two — so the promotion should be triggered by soap and tissue, not by detergent. And because the baskets are synthetic, we can confirm the method works: every top rule is a fragment of one of the generator's bundles.

Common Mistakes

  1. Setting min_support too low. If you allow itemsets that appear in only 0.001% of baskets, you get millions of meaningless rules. Start at 1% and adjust.
  2. Reporting confidence instead of lift. High confidence means "the consequent is common", not "there is an association". Lift is the metric stakeholders should see.
  3. Ignoring reverse direction. $A \to B$ and $B \to A$ have different confidences — present both if direction matters.
  4. Treating rules as causal. Lift measures association, not causation. A rule is a correlation, not a causal claim.

Reflection

Association rules are the oldest trick in the unsupervised book and they still earn their keep. The algorithm is simple, the metrics are interpretable, and the output is a list of rules that a non-technical stakeholder can read and argue about. This is unusual in modern ML — most algorithms produce predictions, not explanations. When you need to have a meeting about why, association rules are often the easiest place to start.
