Speaker Notes — Lesson 4.5 Association Rules and Market Basket Analysis

Total time: ~60 minutes  ·  16 slides  ·  ~3.5 min per slide average

A shorter, lighter lesson — the algorithms are simple and the value is in the metric discipline. The one battle to fight: confidence is not lift. Students will arrive reporting "69% confidence!" as a finding; they should leave reporting "5.1× the base rate". The feed-forward to features (rules as supervised inputs) is the M3 bridge that makes this more than a retail party trick.

Slide 1~1 minTitle

Association Rules and Market Basket Analysis

Open: "Every unsupervised method so far worked on rows of numbers. Today's data is baskets — sets of items. The question: what appears together more often than chance? Outcome: discover retail patterns and turn them into features."

→ "The oldest trick in the book."
Slide 2~3 minOldest trick

The oldest unsupervised trick in the book

Frame it: customers don't buy one item — they buy baskets. The goal is to find items that co-occur more than chance. The output: rules of the form "A ⇒ B" with three statistics attached.

Read the green box: "Still powers 'frequently bought together' widgets everywhere. Thirty-year-old algorithm, billion-dollar widget."

→ "The pipeline."
Slide 3~3 minPipeline

The pipeline

Walk the three boxes on the toy data. Five transactions on the left. The middle box: frequent itemsets with their supports — {milk} 0.8, {bread} 0.6, {milk, bread} 0.4. The right box: rules with confidence and lift — milk → bread at confidence 0.50 but lift 0.83; bread → butter at 0.67 and 1.11.

"Two stages: find the frequent sets, then generate rules from them. And notice milk → bread already: confident-ish, but lift below 1. Hold that thought."

→ "The three metrics."
Slide 4~3 minThree metrics

Three metrics

Walk the three formulas. Support: $P(X)$ — how common is the itemset. Confidence: $P(Y|X)$ — how often the rule fires. Lift: $P(X \cap Y) / (P(X)P(Y))$ — how the co-occurrence compares to independence.

"Support filters, confidence directs, lift judges. You need all three because they answer three different questions."

→ "Why lift is the one you report."
Slide 5~4 minLift

Lift is the metric you report

Walk the four bullets. Lift = 1: independent — the rule tells you nothing. Lift > 1: positive association. Lift < 1: negative — one substitutes for the other; also a finding, but not a cross-sell. And the killer line: "High confidence + low lift means the consequent is just common. Everyone buys bread; of course milk-buyers buy bread. Not a finding."

Read the amber box: "Rookie mistake: reporting confidence without lift. Confidence measures reliability of the rule; lift measures whether the rule exists."

→ "Numbers."
Slide 6~3 minNumerical example

Numerical example

Do the arithmetic at the board: $P(\text{bread}) = 0.20$, $P(\text{butter}) = 0.10$, $P(\text{both}) = 0.05$. Confidence (bread → butter) = 0.05/0.20 = 0.25. Lift = 0.25/0.10 = 2.5.

"25% of bread-buyers buy butter — sounds modest. But it's 2.5× the base rate — a real association. Confidence alone would have undersold it; that's why we normalise by the consequent's popularity."

→ "How do we find frequent sets without checking $2^d$ of them?"
Slide 7~3 minApriori property

Apriori property

Read the blue box both ways: "If an itemset is not frequent, no superset of it is frequent. Equivalently: every subset of a frequent itemset is itself frequent."

"This is what saves us from $2^d$ itemsets. Prune an itemset and you prune its entire subtree of supersets — most of the search space vanishes without being counted."

→ "See the pruning."
Slide 8~3 minApriori picture

Apriori in pictures

Walk the levels. L1: {A}, {B}, {C} survive; {D} fails support — red box. L2: pairs built only from survivors — {A,D} is greyed out, never even counted, because D died at L1. L3: {A,B,C}.

Read the italic line: "{A,D} pruned without counting → {A,B,D}, {A,C,D} never considered. One dead item kills whole branches of the lattice. Level by level, candidate, count, prune."

→ "The faster cousin."
Slide 9~3 minFP-Growth

FP-Growth — faster cousin

Walk the bullets. FP-Growth builds a compressed FP-tree in two passes — count items, insert each basket in frequency order — then mines the tree recursively. No candidate generation at all. Usually much faster than Apriori on large or dense data. Same output: identical frequent itemsets.

Read the footer: "The usual choice in practice — mlxtend, Spark MLlib. Apriori is the one you implement by hand to understand pruning; FP-Growth is the one you call in production."

→ "Run it on the baskets."
Slide 10~4 minWorked example

Worked example: Singapore retail baskets

Walk the code. generate_transactions() gives 2,500 synthetic SG baskets; transactions_to_onehot makes a polars frame with one boolean column per product. Then mlxtend's fpgrowth at min_support 0.03 and association_rules at lift ≥ 2.0.

Read the footer — two teaching points. "mlxtend only accepts pandas, so convert at the call boundary with .to_pandas() and return to polars straight away — the conversion is an interop detail, not a workflow. And the results: 159 frequent itemsets, 126 rules at lift ≥ 2."

→ "Read one rule like a stakeholder."
Slide 11~4 minInterpreting a rule

Interpreting a rule

Walk the printout. {cooking_oil, rice} → {fish}: support 0.044, confidence 0.69, lift 5.1. Reverse it: {fish} → {cooking_oil, rice}: same support, confidence 0.33 — same lift 5.1.

"Business reading: 69% of baskets with cooking oil and rice also hold fish — 5.1× the base rate. Note the asymmetry: lift is symmetric, confidence is not. Direction matters for the story you tell and the action you take — a three-item promo, shelf placement, or a recommendation widget."

→ "Feed the rules forward."
Slide 12~4 minRules as features

Rules as features — the feed-forward

Walk the code: materialise a binary flag from the rule's antecedent — cooking_oil & rice becomes a column — and hand the enriched frame to a classifier.

Read the teal box: "There is no Kailash association-rule engine — mine with mlxtend, materialise flags with polars. Exercise 5.4 predicts a shopper's next trip: the product-presence baseline reaches AUC ≈ 0.69, and whether rule flags add signal is a real empirical question — 'no lift' is a valid finding. Discovery doesn't guarantee downstream value; you measure it."

→ "One more metric for directional rules."
Slide 13~3 minConviction

Conviction — direction matters

Walk the formula: $(1 - \text{support}(Y)) / (1 - \text{confidence}(X \to Y))$ — how much better than independence the rule performs, asymmetrically. "Lift treats both directions the same; conviction doesn't. Reach for it when the rule's direction is causally meaningful — 'insurance → alarm system' tells a different story than its reverse."

→ "Four mistakes."
Slide 14~3 minMistakes

Four rookie mistakes

Walk the four. One: min_support too low — millions of noise rules bury the fifty good ones. Two: reporting confidence instead of lift — the lesson's refrain. Three: ignoring the reverse direction — 0.69 one way, 0.33 the other; they imply different actions. Four: treating rules as causal claims — co-occurrence is not causation; 2.7's discipline applies here too.

→ "Where this connects."
Slide 15~2 minCross-references

Cross-references

Walk the three links. M3: manual feature engineering — rules are automated feature engineering. 4.7: rules find discrete co-occurrences; matrix factorisation finds continuous latent structure in the same co-occurrence data. 4.6: topic modelling is association mining for words — same discovery problem, different atoms.

→ "Recap and bridge."
Slide 16~2 minRecap

What you can now do

Walk the bullets: compute support/confidence/lift, apply Apriori and FP-Growth, interpret rules for stakeholders, materialise rules as features, and avoid the confidence-lift confusion. Tease 4.6: "Same discovery problem, but the 'baskets' are documents and the 'items' are words — NLP and topic modelling."
