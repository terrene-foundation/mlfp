Lesson 4.4 Anomaly Detection and Ensembles

Clustering asked which group a point belongs to. Anomaly detection asks the opposite question: which points do not belong to any group? Today you will learn four methods — statistical (Z-score, IQR), Isolation Forest, and Local Outlier Factor — and the ensemble pattern for blending their scores. By the end you will be able to rank suspicious credit applications without any labelled fraud examples, and you will know why no single detector catches every kind of anomaly.

Why This Matters

Fraud, defective products, faulty sensors, network intrusions, churn precursors — every business has rare but critical events that matter more than the common ones. You usually do not have enough labelled examples to train a supervised classifier (fraud may be 0.1% of transactions). Anomaly detection finds the strange points without labels, by modelling what "normal" looks like and flagging everything that deviates.

🟢 Foundations

Anomaly detection is an unsupervised problem with a supervised framing: you are implicitly classifying points as "normal" or "anomalous". But you do not have labels — you have to infer normality from the shape of the data itself.

Core Concepts

Statistical outliers: Z-score and IQR

The simplest anomaly detectors look at one feature at a time. The Z-score method standardises and thresholds:

$$ z_i \;=\; \frac{x_i - \bar{x}}{s} \qquad\text{flag as outlier if } |z_i| > 3 $$

The "3-sigma rule" catches the ~0.27% of points that would be in the tails of a normal distribution. The trade-off is obvious: the method assumes the data is normally distributed, which is rarely true for business quantities (revenue, session duration, basket size are right-skewed), and a few extreme values inflate both $\bar{x}$ and $s$, hiding everything else.

The IQR method is more robust:

$$ \text{outlier if } x < Q_1 - 1.5\,\text{IQR}\quad\text{or}\quad x > Q_3 + 1.5\,\text{IQR} $$

where $\text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1$ is the interquartile range. Because quartiles are not affected by extreme values, IQR is the standard univariate outlier rule for skewed data. It is also the method behind box plot whiskers.

Winsorisation

Winsorising is a related technique — instead of removing outliers, cap them at a percentile (typically 1st and 99th). This preserves sample size and makes downstream statistics robust without losing information about the fact that a point was extreme. In production, winsorise before you cluster or regress, not instead of investigating outliers.

Isolation Forest

Statistical methods only look at one feature at a time. Real anomalies are usually strange combinations of normal values — a small transaction is normal, a midnight transaction is normal, but a small midnight transaction from a new device is suspicious. You need a multivariate method.

Isolation Forest is a beautifully simple idea: anomalies are points that can be "isolated" with fewer random splits. Build a random tree by picking a feature and a split value uniformly at random. Count how many splits are needed to isolate each point into its own leaf. Normal points require many splits (they are in the dense core); anomalies require few (they are out on their own).

Formally, the anomaly score is

$$ s(\mathbf{x}, n) \;=\; 2^{-E(h(\mathbf{x}))/c(n)} $$

where $E(h(\mathbf{x}))$ is the average path length from root to the leaf containing $\mathbf{x}$ across all trees, $n$ is the sample size, and $c(n)$ is the expected path length of a random binary tree with $n$ leaves. Read the exponent $-E(h)/c(n)$: if a point is isolated in far fewer splits than average, $E(h)/c(n)$ is close to 0, the exponent approaches 0 and $s \to 1$ (anomalous); if $E(h) \approx c(n)$, the exponent is about $-1$ and $s \approx 0.5$ (no evidence either way); if $E(h) \gg c(n)$, the exponent is large and negative and $s \to 0$ (very normal). In practice you rarely threshold $s$ directly: you rank by score and flag the top fraction you can afford to review. Note that scikit-learn's decision_function uses the opposite orientation — lower means more anomalous.

LOF (Local Outlier Factor)

Isolation Forest is a global method — it does not care whether a point is in a dense region or a sparse one. LOF is local: it measures the density around a point and compares it to the density around its neighbours. A point in a region much sparser than its neighbours is flagged.

Formally, let $N_k(\mathbf{x})$ be the $k$ nearest neighbours of $\mathbf{x}$ and let $\text{lrd}_k(\mathbf{x})$ be the local reachability density (inverse of the average reachability distance to neighbours). Then

$$ \text{LOF}_k(\mathbf{x}) \;=\; \frac{1}{|N_k(\mathbf{x})|}\sum_{\mathbf{o} \in N_k(\mathbf{x})}\ \frac{\text{lrd}_k(\mathbf{o})}{\text{lrd}_k(\mathbf{x})} $$

$\text{LOF} \approx 1$ means "same density as neighbours" (normal). $\text{LOF} > 1.5$ typically flags an anomaly. LOF is the right choice when your data has clusters of varying density — a point that is "close" by absolute distance can still be an outlier relative to its own neighbourhood.

Score blending

Each method catches different anomalies. Z-score catches tail events on individual features. Isolation Forest catches multivariate outliers. LOF catches local density anomalies. Running several and blending the scores protects you against relying on the one detector that is blind to the kind of anomaly you actually have.

The blending pattern is: (1) put every detector on the same orientation — higher = more anomalous (scikit-learn's IsolationForest.decision_function and LOF's negative_outlier_factor_ are both lower = more anomalous, so negate them); (2) put them on the same scale — min-max to $[0, 1]$, or ranks; (3) average them (or take the max, or a weighted sum) and apply a single threshold. Blending is insurance, not a guarantee: an equal-weight blend can score below the best single detector, because averaging in a weak detector dilutes a strong one (the worked example shows this). If labelled cases exist, tune the weights on one labelled sample and measure on another.

The masking trap

LOF compares a point with its $k$ nearest neighbours. If anomalies arrive as a tight group — say 40 near-identical applications from a coordinated ring — and $k$ is smaller than the group, every member's neighbours are the other members. Their local density matches their neighbours' density, LOF is close to 1, and the whole group looks normal. This is masking: LOF does not flag a tight, dense cluster of frauds. The cure is a neighbourhood larger than any plausible anomalous group, so it reaches the normal data around it.

Mathematical Foundations

Expected path length in a random binary tree

The normalisation constant $c(n)$ in Isolation Forest is the expected path length from root to leaf in a random binary search tree with $n$ elements — a classical result:

$$ c(n) \;=\; 2 H(n-1) - 2(n-1)/n \qquad\text{where } H(k) \approx \ln k + 0.5772 $$

This is what makes the anomaly score comparable across different sample sizes: without the $c(n)$ normalisation, a point's raw path length would scale with $\log n$ and the scores would not be interpretable.

Why unsupervised anomaly detection works at all

Every method here rests on a single assumption: anomalies are rare and different. If anomalies make up more than ~10% of your data, or if they look indistinguishable from normal points until you know the label, unsupervised detection fails and you need a supervised classifier (or semi-supervised methods with a small labelled set). Always check: is the assumption justified for your problem?

The Kailash Engines: AnomalyDetectionEngine and EnsembleEngine

⚙ Kailash Engine

AnomalyDetectionEngine fits Isolation Forest, LOF or a one-class SVM behind one detect() call. It flips scikit-learn's sign convention so that a higher score always means more anomalous, normalises the scores to $[0, 1]$ and applies the contamination threshold; labels follow scikit-learn ($-1$ = anomaly, $1$ = normal). ensemble_detect() runs several detectors and combines them: voting="score_average" averages the normalised scores (the blend above); the default "majority" votes on the labels. Extra keyword arguments such as n_neighbors pass through to the detector.

EnsembleEngine is a different tool. Its blend(), stack(), bag() and boost() combine supervised models: blend(models, data, target, weights=..., method="soft") votes fitted classifiers against a target column. It cannot blend unsupervised anomaly scores — there is no target. It becomes useful once analysts have labelled a review sample: a classifier trained on the detector scores learns which detector to trust (Exercise 4.4).

DriftMonitor (Module 3, Lesson 3.8) is not an anomaly detector. It tests whether a whole feature distribution has shifted — population stability index, Kolmogorov–Smirnov, Jensen–Shannon and chi-squared tests — rather than scoring individual rows. "Is this application strange?" is anomaly detection; "is this week's applicant population different from last month's?" is drift monitoring.

Worked Example: Anomalies in Credit Applications

Real anomaly labels are rare, and a label made by thresholding a feature ("fraud = top 1% of returns") makes every evaluation circular — the detector that looks at that column wins by construction. Exercise 4 therefore follows the standard benchmark recipe: take 20,000 real applications from the course's Singapore credit-scoring dataset (mlfp02/sg_credit_scoring.parquet, 20 numeric fields) as the normal population and inject 200 anomalies of three known types, so the label comes from the injection, never from a feature threshold:

  • global (80) — a real application with one field pushed 5–8 standard deviations above the mean (a fat-finger entry);
  • dependency (80) — every field copied from a different real application: a "synthetic identity" whose values are each plausible but whose combination is not;
  • clustered (40) — a tight group of near-identical applications shifted 3 standard deviations on three fields: a coordinated application ring.

The detectors never see the label; it is used only to score them.

from sklearn.ensemble import IsolationForest
from sklearn.metrics import average_precision_score, roc_auc_score
from sklearn.neighbors import LocalOutlierFactor
from shared.mlfp04.ex_4 import auc_by_type

# Method 1: Z-score — the most extreme standardised field of each row
z_scores = np.abs(X).max(axis=1)

# Method 2: Isolation Forest (negate decision_function: higher = more anomalous)
iforest = IsolationForest(n_estimators=200, contamination=0.01, random_state=42).fit(X)
iforest_scores = -iforest.decision_function(X)

# Method 3: LOF with a neighbourhood larger than any plausible ring
lof = LocalOutlierFactor(n_neighbors=50, contamination=0.01)
lof.fit_predict(X)
lof_scores = -lof.negative_outlier_factor_

def normalise(scores):
    return (scores - scores.min()) / (scores.max() - scores.min())

blended = (normalise(z_scores) + normalise(iforest_scores) + normalise(lof_scores)) / 3

for name, sc in [("Z-score", z_scores), ("Isolation Forest", iforest_scores),
                 ("LOF (k=50)", lof_scores), ("Equal blend", blended)]:
    top = sc > np.quantile(sc, 0.99)   # flag the top 1%, about 202 rows
    per_type = "  ".join(f"{t}={v:.2f}" for t, v in auc_by_type(frame, sc).items())
    print(f"{name:<17} AUC={roc_auc_score(y, sc):.3f}  AP={average_precision_score(y, sc):.3f}  "
          f"hits in top 1%={int(y[top].sum()):>3}  |  {per_type}")

Read the table by column. The Z-score finds single-field extremes (global ≈ 1.00) but is blind to synthetic identities (0.55, barely better than chance) — no single field is unusual; LOF catches them (0.98) because their combination of values is rare. Isolation Forest ranks the injected anomalies above most applications (AUC 0.77) but almost never at the very top — only 3 of its 202 highest-scoring rows are injected: real credit data have heavy tails of genuinely extreme but legitimate applications, and those are what it isolates first. "Statistically unusual" and "the anomaly you are looking for" are different questions. The equal blend's worst type (0.80) is far better than the Z-score's 0.55, but its overall AUC (0.91) is below LOF alone (0.97).

Now the masking trap, directly:

for k in (5, 20, 50):
    lof_k = LocalOutlierFactor(n_neighbors=k).fit(X)
    by_type = auc_by_type(frame, -lof_k.negative_outlier_factor_)
    print(f"k={k:<3} " + "  ".join(f"{t}={v:.2f}" for t, v in by_type.items()))

All three settings catch the global and dependency anomalies. The ring is the difference: AUC about 0.36 at $k = 5$ and 0.22 at $k = 20$ — worse than random, LOF actively ranks the ring members as more normal than real applications — and 0.89 at $k = 50$. With 40 near-identical members, any $k$ below 40 finds only fellow members as neighbours. The practical lesson: look at the overlap between detectors, choose $k$ larger than any plausible coordinated group, and send the top of each detector's list to human review.

Common Mistakes

  1. Setting contamination too high. contamination tells Isolation Forest and LOF "approximately what fraction of the data should I flag". It only moves the threshold — the ranking of points is unchanged — so setting it to 0.1 when your real anomaly rate is 0.001 flags about 100 times too many points, nearly all of them normal. Set it from the base rate and the review capacity you actually have.
  2. Using Z-score on skewed data. For revenue, session duration, basket size — anything right-skewed — use IQR or log-transform first.
  3. Not standardising before multivariate methods. Same rule as clustering: scale-sensitive methods need standardised inputs.
  4. Treating anomaly scores as probabilities. They are not — different methods use different ranges, scales and even orientations. Put every score on "higher = more anomalous" and normalise to $[0, 1]$ (or use ranks) before blending or thresholding.
  5. Using a small LOF neighbourhood against coordinated anomalies. A ring of near-identical anomalies larger than $k$ masks itself (ring AUC 0.22 at $k = 20$ in the worked example). Choose $k$ larger than any plausible anomalous group.

Reflection

Anomaly detection is deceptively hard in practice. The algorithms are simple; the difficulty is the labelling. Because you do not have a ground truth, you cannot compute precision or recall — the only way to evaluate a detector is to investigate the top-$k$ flagged points and ask a domain expert "is this an anomaly?". The engineering lesson is that unsupervised methods are starting points, not finish lines. They tell you where to look, and a human decides what to do.
