Speaker Notes — Lesson 4.3 Dimensionality Reduction

Total time: ~90 minutes  ·  21 slides  ·  ~4 min per slide average

The longest lesson of M4 and the most visual. The through-line: PCA is the only reduction method that's also a feature method — t-SNE and UMAP are for seeing, not for feeding downstream. Two honesty points to land hard: trustworthiness (not silhouette) is how you rank reducers, and UMAP's inter-cluster distances are still not quantitative. The worked example's answer — 7 features compress to only 5 — is itself a teaching moment about redundancy.

Slide 1~1 minTitle

Dimensionality Reduction

Open with the outcome: "4.1 and 4.2 asked 'which group does this point belong to?' Today: 'which axis does the data actually live along?' Outcome: find how many of 7 customer features you really need."

→ "From groups to axes."
Slide 2~3 minGroups to axes

From "which group" to "which axis"

Draw the contrast: clustering partitioned the points; dimensionality reduction rotates the features. Goal: fewer features, most variance kept. Uses: visualisation, compression, feature extraction.

Read the green box: "If two features carry the same information, you have one feature's worth of signal. Reduction finds the non-redundant directions."

→ "The rotation, drawn."
Slide 3~3 minPCA picture

PCA rotates the axes to the data

Walk the two panels. Left: the data stretched diagonally across the original $x_1, x_2$ axes — neither axis alone captures the spread. Right: PCA's rotation — PC1 lies along the long direction of the cloud, PC2 across it. "PCA doesn't create new information. It re-aims the coordinate system so the first axis carries the most variance possible, the second the most of what's left."

→ "One Lagrangian."
Slide 4~4 minPCA derivation

Deriving PCA — one Lagrangian

Set up the problem: maximise the projected variance $\mathbf{w}^\top C \mathbf{w}$ subject to $\|\mathbf{w}\| = 1$. One Lagrangian later: $C\mathbf{w} = \lambda\mathbf{w}$ — an eigenvalue equation.

Read the blue box: "The solution is the top eigenvector of the covariance matrix, and the variance it captures is the eigenvalue. PCA is eigen-decomposition of a covariance matrix — everything else is bookkeeping."

→ "Nobody computes it that way. SVD."
Slide 5~3 minSVD

SVD = PCA's computational engine

Walk the factorisation picture: $X = U\Sigma V^\top$ — $U$ the left singular vectors, $\Sigma$ the diagonal of singular values, $V^\top$ the right singular vectors. "The columns of $V$ are the principal directions; $\Sigma^2/(N-1)$ gives the variances. Numerically, every serious PCA goes through SVD — it never forms the covariance matrix, so it's faster and better conditioned."

→ "How many to keep?"
Slide 6~3 minScree plot

Scree plot: how many components?

Walk the bar chart: variance per component, falling off. The red dashed line marks the elbow at $k \approx 4$ — where the bars stop falling fast and start tailing.

Read the rule: "Take the smallest $k$ where cumulative variance reaches your threshold — 90–95% is the usual range. The elbow is the visual version; the threshold is the operational one."

→ "But what do the components mean?"
Slide 7~4 minLoadings

Loadings make PCA interpretable

Walk the code: sort each component's weights by magnitude and print the top features. Then read the answer on the slide: on the 7 standardised customer features, PC1 = +0.71 avg order value + 0.65 revenue − 0.28 order count — "spend per order", 26.5% of the variance.

"A component you can't name is a component you shouldn't ship. The loadings turn 'PC1' into a sentence a stakeholder can argue with — that's what makes PCA a feature method and not just a compressor."

→ "When the structure is curved."
Slide 8~4 minKernel PCA

Kernel PCA — for curved structure

Walk the bullets. Standard PCA can only rotate — a curved manifold stays curved. Kernel PCA runs PCA in an implicit feature space via the kernel trick — the same trick as 3.3's SVM. Common kernels: RBF, polynomial.

Two costs to name: "$O(N^2)$ memory — only viable for a few thousand points. And there's no exact inverse: mapping back to input space — the pre-image problem — needs an approximation like fit_inverse_transform=True."

→ "The famous one: t-SNE."
Slide 9~4 mint-SNE

t-SNE: good for plots, bad for features

Walk the objective: minimise the KL divergence between pairwise neighbourhood distributions in high-dim and low-dim. "It preserves local structure — who your neighbours are — and distorts global structure — how clusters relate. Non-deterministic, no inverse transform, and the knob is perplexity, roughly 5–50."

Read the amber box as a rule of the course: "Never use t-SNE for feature extraction. The coordinates have no consistent meaning across runs, there's no transform for new points, and distances between clusters are fiction. Use it only to see."

→ "The modern default: UMAP."
Slide 10~3 minUMAP

UMAP: newer, faster, better defaults

Walk the four bullets. Better global layout than t-SNE — but cluster sizes and gaps are still not quantitative. About 10× faster; scales to millions of points. It can embed new points with .transform() — t-SNE cannot. And it's deterministic given a seed.

Read the footer: "Default choice for any visualisation task. Fall back to t-SNE only when specifically needed."

→ "Head to head."
Slide 11~3 mint-SNE vs UMAP

t-SNE vs UMAP at a glance

Walk the table quickly — the rows students actually use: speed (UMAP fast; t-SNE's Barnes-Hut is $O(n\log n)$ with a large constant, exact is $O(n^2)$), global structure (poor vs better- but-still-qualitative), new-point embedding (no vs yes), determinism (no vs yes with seed), key knob (perplexity vs n_neighbors), best for (dense small data vs everything else).

→ "See the guarantees, not the marketing."
Slide 12~4 minPreservation picture

Same data, different preservation guarantees

Walk the two panels — same five clusters through both methods. t-SNE: tight beautiful blobs, and the caption says the quiet part — inter-cluster distances are meaningless. UMAP: better layout — related clusters sit near each other — and its caption: sizes and inter-cluster distances still not quantitative; the dashed connectors are layout hints only.

"Both are microscopes, not maps. You may say 'these points are neighbours'. You may never say 'cluster A is twice as far from B as from C' — not even with UMAP."

→ "The classics, for completeness."
Slide 13~3 minOther manifolds

Other manifold learners: Isomap, LLE, MDS

Walk the table briefly — this is a reference slide. MDS preserves all pairwise distances — the pick when you only have a dissimilarity matrix. Isomap preserves geodesic distances along a k-NN graph — for unrolling one smooth manifold like the Swiss roll. LLE preserves each point as a weighted mix of its neighbours — for small, clean, locally linear manifolds.

Read the footer: "All in sklearn.manifold. In practice UMAP has replaced them for visualisation — know them for the ideas: distances, geodesics, local linearity."

→ "How do you rank reducers honestly?"
Slide 14~4 minTrustworthiness

Comparing reducers: trustworthiness, not silhouette

Walk the first bullet — the circularity trap: "t-SNE and UMAP pull points into blobs by construction. Running K-means silhouette inside the embedding rewards the picture the method manufactured, not faithfulness to the data."

Then the metric: trustworthiness in [0, 1] — are a point's embedding neighbours its real neighbours? 1 means no false neighbours. Walk the code: computed on the same 3,000-row subsample for every reducer — it needs all pairwise distances. "Exercise 3.5 ranks PCA, kernel PCA, t-SNE, UMAP, and Isomap by trustworthiness; silhouette survives only as a 'clusterability' column."

→ "And PCA's crown: provable optimality."
Slide 15~3 minEckart-Young

Eckart-Young: PCA is optimal

Walk the theorem: the best rank-$k$ approximation in Frobenius norm has error equal to the sum of the discarded squared singular values — achieved by the truncated SVD.

Read the blue box: "PCA is provably the best linear low-rank approximation. No linear method beats it. When someone proposes a fancier linear reduction, this theorem is the answer."

→ "So how many dimensions are really there?"
Slide 16~4 minIntrinsic dimension

Intrinsic dimension

Walk the four estimators. Scree elbow — visual. Variance threshold — smallest $k$ hitting 90–95%. Participation ratio — $(\sum\lambda)^2 / \sum\lambda^2$, an effective count of active directions. Nearest-neighbour MLE (Levina–Bickel) — uses neighbour-distance ratios; non-linear, so it sees curved manifolds PCA cannot.

Read the footer numbers: "Customer data: 90% → 5 PCs, 95% → 6 PCs, participation ratio ≈ 5.3 — three methods agreeing. And the sanity check: the MLE returns ≈ 8 on 8-D Gaussian noise — it recovers the truth when we know it."

→ "Compress the customers."
Slide 17~3 minWorked compression

Worked example: compress customer features

Walk the code: fit PCA on the 50,000-row standardised frame, cumulative-sum the explained variance, and search for the 90% and 95% marks. The answers: 5 components for 92.2%, 6 for 98.0%.

Read the interpretation: "Only 7 → 5 — these features are not very redundant. Teach the lesson in that: PCA pays off most when you have many correlated features. Then downstream models train faster and overfit less. Here the win is modest — and honesty about small wins is part of the course."

→ "The Kailash surface."
Slide 18~4 minKailash engines

Kailash DimReductionEngine + ModelVisualizer

Walk the code. Wrap the standardised matrix as polars, then DimReductionEngine: variance_analysis gives the 95% count and the elbow; reduce(algorithm="pca", n_components=2) returns the embedding with explained variance and reconstruction error. Wrap the result as a polars frame and scatter it with ModelVisualizer.

Read the teal box — two practical facts: "The engine reports variance and reconstruction error; loadings you read yourself, as on the earlier slide. And ModelVisualizer has no scree helper — plot cumulative_variance with plotly directly. Also note: the engine does not standardise for you — that's your job upstream, as always."

→ "Four mistakes."
Slide 19~3 minMistakes

Four rookie mistakes

Walk the four. One: forgetting to centre/standardise — PCA on raw scales chases the biggest units, not the biggest structure. Two: t-SNE as a feature extractor — forbidden, slide 9. Three: $k$ too small — over-aggressive compression throws away signal you'll blame the downstream model for missing. Four: reporting components without loadings — black-box PCA; name every component you keep.

→ "The decision table."
Slide 20~2 minWhich to use

When to use which

Walk the table. Linear compression or feeding a downstream linear model: PCA. Non-linear compression: kernel PCA at small $N$. Visualise high-dimensional structure: UMAP. Show local neighbourhoods for teaching: t-SNE. Extract features for new points: PCA or UMAP — the only two with a transform().

→ "Recap and bridge."
Slide 21~2 minRecap

What you can now do

Walk the bullets: derive PCA as variance maximisation, connect it to SVD, read scree plots and loadings, choose among PCA/kernel PCA/t-SNE/UMAP, and explain why t-SNE is never a feature extractor. Tease 4.4: "What about the points that don't belong to any cluster? Anomaly detection."

→ End of Lesson 4.3. Ten-minute break.
