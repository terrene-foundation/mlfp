Speaker Notes — Lesson 3.5 Evaluation, Imbalance, and Calibration

Total time: ~75 minutes  ·  16 slides  ·  ~4.5 min per slide average

The decision-system lesson: turn a raw classifier into something a business can act on. The spine is the full credit-scoring pipeline — cost-sensitive booster, calibration on held-out rows, then the cost-derived threshold t* = 0.130. The subtle teaching point is the ordering: class weights shift the raw scores, calibration removes that shift, and only then is the Bayes threshold valid. Get that sequence right and the rest of the lesson falls into place.

Slide 1~1 minTitle

Evaluation, Imbalance, and Calibration

Open: "3.4 gave you a trained booster. Today: turn it into a calibrated decision system — the right metric, the right imbalance technique, and a decision threshold derived from money, not from 0.5."

→ "Back to the four cells."
Slide 2~3 minConfusion matrix

The confusion matrix

Quick refresh from 2.6 — the four cells: TN, FN, FP, TP. "Every metric you'll ever use is a ratio of these four numbers. The skill is knowing which ratio answers the business question."

→ "Three metrics, three different questions."
Slide 3~4 minMetric reads

Each metric reads different cells

Walk the annotated matrix: TN 850, FN 30, FP 50, TP 70. Precision reads the predicted-positive row: 70/120 = 0.58 — "of those I flagged, how many were real?" Recall reads the actual-positive column: 70/100 = 0.70 — "of all real positives, how many did I catch?" Specificity reads the actual-negative column: 850/900 = 0.94 — "of all negatives, how many did I spare?"

"Three different cells, three different questions. When a stakeholder asks 'how good is the model?', the honest answer is: which of these three do you care about?"

→ "The formulas — and the accuracy trap."
Slide 4~3 minFormulas

The formulas

Precision, recall, and F1 — the harmonic mean that punishes lopsidedness. Then the credit-data example on the slide: "12.9% default rate. Predict 'no default' for everyone and you score 87% accuracy. F1 = 0. Accuracy lies — it grades on a curve set by the majority class."

→ "ROC vs PR — the honest curve."
Slide 5~4 minROC vs PR

ROC vs PR — why PR is honest on imbalanced data

Walk the two panels of the same model on the same imbalanced data. Left: ROC — AUC 0.78, looks respectable, the diagonal far below. Right: PR — average precision 0.38 against a base rate of 0.13. "Same model. One curve flatters, one tells the truth."

"Note the PR baseline: it's not 0.5 — it's the base rate, 0.13. AP of 0.38 means roughly triple the baseline. Always quote AP against the base rate, never against 0.5."

→ "Why does ROC get away with it?"
Slide 6~3 minROC's blind spot

Why ROC fails on imbalanced data

Walk the four bullets. FPR = FP/(FP+TN) — when negatives dominate, the denominator is enormous, so moving FP from 10 to 1,000 barely moves the false-positive rate. The ROC curve stays beautiful while precision is miserable. PR has no blind spot: it ignores TN entirely, so every false positive counts.

Read the blue rule of thumb: "Positive class under 20%? Report PR-AUC. Otherwise either works. Credit at 12.9% — PR-AUC it is, exactly as 3.4's training code did with aucpr."

→ "Four ways to fight the imbalance."
Slide 7~4 minImbalance techniques

Four imbalance techniques

Walk the four. SMOTE — synthesise minority points by interpolating between nearest neighbours; fragile in high dimensions or with a noisy minority. Cost-sensitive — class_weight in the loss; no fake data, just a reweighted objective. Focal loss — down-weight easy examples so the gradient focuses on the hard ones; next slide. Threshold tuning — derive the cutoff from the business cost matrix, never leave it at 0.5.

"These compose — but carefully. We'll see the one forbidden combination at the threshold slide."

→ "Focal loss."
Slide 8~3 minFocal loss

Focal loss

Walk the formula: ordinary cross-entropy multiplied by $(1 - p_t)^\gamma$, where $p_t$ is the probability assigned to the true class. $\gamma = 0$ recovers weighted cross-entropy. With $\gamma = 2$: an easy example at $p_t = 0.9$ gets a factor of 0.01 — a hundred times less influence — while a hard example at $p_t = 0.3$ keeps 0.49.

"The gradient ends up dominated by the hard cases — which, on imbalanced data, are the minority class. The model stops earning free accuracy from easy negatives."

→ "The threshold, from money."
Slide 9~5 minCost threshold

Threshold from the cost matrix

Derive it: predict positive when the expected cost of a false negative exceeds the expected cost of a false positive — $t^* = c_{FP}/(c_{FP} + c_{FN})$. With the Exercise 5 costs — missed default S$10,000, false decline S$1,500 — $t^* = 1500/11500 \approx 0.130$.

"Not 0.5! A 13% threshold looks alarming until you do the arithmetic: missing a default costs nearly seven times a false alarm, so you should flag liberally."

Now the red warning — the lesson's subtlest point: "$t^*$ is valid only for calibrated probabilities from an unweighted loss. Class weights already move the decision boundary — applying a cost threshold on top double-counts the cost. Never both. The pipeline slide shows the correct escape: calibrate after the weighted fit, and the threshold becomes legal again."

→ "Which brings us to calibration."
Slide 10~3 minReliability diagram

Calibration — reliability diagram

Walk the diagram: predicted probability on the x-axis, observed frequency on the y-axis, the dashed diagonal is perfect. The red curve is the raw weighted model — sitting below the diagonal where predicted probabilities run too high. The blue curve hugs the diagonal: after calibration.

"A calibrated model means: of the rows it scores 0.3, about 30% really default. Only a calibrated probability is a probability you can threshold with a cost formula — and only then is '$p = 0.13' a statement a bank can act on."

→ "Two ways to calibrate."
Slide 11~4 minCalibration methods

Two calibration methods

Walk the two. Platt scaling: fit a sigmoid $\sigma(Ax + B)$ on the model's scores — one parameter plus a bias, cheap, and exactly right when the miscalibration is sigmoid-shaped. Isotonic regression: a non-decreasing piecewise-constant map — flexible, but hungry for data.

Walk the code: TrainingPipeline.calibrate with method="sigmoid" or "isotonic", fitted on X_cal / y_cal — a held-out 20% of train. Read the footer: "Calibrate on rows the model never trained on. Calibrating on training rows is 3.1's leakage in a new outfit."

→ "Score the calibration."
Slide 12~3 minBrier score

Brier score — proper scoring rule

Walk the formula: mean squared error between predicted probabilities and 0/1 outcomes. Lower is better; zero is a perfect prediction, not just perfect calibration.

Walk the bullets. Brier mixes calibration and discrimination: a calibrated base-rate forecast ($p = 0.13$ everywhere) still scores $0.13 \times 0.87 \approx 0.11$ — so it's not a pure calibration measure; use the reliability diagram or ECE for calibration itself. It's a proper scoring rule — minimised by reporting the true probability, so hedging scores worse than honesty. Report it before and after calibration to show the improvement.

→ "The full pipeline, in order."
Slide 13~5 minFull pipeline

Full pipeline — credit scoring

Walk the four steps slowly — this is the lesson's payload. Step 1: hold back 20% of train for calibration, stratified. Step 2: fit the cost-sensitive booster — scale_pos_weight = (1 - pos_rate) / pos_rate, the negative-to-positive ratio. Step 3: calibrate on the held-out rows with isotonic — this undoes the probability shift the weights introduced. Step 4: now apply the Bayes threshold 0.130 on the calibrated probabilities, and score with Brier.

"Why is this not the double-counting the warning slide forbade? Because recalibration removes the weight's shift: the weights buy you a better ranking, calibration restores honest probabilities, and the cost threshold then applies to numbers that mean what they say. Sequence matters: weight → calibrate → threshold."

→ "One more squeeze: combine models."
Slide 14~4 minStacking and blending

Stacking and blending

Walk the two bullets. Blending: average the base models' probabilities — a soft vote, optionally weighted. Stacking: a meta-model learns how to combine the base models from their out-of-fold predictions — never in-sample, or the meta-model learns to trust whichever base model overfit hardest.

Walk the code: EnsembleEngine with stack(base, train_df, target=..., fold=5) and blend(..., method="soft"). The engine carves its own internal holdout for metrics — pass only training rows; the test set is still used once, at the very end. Read the footer: "Gains come from diverse base models. Three copies of one booster add little — a booster, a logistic regression, and a forest disagree in useful ways."

→ "Three traps."
Slide 15~2 minTraps

Three traps

Walk the three red boxes. One: reporting accuracy on imbalanced data — the 87% illusion from slide 4. Two: SMOTE-ing the test set — SMOTE is training-only; synthetic neighbours of real test rows leak structure into evaluation. Three: leaving the threshold at 0.5 — you just derived 0.130 from money; 0.5 is never the answer when the costs are asymmetric.

→ "Recap and bridge."
Slide 16~2 minRecap

What you can now do

Walk the bullets: read every cell of the confusion matrix, pick ROC vs PR, apply the four imbalance techniques, derive the cost-optimal threshold, calibrate on held-out rows, and combine models with EnsembleEngine. Tease 3.6: "The model is accurate and calibrated. But should it be deployed? SHAP, LIME, fairness metrics, and the impossibility theorem."

→ End of Lesson 3.5. Ten-minute break.
