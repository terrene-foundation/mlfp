Lesson 4.6 NLP — From Text to Topics

Text is the most common unstructured data type in every business: product reviews, support tickets, news articles, chat logs, legal contracts. Today you will turn raw text into numbers with TF-IDF and BM25, learn how LDA generates topics probabilistically, and watch BERTopic combine transformer embeddings with clustering. By the end you will be able to extract topics from a real news corpus, score them with NPMI coherence, and explain what the numbers in a topic model actually mean.

Why This Matters

Every week your company generates thousands of text artefacts — tickets, emails, reviews, reports. Nobody has time to read them all. Topic modelling discovers the themes automatically: clusters of words that appear together in documents, each corresponding to a theme like "billing complaints", "feature requests", or "Q4 legal updates". The themes become features (how much of this document is about billing?) and the features drive downstream decisions (auto-route to the right team).

Core Concepts

TF-IDF derivation

The first step in any text pipeline is to turn documents into vectors. The simplest approach — bag of words — counts how many times each term appears in each document. This overweights common words (the, and, to). TF-IDF (term frequency times inverse document frequency) fixes this by downweighting terms that appear in many documents.

$$ \text{tf}(t, d) \;=\; \frac{\text{count}(t \text{ in } d)}{\text{len}(d)} $$ $$ \text{idf}(t) \;=\; \log \frac{N}{\text{df}(t)} $$ $$ \text{tfidf}(t, d) \;=\; \text{tf}(t, d) \cdot \text{idf}(t) $$

where $N$ is the number of documents and $\text{df}(t)$ is the number of documents containing term $t$. The intuition: a word that appears in every document (the) has $\text{idf} = \log(N/N) = 0$, contributing nothing. A word that appears in one document has $\text{idf} = \log N$, contributing a lot. TF-IDF vectors are the workhorse of classical NLP — sparse, interpretable, fast to compute.

Libraries use variants of this textbook form. scikit-learn's TfidfVectorizer by default uses raw counts for tf, a smoothed idf $\ln\frac{1 + N}{1 + \text{df}(t)} + 1$ (as if one extra document contained every word, so no idf is zero), and then scales each document vector to unit length. The ranking intuition is the same; the numbers are not, so do not expect a hand-rolled TF-IDF to match the library unless you copy its settings. Exercise 6.1 builds both.

BM25 — TF-IDF's smarter cousin

TF-IDF has two weaknesses: it is linear in term frequency (repeating a word 10 times contributes 10×, even though the 10th repetition tells you almost nothing new) and it does not account for document length (a term in a 10-word tweet should count more than the same term in a 10,000-word report). BM25 fixes both:

$$ \text{BM25}(t, d) \;=\; \text{idf}(t) \cdot \frac{(k_1 + 1)\,\text{tf}(t,d)}{\text{tf}(t,d) + k_1 \left(1 - b + b\,\frac{|d|}{\text{avg}|d|}\right)} $$

where $k_1 \approx 1.2$ and $b \approx 0.75$ are the standard parameters. The term-frequency contribution saturates (no matter how many times a word appears, it contributes at most $k_1 + 1$), and document length is normalised against the corpus average. BM25 is the default ranking function in Apache Lucene and in search engines built on it, such as Elasticsearch, and a standard baseline in information retrieval.

Word embeddings — a preview

TF-IDF represents each term as a one-hot dimension. "Bank" (financial) and "bank" (river) map to the same dimension. "Happy" and "joyful" map to different dimensions. This is the semantic limitation of sparse representations.

Word2Vec, GloVe, and FastText solve this by learning dense vectors where similar words have similar coordinates. The principle: "words that appear in similar contexts have similar meanings" (the distributional hypothesis). Skip-gram Word2Vec trains a small neural network to predict the context of a target word, and the hidden-layer weights become the embeddings.

🔵 Theory — preview of the pivot

How does Word2Vec learn these vectors? It uses gradient descent on a classification loss. The hidden layer activations are the word embeddings — features learned by optimisation. We will meet this idea explicitly in Lesson 4.8: hidden layers are automated feature engineering with error feedback.

Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA)

LDA is a generative model for text. Its story:

  1. For each topic $k$, draw a word distribution $\boldsymbol{\phi}_k$ from a Dirichlet prior.
  2. For each document $d$, draw a topic distribution $\boldsymbol{\theta}_d$ from another Dirichlet.
  3. For each word position in document $d$: first draw a topic $z$ from $\boldsymbol{\theta}_d$, then draw a word from $\boldsymbol{\phi}_z$.

Inference reverses the story: given the words, find the most likely topic distributions. This is usually done with variational inference or collapsed Gibbs sampling. Each fitted document is represented as a distribution over topics — a soft assignment, just like GMM's responsibilities.

Because LDA models integer word counts, fit it on a count matrix (CountVectorizer), not on TF-IDF weights — TF-IDF values are not counts and violate the model's likelihood.

NMF — the matrix factorisation view

Non-negative Matrix Factorisation (NMF) decomposes the TF-IDF matrix into $W H$ where both $W$ and $H$ have non-negative entries. $W$ rows become topic weights for each document; $H$ rows become word weights for each topic. NMF is faster than LDA, gives sparser and often more interpretable topics, and is our go-to when the corpus is medium-sized and the task is exploratory.

BERTopic

BERTopic is the 2020s upgrade to topic modelling. It replaces TF-IDF with transformer embeddings (typically sentence-transformers), reduces them with UMAP, clusters with HDBSCAN, then computes a class-based TF-IDF (c-TF-IDF) to extract representative words for each cluster. The result: topics that capture semantic relationships (not just co-occurrence) and automatically determine their own number. 

from bertopic import BERTopic
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import CountVectorizer
from umap import UMAP
from shared.mlfp04.ex_6 import NEWS_STOP_WORDS, corpus_as_lists, load_corpus, topic_embedding_model

documents, categories = corpus_as_lists(load_corpus())     # AG News leads (worked example)

# The sentence-embedding model name comes from TOPIC_EMBED_MODEL in .env (never hardcoded)
topic_model = BERTopic(
    embedding_model=topic_embedding_model(),
    umap_model=UMAP(n_neighbors=15, n_components=5, min_dist=0.0, metric="cosine", random_state=42),
    vectorizer_model=CountVectorizer(stop_words=NEWS_STOP_WORDS, min_df=2),
    min_topic_size=15,
    nr_topics="auto",
)
bert_topics, _ = topic_model.fit_transform(documents)
n_topics = len(set(bert_topics) - {-1})
print(f"BERTopic: {n_topics} topics, {sum(t == -1 for t in bert_topics):,} outlier documents")

UMAP reduces the embeddings to about five dimensions for clustering (two dimensions are used only for plotting), and documents that fit no dense HDBSCAN cluster go to the outlier topic $-1$. With TOPIC_EMBED_MODEL=sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2, one run on the worked-example corpus produced 40 topics and about 25% outlier documents; the exact topics depend on the embedding model you configure.

Coherence metrics — how good is a topic?

Topic model quality is hard to evaluate objectively. The standard approach is coherence — how often do the top words of a topic co-occur in a reference corpus? A common variant is NPMI (normalised pointwise mutual information):

$$ \text{NPMI}(w_i, w_j) \;=\; \frac{\log\bigl(P(w_i, w_j) / (P(w_i)\,P(w_j))\bigr)}{-\log P(w_i, w_j)} $$

NPMI lives in $[-1, 1]$: $-1$ when the two words never appear in the same document, 0 when they co-occur exactly as often as chance predicts, $+1$ when they always co-occur. Average NPMI over all pairs of a topic's top 10 words, then across topics — higher is better. The probabilities are document frequencies in a reference corpus (here the corpus itself), so compare models on the same setup rather than against published "typical" values. You do not need an extra library: the course implementation is shared.mlfp04.ex_6.compute_npmi.

UMass coherence (Mimno et al., 2011) is the other common metric. For a topic's top words ordered by frequency it sums, over pairs with $i > j$,

$$ C_{\text{UMass}} \;=\; \sum_{i>j}\ \log\frac{D(w_i, w_j) + 1}{D(w_j)} $$

where $D(w)$ is the number of documents containing $w$ and $D(w_i, w_j)$ the number containing both; the $+1$ avoids $\log 0$. It is computed on the training corpus, is at most about 0, and values closer to 0 are better. UMass is cheap but agrees less well with human judgements than NPMI.

The Kailash Engine: DimReductionEngine (NMF)

⚙ Kailash Engine

There is no Kailash "topic modelling" engine, and ModelVisualizer has no topic-heatmap helper. Topic extraction with NMF is dimensionality reduction of the document–term matrix, so it runs through the same DimReductionEngine you met in Lesson 4.3, with algorithm="nmf". The engine takes a polars frame (one column per vocabulary term), checks that the input is non-negative, and returns the document–topic weights $W$ in transformed. It does not return the topic–word matrix $H$; for topic words use scikit-learn's NMF directly (worked example) or rank terms by $W^\top X$. For BERTopic, use BERTopic's own plots (visualize_barchart, visualize_heatmap). The module assessment's topic task uses this engine. 

import numpy as np
import polars as pl
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer
from kailash_ml.engines.dim_reduction import DimReductionEngine
from shared.mlfp04.ex_6 import NEWS_STOP_WORDS, corpus_as_lists, load_corpus

documents, categories = corpus_as_lists(load_corpus())
tfidf = TfidfVectorizer(max_features=2000, stop_words=NEWS_STOP_WORDS, min_df=3, max_df=0.95)
X_tfidf = tfidf.fit_transform(documents).toarray()          # dense: the engine takes a frame
matrix_df = pl.from_numpy(X_tfidf, schema=[f"t{i}" for i in range(X_tfidf.shape[1])])

nmf_res = DimReductionEngine().reduce(matrix_df, algorithm="nmf", n_components=4, seed=42)
W = np.asarray(nmf_res.transformed)                         # documents x topics
print(f"documents per topic: {np.bincount(W.argmax(axis=1)).tolist()}")

vocab = tfidf.get_feature_names_out()
for k, weights in enumerate(W.T @ X_tfidf):                 # term weight per topic
    print(f"topic {k}: {', '.join(vocab[np.argsort(weights)[::-1][:6]])}")

Worked Example: Topics in a Public News Corpus

The corpus is AG News (mlfp05/ag_news.parquet): the title and lead sentence of 5,000 English news stories from 2004, each labelled by humans with one of four sections — world, sports, business and sci/tech. load_corpus() cleans encoding artefacts and removes duplicate stories, leaving 4,967 documents. The section labels are never used to fit a topic model, only afterwards to see what the topics line up with. 

from sklearn.decomposition import NMF, LatentDirichletAllocation
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import CountVectorizer
from sklearn.metrics import normalized_mutual_info_score
from shared.mlfp04.ex_6 import compute_npmi

N_TOPICS = 8

# NMF works on TF-IDF weights
tfidf_vec = TfidfVectorizer(max_features=3000, stop_words=NEWS_STOP_WORDS, min_df=3, max_df=0.95)
tfidf_matrix = tfidf_vec.fit_transform(documents)
tfidf_vocab = tfidf_vec.get_feature_names_out()
nmf = NMF(n_components=N_TOPICS, init="nndsvd", max_iter=400, random_state=42)
W_nmf = nmf.fit_transform(tfidf_matrix)       # document-topic matrix
nmf_topics = [[tfidf_vocab[j] for j in comp.argsort()[::-1][:10]] for comp in nmf.components_]

# LDA is a model of word COUNTS, so it gets a count matrix — never TF-IDF
count_vec = CountVectorizer(max_features=3000, stop_words=NEWS_STOP_WORDS, min_df=3, max_df=0.95)
count_matrix = count_vec.fit_transform(documents)
count_vocab = count_vec.get_feature_names_out()
lda = LatentDirichletAllocation(n_components=N_TOPICS, random_state=42)
theta_lda = lda.fit_transform(count_matrix)   # document-topic proportions
lda_topics = [[count_vocab[j] for j in comp.argsort()[::-1][:10]] for comp in lda.components_]

for name, topics, vec, doc_topic in [("NMF", nmf_topics, tfidf_vec, W_nmf),
                                     ("LDA", lda_topics, count_vec, theta_lda)]:
    npmi = compute_npmi(documents, topics, analyzer=vec.build_analyzer())
    nmi = normalized_mutual_info_score(categories, doc_topic.argmax(axis=1))
    print(f"\n{name}: mean NPMI = {np.mean(npmi):+.3f}   NMI with sections = {nmi:.3f}")
    for k, words in enumerate(topics):
        print(f"  topic {k}: {', '.join(words[:8])}")

The NMF topics are sharp and specific — stories, not sections: an initial public offering by Google (google, ipo, public, offering), oil prices (oil, prices, crude, barrel), the Athens Olympics (athens, gold, olympic, phelps, medal), the fighting in Najaf, a Windows XP security update, quarterly earnings and Israeli politics, plus a leftover mixed topic. On short news leads, word co-occurrence is dominated by the big stories of the week. Mean NPMI is about +0.41. The LDA topics are broader and noisier, several blending unrelated stories, and mean NPMI is about −0.02 because several top-word pairs never appear in the same document. Neither model recovers the four human sections well (NMI with the sections about 0.28 for NMF and 0.23 for LDA): topic models find whatever co-occurrence structure is strongest, which need not be the categorisation a human editor would use.

To choose $K$ for LDA, use held-out perplexity: fit on a training split of the documents and call lda.perplexity() on the held-out split (perplexity on the training documents always improves as $K$ grows). Exercise 6.3 runs that sweep.

Common Mistakes

  1. Skipping stopword removal. Without stopwords removed, your top words will be "the", "and", "to", "of". Always use a stopword list for the target language.
  2. Too few or too many topics. LDA and NMF both require you to set $K$. Start with 10 – 20 and tune with coherence.
  3. Judging topic quality by perplexity. Held-out perplexity measures how well LDA predicts unseen words — useful for choosing $K$ — but it can disagree with human judgements of how interpretable the topics are. Report a coherence score (NPMI) as well, and never quote training perplexity as if it were held-out.
  4. Fitting LDA on TF-IDF. LDA is a model of word counts; give it a CountVectorizer matrix. TF-IDF is for NMF.
  5. Running BERTopic on tiny datasets. BERTopic needs a few hundred documents minimum. For smaller corpora, fall back to TF-IDF + NMF.
