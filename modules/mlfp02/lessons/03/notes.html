Speaker Notes — Lesson 2.3 Bootstrapping and Hypothesis Testing

Total time: ~45 minutes  ·  16 slides  ·  ~3 min per slide average

Two big ideas today: resampling (get a sampling distribution with no formula) and testing (turn a difference into a decision). The bootstrap feels like cheating the first time students see it — lean into that. The p-value definition is where careers are made or broken; spend the time on the "what it is NOT" warning.

Slide 1~1 minTitle

Bootstrapping and Hypothesis Testing

Open with the bridge from 2.2: "Yesterday we estimated parameters and built intervals with formulas — CLT, $t$-distribution. Today: what do you do when there is no formula? And how do you turn an estimate into a decision?" Agenda: resampling, p-values, power, multiple testing, permutation tests.

→ "The bootstrap: sampling without extra samples."
Slide 2~3 minBootstrap idea

The Bootstrap: sampling without extra samples

Frame the problem crisply: "You have one sample of $n$ observations. You want the sampling distribution of some statistic — say the median. The CLT doesn't cover the median. And you cannot go get more data." Then the audacious solution: "Treat your sample as if it were the population. Resample with replacement. Compute the statistic. Repeat thousands of times."

Read the amber box aloud: "It feels illegal. It is mathematically justified. And it works for statistics — median, correlation, regression coefficients — where no clean formula exists."

For experts: the justification is that the empirical distribution is the non-parametric MLE of the population; the bootstrap is plug-in estimation of the sampling distribution. It fails for extremes (max/min) and heavy dependence.
→ "Here's the loop, drawn out."
Slide 3~2 minResampling picture

The resampling loop

Walk the diagram left to right. The original sample ($n = 500$) fans out into resamples — each the same size, drawn with replacement, so some points repeat and some drop out. Each resample yields one statistic: $\hat\theta_1^* = 26.7$, $\hat\theta_2^* = 26.4$, …, $\hat\theta_B^* = 26.9$. Collect all $B$ of them and you have the bootstrap distribution on the right.

"That distribution is our estimate of 'what would happen if we re-ran the world thousands of times' — built from one sample."

→ "The simplest interval: read off percentiles."
Slide 4~4 minPercentile CI

Percentile CI (the simple version)

Show the formula: the 95% CI is just the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap replicates. Then walk the code. We sample 200 HDB resale prices from the parquet (seed 42), draw $B = 5000$ resamples with rng.choice(..., replace=True), take the median of each, and read off the percentiles.

"No formulas, no CLT, no $t$-tables. Five thousand tiny experiments and two percentiles."

If beginners ask why with replacement: "Without replacement every resample would be the original sample reordered — same median every time. Replacement is what creates the variation that mimics drawing a new sample from the population."
→ "When is the simple version not enough?"
Slide 5~3 minBCa idea

BCa: when percentile is biased

"The percentile method assumes the bootstrap distribution is symmetric and centred on $\hat\theta$. For skewed statistics — the median of skewed data, ratios — that fails." BCa corrects two things: the bias correction $\hat z_0$ (is the median of the bootstrap distribution equal to $\hat\theta$?) and the acceleration $\hat a$ (does the skewness change with $\theta$? — estimated by jackknife).

Don't derive the formula. Say: "It warps the percentile levels so the interval is asymmetric in the right direction."

→ "One line in scipy."
Slide 6~2 minBCa code

BCa in scipy

Walk the code: scipy.stats.bootstrap takes the data as a tuple, the statistic, and method="BCa". One line replaces the entire manual loop.

Give the rule of thumb from the slide: use "BCa" for skewed statistics — median, trimmed mean, ratio. Use "percentile" when the bootstrap distribution is approximately symmetric.

→ "From intervals to decisions."
Slide 7~4 minTesting picture

Hypothesis testing in one picture

Walk the figure. The curve is the null distribution — what the test statistic looks like if $H_0$ is true. The red line is the observed statistic $T_{\text{obs}}$. The p-value is the shaded tail: the probability, under the null, of data at least as extreme as what we saw.

State the formula precisely: $p = P(T \ge T_{\text{obs}} \mid H_0)$ — one-tailed as drawn. Two-tailed is $P(|T| \ge |T_{\text{obs}}| \mid H_0)$: "data at least as extreme in either direction." Mention that one- vs two-tailed is a design decision you commit to before looking at the data.

→ "Which statistic? The $t$."
Slide 8~3 mint-statistic

The $t$-statistic and its distribution

Walk the formula: centre the sample mean at the null value $\mu_0$, divide by the standard error, and under $H_0$ the ratio follows a $t_{n-1}$ distribution. "Large $T$ → the observed mean is far from the null → small p-value → reject."

Now the warning box — read it verbatim, then unpack: "The p-value is NOT $P(H_0 \mid \text{data})$. It's the probability of extreme data given $H_0$ — the exact wrong direction, just like sensitivity vs PPV in 2.1. To get $P(H_0 \mid \text{data})$ you need a prior — that's Bayes, and we did it yesterday."

If beginners mix this up: "A small p-value says 'this data would be weird if nothing were going on'. It does not say 'there's a 3% chance nothing is going on'."
→ "Two ways to be wrong."
Slide 9~3 minErrors and power

Type I / Type II / Power

Walk the 2×2 grid. Rows are reality ($H_0$ true / false), columns are your decision (fail to reject / reject). Top-left: correct, probability $1-\alpha$. Top-right: Type I error — false alarm, probability $\alpha$. Bottom-left: Type II error — missed detection, probability $\beta$. Bottom-right: power, $1-\beta$ — catching a real effect.

"You choose $\alpha$ before the experiment — 0.05 by convention. $\beta$ is determined by your sample size, variance, and the true effect size. Which brings us to power analysis."

→ "How big a sample do you need?"
Slide 10~3 minPower and MDE

Power and MDE

Walk the two formulas for a two-sample $z$-test. Power is $\Phi(\delta\sqrt{n/2}/\sigma - z_{1-\alpha/2})$ — it grows with effect size and $n$, shrinks with variance. Invert it to get the minimum detectable effect: $\delta_{\text{MDE}} = (z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta}) \cdot \sigma\sqrt{2/n}$.

Read the footer line and make it a chant: "More data → smaller MDE. More variance → larger MDE. Stricter $\alpha$ or a higher power target → larger MDE. This is why experiment design starts with 'what's the smallest effect worth detecting?'"

→ "See it in the picture."
Slide 11~2 minPower picture

Power = probability of detecting a real effect

Walk the two curves. Grey is the null, blue is the alternative — the world where the effect is real. The dashed line is the critical value. Red sliver to its right under the null: $\alpha$. Amber region to its left under the alternative: $\beta$ — real effects you miss. Green region to its right under the alternative: power.

"Slide the curves apart — bigger effect — and power grows. Shrink the spread — more data — and both errors shrink. This picture is every power calculation you'll ever run."

→ "Now the trap: running many tests."
Slide 12~3 minBonferroni

Multiple testing: Bonferroni

Set up the trap: "Run 20 tests at $\alpha = 0.05$ on pure random data. You expect one false positive. Test enough metrics and something always looks significant."

Bonferroni divides: $\alpha_{\text{Bonf}} = \alpha/m$. For 20 tests, each is judged at 0.0025. It controls the family-wise error rate — the probability of any false positive. Read the footer: very conservative — right for high-stakes decisions, wasteful for exploration.

→ "A less brutal correction."
Slide 13~3 minBH-FDR

Multiple testing: BH-FDR

Walk the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure: sort the p-values, find the largest rank $k$ where $p_{(k)} \le \frac{k}{m}\alpha$, reject everything up to $k$. It controls the false discovery rate — the expected proportion of false positives among the rejects, not among all tests.

Walk the code: false_discovery_control(pvals, method="bh") returns BH-adjusted p-values; compare to 0.05. "Bonferroni asks 'am I sure none of these is wrong?' BH asks 'what fraction of my discoveries are junk?' — usually the question you actually care about."

→ "One more distribution-free tool."
Slide 14~4 minPermutation test

Permutation test

Walk the four steps: compute the observed statistic, pool the two groups, re-split randomly $B$ times recomputing the statistic, and the p-value is the fraction of shuffles at least as extreme as the observed. "The shuffles are the null distribution — built by destroying the very thing you're testing (the group labels)."

Walk the code, then point at the +1 in numerator and denominator: "The $+1$ counts the observed split itself, so $p$ is never exactly zero. With $B = 5000$ shuffles the smallest attainable p-value is $1/5001$."

For experts: the permutation test is exact under exchangeability — under $H_0$ the labels are arbitrary. It's the gold standard when you doubt the $t$-test's assumptions.
→ "Put it together on three metrics."
Slide 15~3 minWorked A/B

Worked: three-metric A/B test

Walk the code. You have three p-values from the same experiment: conversion, average order value, revenue per visitor. Bonferroni multiplies each by $m=3$ (capped at 1). BH adjusts by rank. Compare the boolean masks side by side.

Read the footer: "BH-FDR typically rejects more than Bonferroni — more power, at the cost of a weaker guarantee. In 2.4 you'll run this experiment for real and pick the primary metric before launching — that's the cleanest multiple-testing defence of all."

→ "Recap."
Slide 16~2 minRecap
