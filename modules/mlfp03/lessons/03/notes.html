Speaker Notes — Lesson 3.3 The Complete Supervised Model Zoo

Total time: ~90 minutes  ·  17 slides  ·  ~5 min per slide average

Five model families in one sitting — the longest lesson of M3. Keep the pace brisk through the theory and spend the saved minutes on the two hands-on moments: the Gini calculation by hand, and the leaderboard comparison. The leaderboard's honest punchline (simple Naive Bayes edges out the forest on AUC) is the lesson's real payload: no family wins everywhere.

Slide 1~1 minTitle

The Complete Supervised Model Zoo

Open: "3.2 taught you how hard to fit. Today: what to fit. Five families cover most non-deep supervised problems — SVM, KNN, Naive Bayes, decision trees, and random forests. By the end you'll train all five on churn data and compare them fairly."

→ "The roster."
Slide 2~3 minFive families

Five families cover most non-deep supervised problems

Walk the roster with one geometry each. SVM: a hyperplane with maximum margin. KNN: a vote of the nearest training points. Naive Bayes: Bayes' theorem with conditional independence. Decision trees: recursive axis-aligned splits. Random forests: a bagged ensemble of decorrelated trees.

Read the italic line: "Every family has a distinct geometry. The data tells you which geometry fits. There is no universally best model — keep that in mind for the leaderboard at the end."

→ "SVM first."
Slide 3~4 minSVM margin

SVM — maximum margin

Walk the figure. Two classes, and many lines could separate them. SVM picks the one with the fattest buffer — the margin, $2/\|w\|$. The dashed lines touch the closest points of each class; those points — circled in red — are the support vectors. "Only those few points determine the boundary. Move any other point and nothing changes."

→ "As an optimisation problem."
Slide 4~4 minSVM QP

SVM — the quadratic program

Walk the primal: minimise $\frac{1}{2}\|w\|^2$ (maximise the margin) plus $C\sum\xi_i$ (penalise violations), subject to each point being on the correct side with slack $\xi_i$.

Then the bullets — connect $C$ to 3.2: "Small $C$ tolerates violations and gives a smooth boundary — high bias. Large $C$ separates everything — high variance. $C$ is the SVM's regularisation dial. And only support vectors have nonzero dual coefficients — the solution is sparse."

→ "What if no line separates them?"
Slide 5~4 minKernel trick

The kernel trick

"Map the data into a higher-dimensional space where a hyperplane can separate it. The trick: the dual only ever needs inner products, so we compute $K(x, x') = \langle\phi(x), \phi(x')\rangle$ directly — and never construct $\phi$ at all."

Walk the table. Linear: $x^\top x'$ — text and high-dimensional sparse data. Polynomial: $(x^\top x' + c)^d$ — degree-$d$ interactions. RBF: $\exp(-\gamma\|x - x'\|^2)$ — smooth non-linear boundaries, the default reach.

→ "KNN — and the curse that kills it."
Slide 6~4 minKNN curse

KNN — the curse of dimensionality

KNN needs no training: store the data, and at prediction time take a vote of the $k$ nearest points. Beautiful — until you see the curve. As dimension $d$ grows, the ratio of farthest to nearest distance collapses toward 1: every point becomes equidistant, and "nearest" stops meaning anything.

Read the labels: "At $d = 2$, neighbours are useful. At $d = 500$, meaningless. This is distance concentration — the geometric reason KNN dies in high dimensions, and one more argument for 3.1's feature selection."

→ "The fastest baseline in the zoo."
Slide 7~4 minNaive Bayes

Naive Bayes — fast baseline

Walk the formula: predict the class that maximises the prior times the product of per-feature likelihoods. "The independence assumption is obviously wrong — words in a sentence are not independent. But the model only needs to rank classes correctly, and rank-correct is much easier than probability-correct."

Walk the three variants: GaussianNB for continuous features, MultinomialNB for counts (text bag-of-words — its natural home), BernoulliNB for binary features. "Fit in milliseconds, hard to beat on text. Always your first baseline."

→ "Trees."
Slide 8~4 minSplitting criteria

Decision trees — splitting criteria

Walk the two impurity measures: Gini, $1 - \sum p_k^2$, and entropy, $-\sum p_k \log_2 p_k$. Both are zero for a pure node and maximal for a 50/50 mix.

Then information gain in the blue box: parent impurity minus the weighted child impurities. "The algorithm is greedily simple: try every feature, every threshold, pick the split with the largest gain. Recurse until a stopping rule fires. Greedy means locally optimal — trees don't look ahead."

→ "What those splits look like in space."
Slide 9~4 minAxis-aligned cuts

Trees partition feature space with axis-aligned cuts

Walk the left panel: the first split is a horizontal cut at bp = 150; the second is a vertical cut at age = 55, applied only inside the high-bp region. Three rectangles result — R3 (low bp, all survive), R2 (old, high bp, all mortality), R1 (young, high bp, survive). The right panel is the same thing as a tree.

Read the amber insight box: "Each split is a single axis-aligned cut. Trees cannot learn diagonal boundaries — SVMs can. A diagonal boundary takes a tree a staircase of many cuts to approximate."

→ "Compute one split by hand."
Slide 10~5 minGini by hand

Decision tree by hand — Gini

Do this one slowly at the board — it's the only hand calculation of the lesson. Parent: 10 churn, 6 retain, $n = 16$. Gini = $1 - 0.625^2 - 0.375^2 = 0.469$. The candidate split gives {8 churn, 1 retain} and {2 churn, 5 retain}: Gini 0.198 and 0.408. Weighted by size: $(9/16)(0.198) + (7/16)(0.408) = 0.290$. Decrease: $0.469 - 0.290 = 0.179$.

"The tree computes this number for every candidate split and takes the biggest. Entropy gives the same recipe with $H$ in place of $G$ — in practice they rarely disagree."

If students ask why not accuracy: "Impurity rewards confident nodes. A split into 90/10 and 10/90 is far better than two 50/50 nodes even if both classify the majority class the same way."
→ "One tree overfits. A forest doesn't."
Slide 11~5 minRandom forest

Random forest — why the trees are decorrelated

Walk the four panels. (a) Bootstrap rows: each tree trains on a sample drawn with replacement — rows repeat, rows drop out. (b) Random feature subsets: at each split, only $m = \sqrt{d}$ columns are candidates. (c) The two sources of randomness produce trees with genuinely different structures. (d) Majority vote aggregates.

Read the key insight box: "Different rows + different columns at each tree = trees make independent errors = averaging reduces variance. A single tree is a low-bias, high-variance dartboard. The forest keeps the bias and shrinks the spread — that's 3.2's decomposition doing work."

→ "And a free validation set falls out."
Slide 12~3 minOOB

OOB = 1/e — free validation

Walk the limit: $(1 - 1/n)^n \to 1/e \approx 0.368$. "Each tree's bootstrap sample leaves about 36.8% of rows out. For any given row, roughly a third of the trees never saw it — vote only those trees and you have an unbiased generalisation estimate with zero extra compute."

"This is why the code sets oob_score=True — free cross-validation, built into the training you were doing anyway."

→ "All five families, one fair fight."
Slide 13~3 minFair comparison

Five models, one dataset, same CV

Walk the code: five models in a dict, each evaluated with cross_validate under the same stratified 5-fold split, scoring accuracy, F1, and AUC. "Same data, same folds, same metrics. Anything less and the comparison is astrology."

→ "The results."
Slide 14~5 minLeaderboard

Leaderboard on e-commerce churn (5-fold CV)

Read the table row by row. First the baseline — always predict "churn": accuracy 0.741, F1 0.851, AUC 0.500. "Churners are the majority here, so the trivial classifier already looks decent on accuracy and F1. The baseline row is what every other number must beat."

Now the models: SVM 0.790/0.871/0.747, KNN 0.764/0.859/0.739, GaussianNB 0.787/0.863/0.791, tree 0.780/0.863/0.769, forest 0.784/0.866/0.779. Read the footer with emphasis: "4,000 training rows, the recency column removed because it defines the label. And the headline: simple Naive Bayes edges out the forest on AUC. No family wins everywhere — the geometry of this problem suits NB's independence story. This is why you always run the table, never skip to your favourite."

For experts: the F1 column barely separates any model from the baseline — a majority-positive class inflates F1 exactly as it inflates accuracy. AUC is the discriminating column here; PR-AUC would be sharper still (3.5's topic).
→ "So how do you pick?"
Slide 15~3 minRight tool

Pick the right tool

Walk the table. SVM: small, clean, high-dimensional data; text with a linear kernel. KNN: small data, low dimensions, interpretable boundaries. Naive Bayes: text baseline, spam, sentiment, fast prototypes. Decision tree: when you need a human-readable rule set. Random forest: most tabular problems — the default when unsure.

"Default when unsure' is doing a lot of work in that last row. The forest is rarely wrong and rarely best — but the leaderboard just showed you why 'rarely best' matters."

→ "Three traps."
Slide 16~3 minTraps

Three traps

Walk the three red boxes. One: unscaled features with SVM or KNN — dollars dominate years; both methods are distance-based, so standardise first. Two: KNN with very large $k$ — the vote collapses to the base rate; tune $k$ by CV. Three: reporting accuracy on imbalanced data — meaningless, as the baseline row just proved; use F1/AUC, and 3.5 goes deeper.

→ "Recap and bridge."
Slide 17~2 minRecap

What you can now do

Walk the bullets: the SVM primal-to-dual and the kernel trick, distance concentration, Gini and information gain by hand, OOB = 1/e, and training five families for a fair comparison. Tease 3.4: "Random forests average independent trees. Gradient boosting does the opposite — each tree corrects the last one's mistakes. XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost, and a second-order Taylor derivation."

→ End of Lesson 3.3. Ten-minute break.
