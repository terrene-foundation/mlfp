Speaker Notes — Lesson 3.1 Feature Engineering, ML Pipeline, and Feature Selection

Total time: ~75 minutes  ·  16 slides  ·  ~4.5 min per slide average

The first lesson of M3 sets the tone for the whole module: features beat algorithms, and leakage is the enemy. The running case is an ICU cohort — 5,000 patients, 8,000 admissions — with the target long_stay (length of stay above the cohort median), predicted 24 hours after admission. Two things to land hard: the point-in-time discipline, and the honest window-audit finding that on this extract almost no vitals fall inside the first 24 hours.

Slide 1~1 minTitle

Feature Engineering, ML Pipeline, and Feature Selection

Welcome the class to M3: "M2 gave you statistics. M3 turns it into machine learning — and the first lesson is the least glamorous and most valuable: features." Set the 75-minute expectation and the outcome: engineer and select features for ICU long-stay prediction.

→ "The claim that orders everything."
Slide 2~4 minData > models

Data > Models > Hyperparameters

Read the quote with weight: "XGBoost on the wrong features loses to logistic regression on the right ones — every time." Then the senior-engineer line: the skill is not knowing more algorithms; it is turning a real problem into a feature matrix.

Read the amber box to introduce the case: "Today's dataset is an ICU cohort — 5,000 patients, 8,000 admissions. The target is long_stay: length of stay above the cohort median. The prediction is made 24 hours after admission — that deadline is what makes the feature-engineering discipline real."

→ "Where today sits in the pipeline."
Slide 3~4 minPipeline

The seven-stage ML pipeline

Walk the seven stages left to right. Ingest (5,000 × 12, messy — NaNs, question marks). Clean (4,312 rows — rows dropped). Engineer (columns added: shock index, hr_std, spo2_min, trends). Select (down to 5 columns). Train (XGBoost, 400 trees, depth 6). Eval (AUC 0.89, F1 0.74, Brier 0.11). Deploy (POST /predict, 12 ms). Note the numbers are marked illustrative.

Point at the dimension bar: "Rows shrink through cleaning; columns grow through engineering, then shrink through selection. Today is stages 3 and 4 — the two stages where judgment lives. The rest are machinery M3 will fill in."

→ "Why ML lets us do what statistics forbade."
Slide 4~3 minStats vs ML

Statistics vs ML

Draw the contrast from the slide: statistics explains the past — it cares about the coefficients. Machine learning predicts the future — it cares about expected loss on fresh draws from the same distribution. Walk the minimisation formula.

"The shift in objective justifies everything today: features can be ugly, redundant, non-interpretable — as long as they reduce out-of-sample loss. Nobody reads the coefficient on hr_std; everyone reads the AUC."

→ "The four families every feature belongs to."
Slide 5~4 minFeature families

Four feature families

Walk the table with one sentence each. Aggregation — mean heart rate over the first 24h — gives a fixed shape from an irregular time-series. Lag/temporal — rainfall 1 and 7 days ago — exposes autoregressive structure. Interaction — age × systolic BP — puts non-linear effects into linear models. Domain-derived — shock index, price per sqm — encodes expert knowledge the model cannot discover on its own.

"When you're stuck, run down this list. Ninety percent of useful features are one of these four."

→ "And the rule that decides which features are legal."
Slide 6~4 minPoint-in-time

Point-in-time correctness

Walk the timeline: admission on the left, the 24-hour prediction point in the middle, discharge on the right. Green box — allowed: age, sex, comorbidities, first-24h vitals and labs. Red box — leaky: los_days and discharge_time (they are the label's source), plus vitals, meds, and labs recorded after hour 24.

Read the caption as the test: "A feature is legal only if its value would be known at the moment the prediction is made. Same point-in-time rule as M2's FeatureStore — now enforced by hand."

The classic trap: "Leakage never looks like cheating. It looks like a really good AUC. If your model is suspiciously excellent, check the timeline before you celebrate."
→ "Three ways to pick among the legal features."
Slide 7~4 minSelection families

Three selection families

Walk the three. Filter — model-free ranking against the target (mutual information, chi², ANOVA): fast, but ignores interactions. Wrapper — re-train on candidate subsets (forward, backward, RFE): accurate but slow, $O(d^2)$ fits. Embedded — selection falls out of training itself (L1 penalties, tree importance): one fit, family-specific.

Read the blue box: "Use the intersection of the three rankings as your robust core, the union as your candidate pool for tuning. A feature all three methods agree on survives almost any modelling choice."

→ "The default filter."
Slide 8~3 minMutual information

Mutual information — the default filter

Walk the formula: $I(X;Y) = H(Y) - H(Y \mid X)$ — how much the entropy of the target drops once you know the feature. Zero if and only if independent; captures non-linear dependencies that correlation misses; and invariant to monotonic reparameterisation, so it's unit-free.

"Correlation asked 'is there a straight-line relationship?' Mutual information asks 'is there any relationship?' — that's why it's the default filter."

→ "And for categorical targets, the classical choice."
Slide 9~3 minChi-squared

Chi-squared — for categorical targets

Walk the formula: observed counts against expected counts under independence. "Large $\chi^2$ means the feature's distribution differs from what independence predicts — it carries information." Point back to 2.4: "Same chi-squared you used for SRM. There it tested allocation; here it tests association. One statistic, many jobs."

→ "The Kailash engines that do the work."
Slide 10~5 minKailash engines

Kailash engines: three verbs

Walk the three bullets. FeatureSchema declares the feature contract — names, dtypes, entity id. FeatureEngineer generate()s candidates (interactions, squares, bins) and select()s the top-k. PreprocessingPipeline imputes, encodes, scales — polars-native — and is fit on training rows only.

Walk the code: build the schema for the ICU vitals, generate interaction candidates on the training frame, then select(..., target="long_stay", method="mutual_info") — again on train rows only. Then read the footer: "Ratios like the shock index are not generated for you — you write those in polars, next slides. The engine generates; the domain expert directs."

→ "Worked example, step one: enforce the timeline."
Slide 11~5 minClip to 24h

Load and clip to the first 24h

Walk the code: join vitals to admission times, compute hours_since_admit, filter to the [0, 24] window. "This filter is where point-in-time correctness is enforced. A vital at hour 48 cannot be a feature for an hour-24 prediction."

Now the honest finding — teach it plainly: "When we run this clip on the course extract, the result is alarming: of 69,421 Of 63,452 vital-sign rows, 16,435 fall inside the window — 996 of the 1,000 monitored admissions. Meds and labs are sparser (765 / 821 admissions) but real. The window is populated because the feed was fixed: an earlier cut of this dataset had event timestamps unaligned to admissions (4 of 8,000 had any in-window vitals), and this exact audit caught it. The honest sequel: with the window populated, the features carry modest real signal — full-matrix CV AUC ≈ 0.57. The data fix works; the signal is what it is. Exercise 3.1 runs the same window audit."

For experts: this is why "predict at 24h" is a data contract, not just a modelling choice. A prediction time with no usable measurements forces either a different cutoff, better instrumentation, or static-only features.
→ "Step two: collapse the series."
Slide 12~4 minAggregation

Aggregate irregular time-series

Walk the aggregation: group by admission, take mean, std, max of heart rate; mean and min of systolic BP; min of SpO₂; and the count of readings. "An irregular time-series becomes one fixed-shape row — that's what a model can eat."

Pause on the footer — it's a lovely subtlety: "n_vital_readings is informative missingness: sicker patients get monitored more. The count itself carries signal. Never drop it thinking it's just metadata."

→ "Step three: domain knowledge."
Slide 13~4 minShock index

Domain feature: shock index

Walk the formula: shock index = heart rate / systolic BP. "Clinically, a value above 1 is a red flag for early sepsis. This single derived feature often outperforms the two raw inputs — a tree can split on it in one cut instead of needing two."

Walk the one-liner: it's plain polars, hr_mean / sbp_mean. "FeatureEngineer doesn't invent ratios for you — this is the domain-derived family from slide 5, written by hand. Three lines of expert knowledge can beat a hundred generated interactions."

→ "Now select — all three families at once."
Slide 14~4 minThree selectors

Run all three selection methods

Walk the code. Filter: mutual_info_classif on the training split. Wrapper: RFE wrapping logistic regression — note the comment: scale first, LR needs comparable units. Embedded: a random forest's feature importances. Every selector fit on the training split only.

Read the footer: "The three rankings will disagree — that is the point. Each family has a blind spot; the intersection is your robust core."

→ "Three ways this all goes wrong."
Slide 15~3 minMistakes

Three expensive mistakes

Walk the three red boxes — all three are leakage wearing different clothes. One: fitting the scaler on the full dataset before splitting — test-set statistics leak into training. Two: future-looking features — discharge time, anything derived from length of stay (it defines the label), meds or labs after hour 24. Three: selecting features on the full data, then splitting — selection has already seen the test set.

"Notice the pattern: every mistake is 'something fitted before the split'. Split first. Fit everything second. In that order."

→ "Recap and bridge."
Slide 16~2 minRecap

What you can now do

Walk the bullets: why features beat algorithms, spotting leaky features on a timeline, aggregating irregular time-series, the three selection families, and the intersection as a robust subset. Tease 3.2: "You can now build and choose features. Next: how hard should the model fit them? Bias-variance, L1/L2 regularisation, and cross-validation."

→ End of Lesson 3.1. Ten-minute break.
