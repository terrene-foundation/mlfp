Speaker Notes — Lesson 6.3 Preference Alignment — DPO and GRPO

Total time: ~60 minutes  ·  20 slides  ·  ~3 min per slide average

The derivation lesson of M6: RLHF's objective has a closed-form optimum, and DPO is that solution expressed as a single loss. The practical run is Exercise 3.3 — a DPO-aligned LoRA adapter with a measured refusal-rate change, not an asserted one. The two facts students must not garble: β direction (larger β = closer to the reference) and the DPO learning rate (~5e-5 with LoRA, not the SFT 2e-4).

Slide 1~1 minTitle

Preference Alignment — DPO and GRPO

Open with the outcome: "A DPO-aligned LoRA adapter with a measured refusal-rate change. Derivation first — RLHF to DPO — then GRPO, then the evaluation harness that makes the change a number."

→ "Why SFT isn't enough."
Slide 2~3 minWhy alignment

Why alignment is not just more fine-tuning

Draw the distinction: "SFT teaches the model what to say — instruction in, response out. Alignment teaches which valid answer is better — tone, safety, helpfulness. And preferences are naturally pairwise: 'chosen vs rejected' is far easier for a rater than 'rate on a 1–10 scale'."

Read the blockquote — it lands the point: "'Explain how to restructure a derivative to avoid MAS reporting' — an accurate answer is the wrong answer. That is a preference, not a fact. No amount of SFT data fixes it."

→ "The classical three stages."
Slide 3~3 minRLHF stages

Classical RLHF — three stages

Walk the figure: SFT first; then train a reward model on human preference comparisons; then optimise the policy against the reward model with PPO — 5.8's bridge made real.

Read the caption: "Stages 2 and 3 are the painful ones — the reward model can be exploited, and PPO is unstable. Two moving parts, two failure modes. The next two slides eliminate both."

→ "The objective."
Slide 4~3 minRLHF objective

The RLHF objective

Walk the objective: maximise expected reward while paying a KL penalty for drifting from the reference (SFT) policy. "β controls how far we let the policy drift — hold that knob in mind; the whole lesson turns on it."

Read the teaser: "This constrained optimum has a closed form. That's the key insight — and it's next."

→ "The closed form."
Slide 5~4 minClosed form

Closed-form optimum

Walk the solution: $\pi^*(y|x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y|x) \exp(r_\phi(x, y)/\beta)$ — the optimal policy is the reference, reweighted exponentially by reward.

Then the rearrangement that changes everything: solve for the reward — $r_\phi = \beta\log(\pi^*/\pi_\text{ref}) + \beta\log Z(x)$. "The reward is implicitly determined by the optimal policy. We never needed a separate reward network — the policy is the reward model, in disguise."

→ "From rewards to preferences."
Slide 6~3 minBradley-Terry

Bradley-Terry preference model

Walk the model: the probability that $y_w$ beats $y_l$ is the sigmoid of their reward difference. Substitute the closed-form reward — and watch the magic: "The $\log Z(x)$ terms cancel — same on both sides. The partition function we couldn't compute simply drops out."

"What's left: the preference probability written purely in terms of the policy and the reference. No reward model anywhere in the expression."

→ "Three stages become one."
Slide 7~3 minDPO picture

DPO: from three stages to one

Walk the figure: the three-stage pipeline collapsed into a single supervised-style step — preference pairs in, aligned policy out. "SFT, reward model, PPO — replaced by one loss on preference pairs. That's Direct Preference Optimisation."

→ "The loss itself."
Slide 8~4 minDPO loss

The DPO loss

Walk the loss: negative log-sigmoid of the β-scaled difference of log-ratios — chosen versus rejected, each against the reference. "Read it as binary classification: is this pair (chosen, rejected) or (rejected, chosen)? The classifier's weights are the aligned policy's weights. No reward model. No PPO. One loss, one optimiser."

Read the blue box: "DPO is RLHF's closed-form solution expressed as a loss. You just watched the derivation — the algebra is the whole trick."

→ "Run it."
Slide 9~4 minDPO pipeline

DPO with AlignmentPipeline

Walk the config against 6.2's: method="dpo" — the real change. Same env-read base model, same LoRA config. The DPOConfig: beta=0.1, and note the learning rate — 5e-5, four times below the LoRA-SFT 2e-4. Two epochs, batch 2, max length 512.

Then the data: a 90% split of 2,000 UltraFeedback pairs — prompt, chosen, rejected. Read the footer honestly: "AI-feedback data: the chosen/rejected labels come from GPT-4 ratings, not human raters. Say so when you report results — the label provenance is part of the finding."

→ "The β knob."
Slide 10~4 minChoosing β

Choosing $\beta$

Walk the table, and pin the direction first — students reverse it: "Larger β keeps the policy closer to the reference. β is the leash length: short leash, small drift."

Then the rows: 0.01 — aggressive drift, fast; for plenty of clean preferences. 0.1 — the default; start here. 0.3 — conservative; small datasets, preserve general skills. 0.5 — very conservative; when general-capability drift is a risk. Read the footer: "Tune only if the default plateaus or eval-set general capability degrades."

→ "2024's upgrade: GRPO."
Slide 11~3 minGRPO

GRPO — 2024–25's upgrade

Walk the three bullets. DPO wastes signal: two samples, one binary label. GRPO: sample $G$ responses per prompt, score each, normalise within the group, update. Introduced in DeepSeekMath (Shao et al., 2024); later used to train DeepSeek-R1 (2025) — the common recipe for reasoning models.

"The relative ranking within a group of siblings is a richer signal than one pair's verdict — and it needs no reference reward model either."

→ "The objective."
Slide 12~4 minGRPO objective

GRPO objective

Walk the objective. The advantage: $A_i = (r_i - \mu)/(\sigma + \epsilon)$ — each response's reward std-normalised within its group, exactly as grpo_advantages() computes it. The ratio-and-clip term: PPO's clipped update, without a critic — 5.8 again. And the KL to the reference keeps the policy from collapsing onto reward hacks.

"Three pieces, each borrowed from something you already own: group-normalised advantages, PPO clipping, KL leash. GRPO is an assembly of known parts — that's why it trains stably."

→ "Which one when?"
Slide 13~3 minDPO vs GRPO

DPO vs GRPO — when to use which

Walk the table. Only pairwise preferences (human or AI-rated): DPO. Automatic scoring — unit tests, a math checker, a rubric: GRPO. Training a reasoning model with chain-of-thought rollouts: GRPO. Want simplicity and stability: DPO. Want every drop of performance and have the compute: GRPO.

→ "Judge thyself: LLM-as-judge biases."
Slide 14~4 minJudge biases

LLM-as-judge — biases to know

Walk the three biases with their mitigations. Position bias: consistently prefers response A or B — swap positions and average. Verbosity bias: longer reads as better — normalise by length or instruct for conciseness. Self-enhancement: a judge prefers its own model family's style — use a different family as judge.

Read the amber box: "Always measure your judge. Run the same pairs in both orders — any flip is position bias. Count parse failures and ties separately; never score them as 'consistent'. Exercise 3.4 uses a swap-averaged judge for exactly this reason."

→ "The standard benchmarks."
Slide 15~3 minBenchmarks

Standard benchmarks

Walk the table: MMLU (57-subject knowledge, acc), HellaSwag (commonsense completion, acc_norm), GSM8K (grade-school maths, exact_match), HumanEval (code from docstrings, pass@1). All four run in lm-eval-harness; MT-Bench is not an lm-eval task — it's LLM-judged, with the biases from the previous slide.

Read the operating rule: "Run before and after alignment. Task metrics should improve; MMLU / HellaSwag should stay flat. A drop means the policy drifted too far from the reference — raise β or train for fewer steps."

→ "The harness in code."
Slide 16~4 minlm-eval

lm-eval-harness in 10 lines

Walk the code: simple_evaluate with the HF base model plus peft= pointing at the adapter — one process evaluates the aligned model; drop the peft= for the base-line. limit=50 — "a quick before/after check, not a leaderboard claim."

Then the metric-key discipline: "Each task reports its own key — mmlu is acc,none; hellaswag is acc_norm,none; gsm8k is exact_match,strict-match. Read the task's key, never assume 'acc'. And HumanEval executes generated code, so lm-eval requires an explicit confirm_run_unsafe_code=True opt-in."

→ "Measure the actual safety win."
Slide 17~4 minSafety win

Measure the safety win

Walk the code: a keyword-based refusal detector, and one model in memory toggled with disable_adapter() — base and aligned, same weights, adapter off and on. Run the adversarial prompt set both ways and compare refusal rates.

Read the footer with emphasis: "Report the rates your run measures — there is no expected number to copy. Exercise 3.3 sets a governance gate — ≥ 80% refusal on its adversarial set — and then checks benign prompts. Keyword matching is crude: read a sample of responses too."

→ "The other direction of failure."
Slide 18~3 minBenign refusal

Sanity check: benign refusal rate

Walk the code: three plainly benign Singapore prompts — corporate tax, HDB vs condos, CPF interest — and assert zero refusals.

Read the red box: "Catastrophic refusal: the model refuses even benign prompts. Stop training or raise β — shorten the leash. Alignment that destroys helpfulness is not alignment; it's incapacitation."

→ "Four mistakes."
Slide 19~3 minMistakes

Four common mistakes

Walk the four red boxes. One: wrong LR — DPO needs much smaller than SFT: ~1e-6 for full fine-tuning, ~5e-5 with LoRA — not the 2e-4 from 6.2. Two: noisy preference labels — every flipped pair pushes the policy the wrong way; audit a sample. Three: self-enhancement judging — a judge from the same family as the model under test inflates its scores. Four: catastrophic refusal — over-aligned models refuse everything.

→ "Recap and bridge."
Slide 20~2 minRecap

What you can now do

Walk the checklist: derive the DPO loss from the RLHF objective, run DPO with AlignmentPipeline and tune β, explain GRPO and pick between them, measure the three judge biases, run lm-eval before and after alignment reading each task's own metric, and detect catastrophic refusal. Tease 6.4: "Instead of rewiring the weights, ground the model in your own documents — RAG."

→ End of Lesson 6.3. Ten-minute break.
