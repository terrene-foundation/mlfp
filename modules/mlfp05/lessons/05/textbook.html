Lesson 5.5 Generative Models: GANs and Diffusion

A generative model learns to produce new samples that look like the training data. The VAE in Lesson 5.1 already did this with a reconstruction loss plus KL regulariser. Today we meet two alternatives: the Generative Adversarial Network, which trains a generator and a discriminator in a minimax game, and the diffusion model, which noises data progressively and learns to reverse the process. You implement an MLP GAN and a WGAN-GP on a small synthetic 2‑D problem, measure mode collapse, see the convolutional DCGAN and the wider GAN family (cGAN, CycleGAN, StyleGAN), learn how FID and Inception Score evaluate generators, and get a tour of DDPM.

The GAN Setup

A GAN has two networks playing a zero-sum game:

  • A generator $G_\theta : \mathbb{R}^{d_z} \to \mathbb{R}^{D}$ maps a latent noise vector $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ to a generated sample $G(z)$.
  • A discriminator $D_\phi : \mathbb{R}^{D} \to [0, 1]$ tries to tell real samples from generated ones. It outputs the probability that its input is real.

The minimax objective

Goodfellow's 2014 paper frames it as a two-player game:

$$\min_{G} \max_{D} \; \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}}\![\log D(x)] \;+\; \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p_z}\![\log(1 - D(G(z)))]$$
$D(x)$ — prob real is real $D(G(z))$ — prob fake looks real $\min_G \max_D$ — adversary

D wants to push the first term up (recognise real) and the second up (recognise fake). G wants to push the second term down (fool D). At the optimum, if both networks are infinitely expressive and trained to convergence, $p_G = p_{\text{data}}$ — G exactly matches the true distribution.

🔵 Theory — The JS divergence connection

At the optimum of D, the GAN objective reduces to $2 \cdot \mathrm{JS}(p_{\text{data}} \| p_G) - \log 4$, where JS is the Jensen-Shannon divergence — a symmetric variant of KL. So minimising the original GAN loss with respect to G is minimising JS divergence between real and generated. JS is always finite, but that does not help: when the real and generated supports don't overlap (typical early in training, and for images that live on thin manifolds), JS saturates at the constant $\log 2$, so its gradient with respect to G's parameters is zero. The Wasserstein distance below still varies smoothly with how far apart the two distributions are — that is the motivation for WGAN.

Training dynamics

In practice you alternate:

  1. Update D: sample a real batch and a fake batch, compute the standard binary cross-entropy loss, step D's parameters.
  2. Update G: sample new fakes, compute a loss that is high when D correctly classifies them as fake, step G.

In practice the loss for G is the non‑saturating $\max \; \mathbb{E}_z[\log D(G(z))]$ (i.e. minimise $-\log D(G(z))$) rather than the minimax $\min \; \mathbb{E}_z[\log(1 - D(G(z)))]$. Both have the same fixed point, but the minimax form saturates: when D confidently rejects the fakes (early training), $\log(1 - D(G(z)))$ is flat and G gets almost no gradient. The non‑saturating form gives a gradient of about −1 with respect to D's logit in exactly that situation. The code below — and Exercise 5 — use it. It fixes the vanishing gradient of the minimax form, but not the instability from non‑overlapping supports described above.

An MLP Generator and Discriminator

For the simple 2‑D distribution of today's worked example, small MLPs are enough. (Exercise 5 also uses MLP generators and discriminators, on flattened 28×28 MNIST digits; the convolutional DCGAN is shown after the worked example.) 

import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

class Generator(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, z_dim=8, out_dim=2):
        super().__init__()
        self.net = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(z_dim, 128), nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Linear(128, 128), nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Linear(128, out_dim),
        )
    def forward(self, z):
        return self.net(z)

class Discriminator(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, in_dim=2):
        super().__init__()
        self.net = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(in_dim, 128), nn.LeakyReLU(0.2),
            nn.Linear(128, 128), nn.LeakyReLU(0.2),
            nn.Linear(128, 1),     # logit, no sigmoid
        )
    def forward(self, x):
        return self.net(x).squeeze(-1)

Discriminators traditionally use LeakyReLU (not plain ReLU) because dead neurons in D stall training for G. Leaky avoids this by letting a small gradient through for negative inputs.

The training loop

opt_g = torch.optim.Adam(G.parameters(), lr=2e-4, betas=(0.5, 0.999))
opt_d = torch.optim.Adam(D.parameters(), lr=2e-4, betas=(0.5, 0.999))

for epoch in range(200):
    for real in real_loader:
        bs = real.size(0)
        # ─── 1. Update D ─────────────────────────────────
        z = torch.randn(bs, z_dim, device=device)
        fake = G(z).detach()
        d_real = D(real)
        d_fake = D(fake)
        loss_d = F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(
            d_real, torch.ones_like(d_real)
        ) + F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(
            d_fake, torch.zeros_like(d_fake)
        )
        opt_d.zero_grad(); loss_d.backward(); opt_d.step()

        # ─── 2. Update G ─────────────────────────────────
        z = torch.randn(bs, z_dim, device=device)
        d_fake = D(G(z))
        # "non-saturating" loss: G wants D to say 1 on fakes
        loss_g = F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(
            d_fake, torch.ones_like(d_fake)
        )
        opt_g.zero_grad(); loss_g.backward(); opt_g.step()

The betas=(0.5, 0.999) is standard for GANs — momentum 0.5 is more stable than the default 0.9. The .detach() on the fake tensor when updating D stops gradients from flowing into G during D's update (we only want D's parameters to move).

Mode Collapse — the Main Failure Mode

GANs are notoriously unstable. The most famous failure is mode collapse: the generator finds one fake that fools the current discriminator and keeps producing it over and over. If the true distribution has eight Gaussian blobs in 2-D, mode collapse makes the generator emit only one of them (or at worst, a single point).

WGAN and the Wasserstein distance

Arjovsky et al. (2017) replaced JS divergence with the Wasserstein-1 (Earth Mover) distance. Instead of a probability, the "critic" (what we call D in Wasserstein land) outputs a real number; the loss is simply the difference of critic outputs on real and fake:

$$\min_{G} \max_{D} \; \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}}\![D(x)] \;-\; \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p_z}\![D(G(z))]$$
No sigmoid, no BCE Requires D to be 1-Lipschitz = Wasserstein-1 distance

The Wasserstein formulation only equals the Earth Mover distance if D is 1-Lipschitz — its outputs cannot change by more than 1 when its input changes by 1. The original WGAN enforced this by clipping D's weights to $[-0.01, 0.01]$ after each step (crude, limits model capacity). WGAN-GP (Gulrajani et al. 2017) replaced weight clipping with a gradient penalty:

$$\mathcal{L}_D = \mathbb{E}[D(G(z))] - \mathbb{E}[D(x)] + \lambda \, \mathbb{E}_{\hat{x}}\!\bigl[(\|\nabla_{\hat{x}} D(\hat{x})\|_2 - 1)^2\bigr]$$
$\hat{x}$ — random interpolation $\lambda$ — penalty weight (usually 10) target gradient norm = 1

The penalty term pushes the gradient norm of D along a random line between real and fake toward 1 — the Lipschitz constraint. Works far better than weight clipping: it preserves model capacity, trains more stably, and in practice greatly reduces (though does not guarantee against) mode collapse. 

def gradient_penalty(D, real, fake):
    """Works for any input shape: (B, 2) points or (B, C, H, W) images."""
    batch_size = real.size(0)
    alpha = torch.rand(batch_size, *[1] * (real.dim() - 1), device=real.device)
    x_hat = (alpha * real + (1 - alpha) * fake).requires_grad_(True)
    d_hat = D(x_hat)
    grads = torch.autograd.grad(
        outputs=d_hat,
        inputs=x_hat,
        grad_outputs=torch.ones_like(d_hat),
        create_graph=True,          # the penalty itself must be differentiable
    )[0]
    # norm over the WHOLE input of each sample, not per pixel or per channel
    return ((grads.reshape(batch_size, -1).norm(2, dim=1) - 1) ** 2).mean()

The flatten before the norm matters for images: grads.norm(2, dim=1) on a $(B, C, H, W)$ tensor would take a per‑pixel norm over channels and constrain the wrong thing. Exercise 5 (file 02) flattens exactly like this.

Evaluating Generative Models: FID

How do you know if your generator is any good? Human evaluation is expensive and subjective. The standard automatic metric for image generation is the Fréchet Inception Distance (FID):

$$\text{FID}(r, g) \;=\; \|\mu_r - \mu_g\|_2^2 \;+\; \text{Tr}\!\bigl(\Sigma_r + \Sigma_g - 2(\Sigma_r \Sigma_g)^{1/2}\bigr)$$
$\mu_r, \Sigma_r$ — stats of real features $\mu_g, \Sigma_g$ — stats of generated Tr — matrix trace

FID passes both real and generated images through a pre-trained Inception network, extracts a pool of activations, fits Gaussians to both, and measures the Fréchet distance between them. Lower is better; 0 means identical feature statistics. FID values are only comparable with the same feature network, image resolution and sample count — there is no universal "good" threshold. Exercise 5 (file 03) computes FID in the 64‑D feature space of a small LeNet trained on MNIST, so its numbers compare its own generators with each other, not with published Inception FIDs.

Two things FID is not. It is not a per‑image quality score (it compares whole distributions), and it is not a privacy test: a generator that memorises and replays its training images gets an excellent FID.

Inception Score (IS)

The older Inception Score (Salimans et al., 2016) uses only generated images. A classifier gives each image a class distribution $p(y \mid x)$. Good samples are confident (peaked $p(y \mid x)$) and diverse (the average over samples, $p(y)$, is spread out):

$$\text{IS} \;=\; \exp\Bigl(\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_G}\, \mathrm{KL}\bigl(p(y \mid x) \,\|\, p(y)\bigr)\Bigr)$$
1 = worst number of classes = best never looks at real data 
import numpy as np

def inception_score(probs, eps=1e-12):
    """probs: (N, K) class probabilities from a classifier, one row per generated image."""
    p_y = probs.mean(axis=0, keepdims=True)
    kl = (probs * (np.log(probs + eps) - np.log(p_y + eps))).sum(axis=1)
    return float(np.exp(kl.mean()))

print(inception_score(np.eye(10)[np.arange(1000) % 10]))       # ~10: confident AND all 10 classes
print(inception_score(np.eye(10)[np.zeros(1000, dtype=int)]))  # 1.0: confident but one class (collapse)
print(inception_score(np.full((1000, 10), 0.1)))               # 1.0: diverse but never confident

Because IS never compares with real data, it cannot tell whether the samples look like your data, and it rewards a generator that produces one perfect image per class. FID is the standard today; quote IS alongside it only with a stated classifier. (For MNIST you would use an MNIST classifier rather than Inception, as Exercise 5 does for FID; Exercise 5 does not compute IS.)

Diffusion Models in One Page

Diffusion models (DDPM, Ho et al. 2020; Stable Diffusion 2022) take a completely different approach. Instead of adversarial training, they:

  1. Forward process: progressively add Gaussian noise to a training image over $T$ steps until it is pure noise.
  2. Reverse process: train a network (usually a UNet) to predict the noise that was added at each step, so the process can be reversed.
  3. Sampling: start from pure noise, apply the reverse process $T$ times, end with a generated sample.

The training objective is simply MSE between the predicted noise and the actual noise added at each step. There is no adversary, mode collapse is rare, and sample quality is typically higher than GANs'. The cost is that sampling requires $T$ forward passes (typically 50–1000 steps), whereas a GAN samples in one forward pass. Recent distillation techniques (consistency models) bring diffusion sampling down to 1–4 steps.

🟣 Advanced — Diffusion vs GAN vs VAE decision

For high-quality image generation with large models and compute, diffusion is the current state of the art. For fast sampling, GANs are still competitive. For structured representations (where you want the latent to mean something), VAEs remain the tool of choice. For text‑to‑image generation (e.g. the open Stable Diffusion models), diffusion plus a text encoder is the dominant stack.

Worked Example: GAN vs WGAN-GP on Eight Gaussians

📝 Goal

Train the MLP GAN and a WGAN‑GP on a synthetic 2‑D "eight Gaussians on a ring" distribution and measure mode collapse: how many of the eight modes each generator covers, and how many samples land close to a real mode. A 2‑D toy is used because you can see every sample; Exercise 5 applies the same two models to MNIST digits.

Step 1 — the target distribution. 
import math
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

torch.manual_seed(42)
z_dim = 8
# Tiny MLPs run faster on the CPU than on a GPU (kernel-launch overhead dominates)

def make_eight_gaussians(n):
    angles = torch.linspace(0, 2 * math.pi, 9)[:-1]
    centers = torch.stack([torch.cos(angles), torch.sin(angles)], dim=1)
    idx = torch.randint(0, 8, (n,))
    return centers[idx] + 0.05 * torch.randn(n, 2), centers

data, centers = make_eight_gaussians(8192)

def real_batch(bs=256):
    return data[torch.randint(0, len(data), (bs,))]
Step 2 — one training function for both losses. 
def train(kind, g_steps=3000, seed=0):
    torch.manual_seed(seed)
    G, D = Generator(z_dim), Discriminator()
    if kind == "gan":      # DCGAN defaults, one D step per G step
        opt_g = torch.optim.Adam(G.parameters(), lr=2e-4, betas=(0.5, 0.999))
        opt_d = torch.optim.Adam(D.parameters(), lr=2e-4, betas=(0.5, 0.999))
        n_critic = 1
    else:                  # WGAN-GP defaults (Gulrajani et al.): 5 critic steps per G step
        opt_g = torch.optim.Adam(G.parameters(), lr=1e-4, betas=(0.5, 0.9))
        opt_d = torch.optim.Adam(D.parameters(), lr=1e-4, betas=(0.5, 0.9))
        n_critic = 5
    for step in range(g_steps):
        for _ in range(n_critic):
            real = real_batch()
            fake = G(torch.randn(real.size(0), z_dim)).detach()
            d_real, d_fake = D(real), D(fake)
            if kind == "gan":
                loss_d = (F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(d_real, torch.ones_like(d_real))
                          + F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(d_fake, torch.zeros_like(d_fake)))
            else:
                loss_d = d_fake.mean() - d_real.mean() + 10.0 * gradient_penalty(D, real, fake)
            opt_d.zero_grad(); loss_d.backward(); opt_d.step()
        d_gen = D(G(torch.randn(256, z_dim)))
        if kind == "gan":  # non-saturating generator loss
            loss_g = F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(d_gen, torch.ones_like(d_gen))
        else:              # G wants large critic values on its fakes
            loss_g = -d_gen.mean()
        opt_g.zero_grad(); loss_g.backward(); opt_g.step()
    return G

The critic loss is the difference of means plus the gradient penalty; the generator loss is the negative critic output. WGAN‑GP takes $n_{\text{critic}} = 5$ critic steps per generator step so the critic stays close to the optimal one that measures the Wasserstein distance.

Step 3 — measure mode coverage. 
def mode_report(G, n=2000):
    with torch.no_grad():
        s = G(torch.randn(n, z_dim))
    dist = torch.cdist(s, centers)                          # (n, 8) distance to each mode
    share = torch.bincount(dist.argmin(1), minlength=8).float() / n
    close = (dist.min(1).values < 0.15).float().mean().item()   # within 3 std of a mode
    return s, share, close

for seed in (0, 1, 2):
    for kind in ("gan", "wgan-gp"):
        s, share, close = mode_report(train(kind, seed=seed))
        print(seed, kind, "modes covered:", int((share >= 0.02).sum()), " near a mode:", round(close, 2))

A mode counts as covered if it attracts at least 2% of the samples (a perfect generator gives each 12.5%). Results from our CPU runs (each GAN run takes seconds; each 3,000‑step WGAN‑GP run about half a minute):

Three honest lessons. Mode collapse is seed‑dependent: the same GAN code covered all eight modes with one seed and collapsed onto two neighbouring modes (the samples sat on the arc between them, near neither) with another — and 12,000 more steps did not rescue it. WGAN‑GP is slow but keeps coverage: at 3,000 generator steps it had not converged, but at 15,000 steps it covered all eight modes on seeds 0 and 2, with samples still spread around the ring rather than tight on each mode (7–13% near a mode); the toy experiments in the WGAN‑GP paper ran for 100,000 iterations. And always measure: a single good‑looking run proves little about a GAN recipe.

DCGAN: Convolutions for Images

The DCGAN (Radford et al., 2015) is what made GANs work on images. Its recipe: the generator upsamples a noise vector with transposed convolutions; the discriminator downsamples with strided convolutions (no pooling); batch norm in both (but not on G's output or D's input); ReLU in G with a Tanh output, LeakyReLU in D. For 28×28 greyscale images such as MNIST: 

import torch
import torch.nn as nn
from torchvision import transforms

class DCGenerator(nn.Module):
    """z (100) -> 1x28x28 image in [-1, 1]."""
    def __init__(self, z_dim=100):
        super().__init__()
        self.net = nn.Sequential(
            nn.ConvTranspose2d(z_dim, 128, 7, 1, 0, bias=False),   # 1x1   -> 7x7
            nn.BatchNorm2d(128), nn.ReLU(True),
            nn.ConvTranspose2d(128, 64, 4, 2, 1, bias=False),      # 7x7   -> 14x14
            nn.BatchNorm2d(64), nn.ReLU(True),
            nn.ConvTranspose2d(64, 1, 4, 2, 1),                    # 14x14 -> 28x28
            nn.Tanh(),
        )
    def forward(self, z):
        return self.net(z.view(z.size(0), -1, 1, 1))

class DCDiscriminator(nn.Module):
    """1x28x28 image -> one logit. Drop the BatchNorm when used as a WGAN-GP critic."""
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        self.net = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Conv2d(1, 64, 4, 2, 1), nn.LeakyReLU(0.2, True),    # 28 -> 14
            nn.Conv2d(64, 128, 4, 2, 1, bias=False),               # 14 -> 7
            nn.BatchNorm2d(128), nn.LeakyReLU(0.2, True),
            nn.Conv2d(128, 1, 7, 1, 0),                            # 7  -> 1
        )
    def forward(self, x):
        return self.net(x).view(-1)                                # logit: no sigmoid

# Real images must share the generator's [-1, 1] range
to_tensor = transforms.Compose([transforms.ToTensor(), transforms.Normalize((0.5,), (0.5,))])

fake = DCGenerator()(torch.randn(16, 100))
print(fake.shape, DCDiscriminator()(fake).shape)   # torch.Size([16, 1, 28, 28]) torch.Size([16])

Two details that silently break GANs: the real data must be scaled to the generator's output range (ToTensor() alone gives $[0, 1]$, a Tanh generator gives $[-1, 1]$, and D can then separate real from fake by brightness alone), and the discriminator should end in a logit, not a sigmoid — use binary_cross_entropy_with_logits for a standard GAN and the raw value for a WGAN critic. Batch norm in a WGAN‑GP critic breaks the per‑sample gradient penalty, which is why WGAN‑GP critics use layer norm or no normalisation. Exercise 5 trains MLP generators on flattened MNIST with the same $[-1, 1]$ scaling; this DCGAN is the convolutional upgrade (the training loop is unchanged).

🎯 Drill — make the MLP GAN conditional

Modify the worked example's Generator and Discriminator so they take the mode index (0–7) as a condition. What has to change in the training loop?

Solution 
class CondGenerator(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, z_dim=8, n_classes=8):
        super().__init__()
        self.n_classes = n_classes
        self.net = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(z_dim + n_classes, 64), nn.ReLU(),
                                 nn.Linear(64, 64), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(64, 2))
    def forward(self, z, y):
        return self.net(torch.cat([z, F.one_hot(y, self.n_classes).float()], dim=1))

class CondDiscriminator(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, n_classes=8):
        super().__init__()
        self.n_classes = n_classes
        self.net = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(2 + n_classes, 64), nn.LeakyReLU(0.2),
                                 nn.Linear(64, 64), nn.LeakyReLU(0.2), nn.Linear(64, 1))
    def forward(self, x, y):
        return self.net(torch.cat([x, F.one_hot(y, self.n_classes).float()], dim=1)).squeeze(-1)

z, y = torch.randn(5, 8), torch.randint(0, 8, (5,))
print(CondDiscriminator()(CondGenerator()(z, y), y).shape)   # torch.Size([5])

The data loader must now yield each point together with its mode index (keep the idx that make_eight_gaussians draws). D scores real points with their true labels and fakes with the labels they were generated for; G is trained with the same random labels. At sampling time you pick the label, so "generate mode 3" becomes possible — and mode collapse across labels is much rarer because every label is asked for.

Synthetic Data: What Generators Can and Cannot Do

The most common business use of generative models is synthetic data: enlarging a rare class (fraudulent transactions, rare defects, rare conditions in medical images), building test data for software, or simulating scenarios (traffic, demand) for planning models. Three rules keep it honest:

  • Evaluate on real data. Synthetic examples may help a classifier train, but the only valid test set is real, held‑out data. A gain measured on synthetic test data is circular.
  • Synthetic is not private by default. GANs and diffusion models can memorise and regenerate training examples; extraction attacks on diffusion models are documented (Carlini et al., 2023). Sharing a generator trained on sensitive records is sharing those records unless the training gives a formal guarantee (e.g. differentially private training such as DP‑SGD) and the outputs pass memorisation and membership‑inference tests.
  • FID is not a privacy or compliance metric. It measures fidelity and diversity; a generator that copies its training set scores well.

Common Mistakes

⚠ Mistake 1: Forgetting .detach() on D's update

When updating D, the fake samples come from G. If you do not detach them, gradients will flow through G during D's backward pass and corrupt G's parameters. Always fake = G(z).detach() when training D.

⚠ Mistake 2: BCE on WGAN, Wasserstein on GAN

Vanilla GAN uses binary_cross_entropy_with_logits. WGAN uses plain means (no sigmoid, no BCE). Mixing them up is a rite of passage. Symptom: WGAN "loss" is meaningless numbers because you accidentally applied a sigmoid somewhere.

⚠ Mistake 3: Wrong gradient penalty target

WGAN-GP penalises the gradient norm toward 1, not toward 0. The penalty is $(\|\nabla\|_2 - 1)^2$, not $\|\nabla\|_2^2$. Both alternatives exist under other names: the one‑sided penalty of WGAN‑LP (Petzka et al., 2018) is $\max(0, \|\nabla\|_2 - 1)^2$, which only punishes norms above 1; the zero‑centred $\|\nabla\|_2^2$ penalty on real data is R1 regularisation (Mescheder et al., 2018), used with ordinary (non‑Wasserstein) GAN losses. Neither is the canonical WGAN‑GP loss.

⚠ Mistake 4: Learning rates too high

GAN training is fragile. Standard LRs for Adam are 2e-4 for both G and D. Beta_1 = 0.5 (not 0.9). These are the Radford et al. DCGAN defaults and they are a decent starting point. Do not deviate without a good reason.

Cross-References

GAN generators are structurally very similar to VAE decoders (Lesson 5.1) — they take a latent and emit a sample. The difference is the training signal: VAEs optimise reconstruction + KL; GANs optimise an adversarial game. Diffusion models descend from the denoising autoencoder (also 5.1): the forward process is just progressive corruption and the reverse process is progressive denoising. An image discriminator is a standard classifier CNN (Lesson 5.2). Module 6 moves on to language models, agents and alignment; it does not return to diffusion or FID.
