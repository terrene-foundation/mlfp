Speaker Notes — Lesson 4.7 Recommender Systems — THE PIVOT

Total time: ~90 minutes  ·  17 slides  ·  ~5 min per slide average

This lesson is the conceptual pivot of the entire course — the title says so. The recommendation content (content-based, CF, ALS) is the vehicle; the payload is one sentence: optimisation can drive feature discovery. Everything in M5's deep learning builds on that sentence. Teach the recommenders well, but never let the room lose the thread: embeddings are features that did not exist before training.

Slide 1~1 minTitle

Recommender Systems — THE PIVOT

Open with the outcome line, verbatim: "Today you'll understand that optimisation drives feature discovery. The recommenders are the story; that sentence is the lesson. Ninety minutes."

→ "The map of where we are."
Slide 2~4 minThe spectrum

The Feature Engineering Spectrum

Walk the four stations. M3: manual features — humans design them. M4.1–4.6: unsupervised discovery — clusters, components, topics — but no error signal driving them. The amber PIVOT box: today, optimisation drives feature discovery — embeddings. Then the bridge: M4.8 → M5, deep learning generalises with non-linearity plus an error signal.

"This one slide is the course's thesis. M3 engineered features by hand. M4.1–6 discovered structure but couldn't say what it was for. Today the features are learned for a task — predicting ratings. By M5 that idea has a name: representation learning."

→ "First approach: content."
Slide 3~4 minContent-based

Content-based filtering

Walk the three steps: represent each item as a feature vector (TF-IDF, metadata); the user profile is the average of items they liked; recommend the top-k by cosine similarity.

Read the green box carefully — it's the precision point students get wrong: "No other users needed, so it handles brand-new items — they have features from day one. It does not solve brand-new users — with no liked items there is no profile; fall back to popularity or onboarding questions. And it cannot discover surprises — you'll only ever be recommended more of what you already liked."

→ "Second approach: the crowd."
Slide 4~4 minCollaborative filtering

Collaborative filtering — two lookup directions

Walk the shared rating matrix, then the two panels. User-based: Alice and Bob rate films similarly (A, B, D — 5,3,4 vs 4,4,3). Bob rates Film C a 5. Recommend Film C to Alice — the lookup runs across rows, finding similar users.

Item-based: Film D gets similar ratings to Film A (Alice 4/5, Bob 3/4, Dan 5/5). Carol rates Film D a 5 and hasn't seen Film A. Recommend Film A to Carol — the lookup runs down columns, finding similar items.

"No features at all — no genres, no metadata. The ratings alone carry the structure. That's the magic CF buys, and the cold-start price it charges."

→ "The matrix itself — mostly empty."
Slide 5~3 minUser-item matrix

The user-item matrix

Walk the small matrix: users × items, ratings where they exist, question marks everywhere else. "Mostly empty — real matrices are 95%+ empty. The recommendation problem is the filling-in problem: what goes in the question marks?"

Read the amber box: "$R \approx U \cdot V^\top$ — user embeddings times item embeddings. That's the factorisation bet: the full matrix is low-rank, so a few latent factors per user and per item can reconstruct the observed entries — and extrapolate the missing ones."

→ "The objective — and the pivot."
Slide 6~5 minMF objective

Matrix factorisation objective

Walk the objective: minimise squared error on the observed entries only, plus an L2 penalty on the embeddings. Each user's embedding $\mathbf{u}_u$ and each item's embedding $\mathbf{v}_i$ is $k$-dimensional; the predicted rating is their dot product; $\lambda$ guards against overfitting the sparse observations.

Now read the amber pivot box — this is the moment of the lesson: "THE PIVOT: these embeddings did not exist before training. They are features created by optimisation. Nobody engineered a 'taste-for-action' dimension. Minimising reconstruction error invented it, because it was useful for the task. M3's features were designed. These were discovered by a loss function."

For experts: note the loss only covers observed entries — the empty cells contribute nothing. That's what separates CF factorisation from 4.3's PCA, which reconstructs a full matrix.
→ "How do you fit it? Alternate."
Slide 7~3 minALS

ALS — Alternating Least Squares

Walk the diagram: fix $U$, solve for $V$ in closed form; fix $V$, solve for $U$ in closed form; repeat until convergence. "Each half is an ordinary least-squares problem — 2.5's normal equations, one per user, one per item. Neither step can increase the objective, so it converges. Same alternating spirit as 4.2's EM — but not EM: there's no latent distribution here, just a non-convex objective made tractable by freezing half of it."

→ "Connect it back to PCA."
Slide 8~3 minPCA connection

Connection to PCA

Walk the table. PCA decomposes a feature matrix as $U\Sigma V^\top$; CF factorisation decomposes the user-item matrix as $UV^\top$. PCA reconstructs the full matrix; MF fits observed entries only. PCA outputs principal components; MF outputs user and item embeddings. Read the merged bottom row: "Low-rank structure → embeddings. 4.3's theorem and today's algorithm are the same idea wearing different data."

→ "Write it — fifteen lines."
Slide 9~5 minALS from scratch

ALS from scratch

Walk the code. Random small initialisations for $U$ and $V$, and a mask marking observed entries. Each iteration has two loops: for each user, gather the items they rated and solve the ridge system for their embedding; for each item, the mirror image. np.linalg.solve — never an explicit inverse, same as 2.5.

"Twenty iterations of this and the embeddings are real, usable features. In Exercise 7 you'll run it on 300 users × 120 SKUs and land at a holdout RMSE of about 0.56 on a 5-point scale — well under the no-skill baselines you'll compare against."

→ "Whose feedback? Explicit vs implicit."
Slide 10~3 minFeedback types

Implicit vs explicit feedback

Walk the table. Explicit: star ratings — actively provided, clear signal, but sparse. Implicit: clicks, views, purchases, dwell time — observed from behaviour, far more abundant, noisy but predictive.

Read the blue box: "SVD++ combines both. Many production systems rely mainly on implicit feedback — but remember: 'no click' is not 'dislike'. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, and modelling that distinction is a whole literature."

→ "Real systems: hybrid."
Slide 11~4 minHybrid

Hybrid recommenders

Walk the bullets. Combine content features with collaborative signals. Mitigate cold start: new items have features but no interactions; content carries them until CF takes over. Typical shape: a neural network with an embedding layer (CF) plus a feature layer (content). And the evaluation discipline: tune blend weights on a validation split; report lift on held-out ratings against no-skill baselines — global mean, popularity, random.

Read the green box: "Exercise 7: 300 users × 120 SKUs on a synthetic marketplace with a known generative model, and 10 brand-new SKUs held out as cold-start items. Content, user-CF, item-CF, and ALS against the baselines, then a validation-tuned hybrid with its measured lift. No Kailash recommender engine — numpy and polars from scratch. Most large production recommenders are hybrids."

→ "See what was learned."
Slide 12~3 minEmbedding visual

Visualising learned embeddings

Walk the t-SNE of item embeddings: electronics clusters with electronics, groceries with groceries, fashion with fashion. Read the corner note: "No labels used — structure from ratings alone."

"The optimisation never saw a category. It saw only who rated what — and the geometry of taste emerged anyway. This picture is the pivot made visible: features invented by a loss function carry real semantics."

→ "The pivot, stated once more, slowly."
Slide 13~4 minTHE PIVOT

THE PIVOT

Read the amber box aloud, slowly — this is the sentence of the module: "Matrix factorisation learns user and item embeddings by minimising reconstruction error. This is the first time you have seen optimisation drive feature discovery."

Then the bridge to 4.8: "Neural networks generalise this. Hidden-layer activations are embeddings — learned by minimising a loss, and combined non-linearly through activation functions. Today's embeddings came from one dot product. Next lesson: stacked layers of them."

→ "The family photo."
Slide 14~3 minFactorisation family

The factorisation family

Walk the table. PCA on the feature matrix (4.3). NMF on the doc-word matrix (4.6). CF factorisation on the user-item matrix (today). Neural networks on the "data matrix" — learned weights (next). Read the closing line: "Same core insight: low-rank structure → learned representations → embeddings. Four lessons, one idea, escalating generality."

→ "Four mistakes."
Slide 15~3 minMistakes

Four common mistakes

Walk the four. One: training on the full matrix — hold out test interactions and never tune on them, or your lift numbers are fiction. Two: ignoring implicit data — clicks and views far outnumber ratings. Three: too many latent factors — $k = 20$–$50$ is typical; higher overfits the sparse observations. Four: "Missing the pivot insight — if you leave thinking this lesson was about recommendations, re-read it. The recommendations are the vehicle; the vehicle is not the lesson."

→ "Connections."
Slide 16~2 minConnections

Connections

Walk the four links. 4.3: same low-rank decomposition. 4.5: discrete co-occurrence becomes continuous latent factors. 4.6: doc-word factorisation becomes user-item factorisation. 4.8: hidden layers generalise embeddings with non-linearity — the bridge to deep learning.

→ "Recap and bridge."
Slide 17~2 minRecap

What you can now do

Walk the bullets: build content-based and collaborative recommenders, implement ALS from scratch, explain how matrix factorisation learns embeddings, articulate the pivot, and visualise learned embeddings. Tease 4.8: "Hidden layers are automated feature engineering with error feedback. DL Foundations — the bridge to M5."

→ End of Lesson 4.7. Ten-minute break.
