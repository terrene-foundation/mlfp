Speaker Notes — Lesson 2.2 Parameter Estimation and Inference

Total time: ~45 minutes  ·  20 slides  ·  ~2 min per slide average

This is the densest math lesson of M2. Your pacing must be careful: derive, pause, check. Bessel's correction is where most learners lose the thread — the expansion now has its own slide, so use it. The payoff is that every statistical test in the rest of M2 has a principled foundation after today.

Slide 1~1 minTitle

Parameter Estimation and Inference

Open with: "Yesterday Bayes gave us the update loop — prior, data, posterior. Today we ask: if I have no prior, just data, what's my best guess? The answer is MLE, and as a bonus we'll prove the $n-1$ you've been typing for two weeks." Point at the agenda: MLE, MAP, CLT, Bessel, confidence intervals.

→ "Population vs sample."
Slide 2~3 minPopulation vs sample

Population vs sample

Pound this in: parameters ($\mu, \sigma^2$) are fixed but unknown. Statistics ($\bar{x}, s^2$) are random — their value changes with each sample. "The population is a ghost. We never see it. All we have is a finite sample, and inference is running the arrow backward — from the sample to the ghost."

Concrete: "There is some true mean HDB price in Singapore right now. No one knows it exactly. When I pull 30 transactions and average them, I get an estimate. Tomorrow I'll pull 30 different ones and get a slightly different estimate. Both are guesses at the same ghost."
→ "Those guesses have a distribution of their own."
Slide 3~3 minSampling distribution

The sampling distribution of $\bar{X}$

State the two facts: $E[\bar{X}] = \mu$ (unbiased) and $\text{Var}(\bar{X}) = \sigma^2 / n$ (variance shrinks with $n$). The standard error is $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ — name it, it comes back all lesson. Then the CLT adds shape: standardise $\bar{X}$ and it converges in distribution to $N(0,1)$.

For experts: mention Lindeberg–Lévy for i.i.d. and Lindeberg–Feller when the $X_i$ have different distributions. Finite variance is the key condition; infinite variance (Cauchy) breaks it.
→ "The classic picture: skewed population, Normal averages."
Slide 4~2 minCLT visualisation

CLT: the HDB example

Walk the three panels: skewed population on the left, slightly less skewed with $n=5$ averages, nearly Normal with $n=100$ averages. "HDB prices are right-skewed: lots of cheap flats, a long tail of luxury units. But if I keep averaging 100 of them, the average is Normal. This is why the CLT feels like magic."

→ "Now MLE."
Slide 5~3 minMLE intro

MLE: pick parameters that make the data likely

Say it in English first: "Given a parametric model, write down the probability of your data under particular parameter values. Then find the parameter values that make that probability as large as possible."

Show the product form of the likelihood. "Because the draws are independent, the joint density is the product of the per-point densities." Flag the numerical issue now: "A product of many tiny numbers underflows a float. The log turns products into sums — and because log is monotonic, the maximum stays in the same place."

→ "What does that peak look like?"
Slide 6~2 minLikelihood picture

MLE finds the peak of the likelihood

Walk the figure slowly. The blue dots on the number line are the observed data. The teal curve is the likelihood as a function of $\mu$ — for each candidate $\mu$, how probable is this dataset? The dashed red line marks the peak: $\hat\mu_{\text{MLE}}$.

Point out the geometry: the peak sits over the densest cluster of data points. "The data made this $\mu$ most likely. A $\mu$ far from the cluster would make the observed points improbable — low likelihood."

→ "Now let's make that computable — take the log."
Slide 7~2 minLog-likelihood

The log-likelihood

Unpack the two terms of $\ell(\mu, \sigma^2)$. The first, $-\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2)$, is normalisation — it doesn't move with $\mu$. The second, $-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum(x_i - \mu)^2$, is where $\mu$ lives — it's a scaled sum of squared residuals.

"Products became sums. Exponents became multipliers. The peak is in the same place. Now calculus works."

→ "Solve for $\hat\mu$."
Slide 8~3 minSolving for mu

Solving for $\hat\mu$

Take the partial, set to zero, solve. Walk each step: the derivative of $-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum(x_i - \mu)^2$ with respect to $\mu$ is $\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum(x_i - \mu)$. Set to zero. $\sum x_i = n\mu$. Divide by $n$. Done.

Punchline: "The MLE of the mean is the sample mean. You've been using MLE since Module 1 without knowing it."

If beginners freeze on partial derivatives: "Think of the log-likelihood as a hill with two axes — $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$. The top of the hill is where both slopes are zero. The partial tells us the slope in the $\mu$ direction; setting it to zero means 'the hill is flat in this direction'."
→ "And for $\sigma^2$ — where something goes wrong."
Slide 9~3 minSolving for sigma²

Solving for $\hat\sigma^2$

Partial with respect to $\sigma^2$, set to zero, solve. The final formula divides by $n$, not $n-1$.

Pause on the warning box. "This is the MLE of the variance — and it's biased. It systematically underestimates the truth. Why? We used the sample mean $\bar{X}$ as the centre — and $\bar{X}$ is, by construction, the value that minimises the sum of squared deviations. Centering there instead of at the unknown true $\mu$ makes the sum too small."

→ "Let's prove exactly how much too small."
Slide 10~4 minBessel expansion

Bessel's correction — the expansion

Do the expansion line by line. Write $(X_i - \bar{X})^2 = ((X_i - \mu) - (\bar{X} - \mu))^2$, expand the square, and sum. The slide has the key warning in text: the cross-term does not vanish. Because $\sum_i (X_i - \mu) = n(\bar{X} - \mu)$, the cross-term collapses to $-2n(\bar{X} - \mu)^2$, leaving $-n(\bar{X} - \mu)^2$ overall.

Then take expectations. Each $E[(X_i - \mu)^2] = \sigma^2$, $n$ of them. And $E[(\bar{X} - \mu)^2] = \text{Var}(\bar{X}) = \sigma^2/n$. Total: $n\sigma^2 - \sigma^2 = (n-1)\sigma^2$. "One $\sigma^2$ is lost — that's the whole story of $n-1$."

If beginners can't follow the algebra: "You don't need to reproduce this proof. Remember the punchline: centering on the sample mean costs one degree of freedom, so one $\sigma^2$ goes missing."
→ "So the fix is one character."
Slide 11~2 minBessel fix

Bessel's correction — the fix

Divide the expansion result by $n$ and you get $\frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2$ — biased low. Divide by $n-1$ and you get exactly $\sigma^2$ — unbiased. That is the formula in the box.

Land the practical point with the Python note on the slide: np.var(x, ddof=1) or x.var(ddof=1) gives the unbiased version. The ddof=0 default is the MLE — biased. "You've been calling ddof=1 since Module 1. Today you found out why."

→ "Let's make the intuition stick with two points."
Slide 12~2 minn-1 intuition

Why $n - 1$: the intuition

Give the two-point example from the slide. "Ten and 14. Mean is 12. Deviations are $-2$ and $2$. Squared: $4$ and $4$. Sum: $8$. Divide by $n=2$: variance 4. Divide by $n-1=1$: variance 8."

"Which is right? With only two points, how certain can you be about the spread? The $n-1$ answer — 8 — is honest. We spent one degree of freedom computing $\bar{X}$; only $n-1$ are free to vary given it."

→ "Confidence intervals."
Slide 13~4 minConfidence intervals

Confidence intervals from the CLT

"CLT says the sample mean is approximately $N(\mu, \sigma^2/n)$. The 95% central region of the standard Normal runs from $-1.96$ to $+1.96$. Solve for $\mu$: the CI is $\bar{x} \pm 1.96 \cdot s/\sqrt{n}$."

Then the small-$n$ caveat: "When $n$ is small and you estimate $\sigma$ with $s$, the ratio isn't quite Normal — it's Student's $t$ with $n-1$ degrees of freedom, fatter tails. Replace $1.96$ with the $t$-critical value."

Read the warning box aloud. "Not a probability about the parameter. A frequentist CI is a statement about the procedure, not about this one interval."

For experts: this is exactly why many practitioners prefer Bayesian credible intervals — the interpretation is cleaner when the data is fixed. Back to 2.1 for that contrast.
→ "What does '95% of procedures' look like?"
Slide 14~3 minCI simulation picture

95% CI means: ~95% of intervals capture the truth

Walk the figure. The vertical black line is the true $\mu$ — fixed, unknown. Each horizontal line is a 95% CI computed from a different random sample. Green intervals cross the truth; the red one misses. Count: 19 of 20 capture — that's 95%.

Drive the interpretation home: "The interval moves; the truth does not. Saying '95% confident' means: of all the intervals this procedure would produce across repeated samples, 95% capture the truth. Your one interval either does or doesn't — you just don't know which."

→ "And MAP bridges back to Bayes."
Slide 15~3 minMAP

MAP: MLE plus a prior

"MAP is MLE with an extra term — the log of your prior. That term acts as a regulariser, pulling the estimate toward the prior. In machine learning the same idea shows up as L2 and L1 penalties."

"Small $n$ — the prior dominates. Large $n$ — the likelihood dominates and MAP converges to MLE. This is where Bayesian and frequentist methods meet in the middle."

→ "Hands on the keyboard."
Slide 16~3 minGDP worked example

Worked: Singapore GDP growth (closed-form)

Walk the code. Load the economic indicators via MLFPDataLoader, pull gdp_growth_pct, drop nulls, convert to numpy. Compute the MLE variance (ddof=0) and the Bessel-corrected variance (ddof=1). Their ratio prints as $n/(n-1)$ — "Bessel's correction in numbers. You can feel it now."

→ "And then the same thing with a numerical optimiser."
Slide 17~4 minNumerical MLE

Worked: Numerical MLE with scipy

Walk the code. Define the negative log-likelihood — scipy minimises, so we negate. Note the parameterisation trick on the slide: wrap $\sigma$ as $e^{\log\sigma}$ so the optimiser ranges freely over $\mathbb{R}$ while $\sigma$ stays positive. Call minimize with BFGS. Compare: mu_fit matches g.mean(), and exp(log_s_fit) matches the biased std.

"Why do this for the Normal, where we have a closed form? Because the next model — logistic regression — has no closed form. You'll reach for exactly this pattern in 2.6."

For experts: BFGS approximates the Hessian — the inverse Hessian at the MLE is the asymptotic covariance of the parameters. That's where standard errors come from.
→ "Building the CI from this sample."
Slide 18~2 min95% CI

95% CI from the sample

Walk the code. Standard error $= s/\sqrt{n}$ with ddof=1. Look up the $t$-critical value at 0.975 with $n-1$ degrees of freedom. Construct and print the interval.

Then the simulation check from the slide footer: "Run 1,000 fake samples, count how many CIs contain the truth. You should get about 950. That is what '95% confidence' actually means — the slide-14 picture, in code."

→ "One last thing: when does MLE break?"
Slide 19~2 minMLE failure modes

When MLE fails

Walk the four rows of the table. Small $n$ → huge variance; fix with MAP and a prior. Multimodal likelihood → local optimum; fix with multiple restarts. Misspecified model → confident garbage; fix with goodness-of-fit checks. Heavy tails → outliers dominate; fix with a Student-$t$ likelihood.

"MLE is not magic. When the model is right, it's optimal. When the model is wrong, MLE hands you a very precise number that is also very wrong."

→ "Recap and bridge."

What you can now do
