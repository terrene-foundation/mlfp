Lesson 6.4 RAG Systems

Your aligned LLM is capable and safe but still has two fatal flaws: it does not know anything that post-dates its training cutoff, and it does not know anything that was private to your organisation. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) fixes both in one architectural move — fetch relevant facts from a search index and inject them into the prompt as context. In this lesson you will build a complete RAG pipeline on the Exercise 4 corpus — 1,000 documents sampled from the public neural-bridge/rag-dataset-12000 collection, each with one question and reference answer about it — compare BM25, dense, and hybrid retrieval, measure retrieval with hit@k, evaluate answers with the four RAGAS metrics, and test whether the HyDE trick helps on this corpus. The same pipeline works unchanged on a Singapore corpus such as public MAS notices or PDPC guidance — swap the documents in.

Why RAG Beats Fine-Tuning for Knowledge

Fine-tuning teaches the model new skills and new styles. It is the wrong tool for teaching the model new facts. The reasons are practical:

  • Freshness: new regulations, product launches, policy changes happen daily. Retraining daily is absurd. Re-indexing daily is trivial.
  • Attribution: RAG can cite the source chunk that produced an answer. Fine-tuning blurs facts into weights with no traceability.
  • Cost: indexing is one embedding pass per chunk. Fine-tuning on the same corpus means GPU training runs — and repeating them every time the facts change.
  • Privacy: the documents stay in your index; only the few retrieved snippets go into the prompt. With the course's local Ollama models nothing leaves your machine at all; with a hosted model only those snippets reach the provider.

The trade-off: RAG pipelines are more complex to operate (indexing, retrieval tuning, evaluation) and retrieval errors cascade directly into generation errors.

The RAG Pipeline in Five Stages

Stage 1: Chunking

You cannot usefully retrieve a 200-page regulatory guideline as one unit — it would not even fit in the prompt. You retrieve chunks. A chunk is a contiguous window of text small enough to fit comfortably in the prompt and focused enough that its meaning is self-contained.

Chunk size trade-offs

Chunk sizeProsCons
Small (~100 tokens)Precise retrieval, many chunks fit in contextOften lacks full context for a claim
Medium (~500 tokens)Good default — balance of context and precisionSometimes splits a single thought across chunks
Large (~1500 tokens)Self-contained — whole sectionsRetrieval is coarse, prompts get expensive

The overlap trick

A fixed-size chunker splits text at arbitrary token boundaries — which means a key sentence can end up cut in half. The fix is overlap: each chunk shares its last $k$ tokens with the start of the next chunk. Typical overlap is 10–20% of the chunk size (e.g., 50 tokens of overlap on a 500-token chunk). Every sentence that crosses a chunk boundary ends up fully inside at least one chunk. 

def chunk_text(text: str, chunk_size: int = 500, overlap: int = 50) -> list[str]:
    """Split text into overlapping chunks of `chunk_size` whitespace words.

    Words are a rough proxy for tokens — for production, count with the
    embedder's own tokenizer so chunks match the model's view of length.
    """
    tokens = text.split()
    chunks: list[str] = []
    step = chunk_size - overlap
    for i in range(0, len(tokens), step):
        window = tokens[i : i + chunk_size]
        if len(window) < 20:     # skip tail fragments shorter than 20 tokens
            break
        chunks.append(" ".join(window))
    return chunks

Smarter chunking

The naive fixed-size chunker is good enough to get started. Two upgrades you should know about:

  • Sentence-boundary aware: split on sentence-ending punctuation and pack sentences into chunks until you hit the target size. Prevents sentence fragments.
  • Semantic chunking: use an embedding model to detect topic shifts and put chunk boundaries where topics change. Slow at indexing time, pays off in retrieval quality.

Exercise 4.1 (ex_4/01_chunking_strategies.py) compares four strategies on the course corpus — fixed-size (500 characters with 100 characters of overlap), sentence-packed, paragraph and semantic — and the later Exercise 4 files index the corpus with the sentence-packed chunker shown in the worked example below (sentences packed into chunks of at most 500 characters).

Stage 2: Sparse Retrieval with BM25

Before embeddings, retrieval was done with keyword scoring. BM25 is the classic, and it still works remarkably well — often better than embeddings on tasks with rare terms (names, numbers, acronyms) that embedding models fail to distinguish.

BM25 scores a document $d$ against a query $q$ as a sum over query terms of three factors: how rare the term is across the corpus, how frequent it is in the document, and a length correction.

$$\text{BM25}(q, d) \;=\; \sum_{t \in q} \text{IDF}(t) \cdot \frac{f(t, d) \cdot (k_1 + 1)}{f(t, d) + k_1 \cdot \left(1 - b + b \cdot \frac{|d|}{\bar{|d|}}\right)}$$
IDF(t) — inverse document frequency of $t$ $f(t, d)$ — term frequency in $d$ $|d|$ — document length $\bar{|d|}$ — average document length $k_1 \approx 1.5$ — term saturation $b \approx 0.75$ — length norm

Where:

$$\text{IDF}(t) \;=\; \log\!\left(\frac{N - n_t + 0.5}{n_t + 0.5} + 1\right)$$

$N$ is the total number of chunks in the corpus and $n_t$ is the number of chunks containing term $t$. Rare terms dominate; common terms like "the" contribute near zero.

🔵 Theory — why BM25 still wins on some queries

Dense embeddings compress meaning into a fixed-length vector (typically 768 or 1024 floats). They are magnificent at semantic similarity but they struggle with exact-match queries like "section 4.3.2 of MAS Notice 626" where the right answer depends on the precise term. BM25 retrieves by exact token match and handles this effortlessly. The standard production pattern is hybrid retrieval: run both, combine the scores, enjoy the best of both worlds.

Stage 3: Dense Retrieval with Embeddings

Dense retrieval replaces token matching with semantic similarity. You embed each chunk into a high-dimensional vector, embed the query into the same space, and retrieve the chunks whose vectors are closest to the query's.

The distance metric is almost always cosine similarity:

$$\text{cosine}(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) \;=\; \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\| \, \|\mathbf{v}\|}$$
$\mathbf{u}$ — query embedding $\mathbf{v}$ — chunk embedding range: $[-1, 1]$ 1 = identical direction

Cosine similarity measures the angle between two vectors — it is invariant to magnitude. Two embeddings that are pointing the same direction have cosine 1, regardless of length. This is the right metric for embeddings because the meaning is encoded in direction, not magnitude. 

import math

def cosine_similarity(u: list[float], v: list[float]) -> float:
    dot = sum(a * b for a, b in zip(u, v))
    nu = math.sqrt(sum(a * a for a in u))
    nv = math.sqrt(sum(b * b for b in v))
    return dot / (nu * nv + 1e-12)


class VectorStore:
    """A minimal in-memory vector store — good enough for teaching."""

    def __init__(self):
        self.chunks: list[str] = []
        self.embeddings: list[list[float]] = []
        self.metadata: list[dict] = []

    def add(self, chunk: str, embedding: list[float], meta: dict):
        self.chunks.append(chunk)
        self.embeddings.append(embedding)
        self.metadata.append(meta)

    def search(self, query_embedding: list[float], k: int = 5) -> list[tuple[float, str, dict]]:
        scored = [
            (cosine_similarity(query_embedding, emb), chunk, meta)
            for emb, chunk, meta in zip(self.embeddings, self.chunks, self.metadata)
        ]
        scored.sort(reverse=True, key=lambda x: x[0])
        return scored[:k]

For real systems use a dedicated vector database (pgvector, Qdrant, FAISS) that can handle millions of chunks with approximate nearest neighbour search. The in-memory version above is enough to teach the mechanics and small enough to debug — the course ships the same idea as shared.mlfp06.ex_4.DenseVectorStore, whose search(query_embedding, top_k) returns dicts with text, score and metadata. The vectors come from the course's local Ollama embedding model (make_embedder(), default nomic-embed-text, 768 dimensions), never from a chat model.

Stage 4: Hybrid Retrieval

BM25 and dense retrieval have complementary failure modes. BM25 misses paraphrases ("credit exposure" vs "counterparty risk"). Dense retrieval misses exact identifiers ("MAS Notice 626"). A hybrid combines both. The problem is that their scores live on different scales — BM25 is unbounded, cosine similarity lives in $[-1, 1]$ — so you cannot simply add them.

Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF; Cormack, Clarke and Büttcher, 2009) sidesteps the scale problem by throwing the scores away and fusing ranks:

$$\text{RRF}(d) \;=\; \sum_{r \in \text{retrievers}} \frac{1}{k + \text{rank}_r(d)}$$
$\text{rank}_r(d)$ — position of $d$ in retriever $r$'s list (1 = top) $k = 60$ — damping constant from the original paper

A chunk ranked near the top by both retrievers collects two large terms and rises; a chunk only one retriever likes still scores, but less. Exercises 4.4 and 4.5 use RRF with $k = 60$.

The alternative is a weighted score blend: min–max normalise both score lists to $[0, 1]$, then $\text{score}_\text{hybrid} = \alpha \cdot \text{score}_\text{dense} + (1 - \alpha) \cdot \text{score}_\text{BM25}$. It gives you a dial ($\alpha$) but the result depends on how each score distribution happens to be shaped, so $\alpha$ must be tuned on a held-out set for every corpus. RRF needs no tuning, which is why it is the common default.

Re-ranking with a cross-encoder

After retrieval, you often have ~20 candidates and need to pick the top 5 for the prompt. A cross-encoder re-ranker scores each (query, chunk) pair jointly — it takes both inputs through a transformer and outputs a single relevance score. This is more accurate than cosine similarity (because the model can attend between query and chunk tokens) but far too slow to use on an entire corpus. The standard pattern is retrieve 20 with dense + BM25, re-rank with a cross-encoder, keep top 5 for the prompt. Exercise 4.5 has no dedicated cross-encoder model available offline, so it uses the local chat model as a stand-in: it asks the LLM to rate each (query, passage) pair 0–10 and re-sorts the top 10 hybrid candidates. A reply with no number raises an error rather than defaulting to a neutral score — a silent default would look like a real judgement.

Stage 5: HyDE — Hypothetical Document Embeddings

Here is a clever trick. User queries are often short and sparse ("How is customer NRIC handled under PDPA?"), while the documents they want to retrieve are long and specific ("Personal data protection obligations include consent requirements before collecting NRIC numbers from individuals..."). The embeddings of short queries and long documents live in different parts of the vector space.

HyDE (Gao et al. 2022) fixes this by asking an LLM to generate a hypothetical answer to the query first, then embedding the hypothetical answer instead of the raw query.

  1. User asks: "How is customer NRIC handled under PDPA?"
  2. LLM writes a hypothetical answer (without retrieval): "Under PDPA, organisations must obtain consent before collecting NRIC numbers, and can only collect them when required by law..."
  3. Embed the hypothetical answer, not the query.
  4. Retrieve. The hypothetical answer is long and specific and lives in the same neighbourhood as the real PDPA text.
  5. Generate the final answer from the real retrieved chunks.
🟢 HyDE in one sentence

"Hallucinate the answer first so that the embedding of the hallucination can retrieve the real answer." The LLM does not need to hallucinate correctly — it just needs to hallucinate in the right neighbourhood.

HyDE costs one extra LLM call per query, and it is not a guaranteed win: when the queries already share vocabulary with the documents (as the Exercise 4 questions largely do — they were written about each document), plain dense retrieval may do as well or better. Exercise 4.5 therefore measures it, scoring BM25, dense, hybrid and HyDE on the same evaluation questions rather than assuming an improvement.

Evaluating RAG with RAGAS

"Does the system work?" is a useless question. RAG quality has at least four independent axes, and a good pipeline should be measured on all of them. The RAGAS framework names and scores them:

MetricQuestionDiagnoses
Faithfulness Does the answer follow from the retrieved context? Hallucination — answer invents facts not in the sources
Answer relevance Does the answer actually address the question? Off-topic answers, verbose padding
Context relevance Is the retrieved context actually relevant to the question? Retrieval misses — wrong chunks fetched
Context recall Does the retrieved context contain all the needed information? Retrieval gaps — relevant chunks not fetched

Each metric is scored by an LLM judge in [0, 1]. Run RAGAS on a small gold-labelled set of questions and expected answers. When one metric drops, you know exactly where to look: low faithfulness means the generator is hallucinating; low context relevance means retrieval is fetching the wrong chunks; low context recall means retrieval is missing the right chunks.

How the course computes them. The ragas library itself cannot be imported in the course environment (one of its dependencies fails to import), so Exercise 4.5 implements the same four metrics directly as LLM-as-judge prompts to the local Ollama model (compute_ragas_metrics in ex_4/05_rerank_rag_pipeline.py), each returning a number in [0, 1]. Two rules make a judge-based metric trustworthy: a judge reply with no number must raise, never default to 0.5; and a small local judge is noisy, so read the scores as a diagnostic shape across a handful of questions, not as a precise benchmark.

Retrieval metrics: hit@k and MRR

The RAGAS metrics need an LLM judge. Retrieval can also be scored without one, whenever you know which document answers each question — and in the Exercise 4 corpus you do, because question $i$ was written about document $i$.

  • hit@k — the fraction of questions whose source document appears in the top $k$ results. With exactly one relevant document per question this is the same number as recall@k. (Precision@k is not informative here: with one relevant document it can never exceed $1/k$.)
  • MRR (mean reciprocal rank) — the average of $1/\text{rank}$ of the source document (0 if it is not retrieved), which rewards putting it first rather than fifth.

The course's LLMObservatory computes both: obs.retrieval.compare_retrievers(retrievers, eval_set, k=5) takes a dict of retrievers — each a callable (query, k) → [(doc_id, text, score), ...] — and an evaluation set of {"query": ..., "relevant_ids": [...]} rows, and returns a leaderboard DataFrame with recall_at_k, precision_at_k, mrr and ndcg_at_k per retriever.

🟣 Advanced — why separate context relevance from recall

Context relevance asks "are the chunks we fetched useful?". Context recall asks "did we fetch everything we needed?". You can have high relevance and low recall (fetched chunks are great, but we missed three others), or high recall and low relevance (we got everything, plus a pile of junk). Fixing these two failures requires different tools — relevance improves with re-ranking, recall improves with broader retrieval (more $k$, hybrid, query expansion).

import re
from shared.mlfp06.ex_4 import load_rag_corpus, split_corpus


def chunk_sentence(text: str, max_chunk_chars: int = 500) -> list[str]:
    """Pack whole sentences into chunks of at most max_chunk_chars characters."""
    sentences = re.split(r"(?<=[.!?])\s+", text)
    chunks, current = [], ""
    for sent in sentences:
        if len(current) + len(sent) + 1 > max_chunk_chars and current:
            chunks.append(current.strip())
            current = sent
        else:
            current = current + " " + sent if current else sent
    if current.strip():
        chunks.append(current.strip())
    return [c for c in chunks if c]

Step 3.

Implement BM25 over the same chunks.

import math
import re
from collections import Counter

class BM25Index:
    def __init__(self, chunks: list[str], k1: float = 1.5, b: float = 0.75):
        self.chunks = chunks
        self.k1, self.b = k1, b
        self.tokens = [re.findall(r"\w+", c.lower()) for c in chunks]
        self.lengths = [len(t) for t in self.tokens]
        self.avg_len = sum(self.lengths) / len(self.lengths)
        # document frequency
        self.df: Counter[str] = Counter()
        for toks in self.tokens:
            for t in set(toks):
                self.df[t] += 1
        self.N = len(chunks)

    def idf(self, term: str) -> float:
        n_t = self.df.get(term, 0)
        return math.log((self.N - n_t + 0.5) / (n_t + 0.5) + 1)

    def score(self, query: str, idx: int) -> float:
        q_terms = re.findall(r"\w+", query.lower())
        doc_toks = self.tokens[idx]
        doc_len = self.lengths[idx]
        tf = Counter(doc_toks)
        score = 0.0
        for t in q_terms:
            f = tf.get(t, 0)
            if f == 0:
                continue
            num = f * (self.k1 + 1)
            den = f + self.k1 * (1 - self.b + self.b * doc_len / self.avg_len)
            score += self.idf(t) * num / den
        return score

    def search(self, query: str, top_k: int = 5) -> list[dict]:
        scored = [(self.score(query, i), i) for i in range(self.N)]
        scored.sort(reverse=True)
        return [{"text": self.chunks[i], "score": s, "chunk_idx": i}
                for s, i in scored[:top_k]]


bm25 = BM25Index(chunk_texts)
Step 4.

Build the hybrid retriever with Reciprocal Rank Fusion: take twice as many candidates from each retriever, fuse by rank.

def dense_search(q_emb: list[float], top_k: int = 5) -> list[dict]:
    return [{"text": r["text"], "score": r["score"], "chunk_idx": r["metadata"]["chunk_idx"]}
            for r in store.search(q_emb, top_k=top_k)]


def reciprocal_rank_fusion(ranked_lists: list[list[dict]], k: int = 60) -> list[dict]:
    fused: dict[int, float] = {}
    texts: dict[int, str] = {}
    for ranked in ranked_lists:
        for rank, item in enumerate(ranked, start=1):
            idx = item["chunk_idx"]
            texts[idx] = item["text"]
            fused[idx] = fused.get(idx, 0.0) + 1.0 / (k + rank)
    order = sorted(fused.items(), key=lambda x: x[1], reverse=True)
    return [{"text": texts[i], "score": s, "chunk_idx": i} for i, s in order]


def hybrid_search(query: str, q_emb: list[float], top_k: int = 5) -> list[dict]:
    return reciprocal_rank_fusion(
        [dense_search(q_emb, top_k * 2), bm25.search(query, top_k * 2)]
    )[:top_k]

Advanced RAG Patterns

The pipeline above answers single-fact questions over a flat pile of chunks. Three patterns extend it when that is not enough.

Metadata filtering

Store structured fields with every chunk — source document, date, section, access level — and filter on them before the similarity search ("only notices issued after 2024", "only documents this user may see"). It is the cheapest precision win in RAG: a filter removes whole categories of wrong answers that no embedding can tell apart, and it is also where access control belongs — never rely on the LLM to ignore a chunk it should not have seen. 

def filtered_search(q_emb: list[float], allowed_docs: set[str], top_k: int = 5) -> list[dict]:
    """Dense search restricted to chunks whose doc_id passes the filter."""
    hits = store.search(q_emb, top_k=len(store.documents))      # score everything
    return [h for h in hits if h["metadata"]["doc_id"] in allowed_docs][:top_k]


# e.g. a user who may only see the first five documents
allowed = set(doc_ids[:5])
for h in filtered_search(query_embeddings[0], allowed, top_k=3):
    print(h["metadata"]["doc_id"], round(h["score"], 3))

This filters after scoring, which is fine for 40 documents. A vector database applies the filter inside the index so it never scores the excluded chunks at all.

Multi-hop retrieval

Some questions need two facts from two documents: "Which country was the director of film X born in?" needs the film's page (to find the director) and then the director's page. One retrieval with the original question rarely finds the second page, because the question never names the director. Multi-hop retrieval loops: retrieve, ask the LLM what fact is still missing, retrieve again with that follow-up query, and answer from the union of both contexts. Lesson 6.5 meets this question type in the HotpotQA dataset, where it is handled by letting a ReAct agent decide when to search again. 

from shared.mlfp06._ollama_bootstrap import make_delegate, run_delegate_text
from shared.mlfp06.ex_4 import generate_embedding


async def two_hop_context(question: str, top_k: int = 3) -> str:
    first = store.search(await generate_embedding(question), top_k=top_k)
    ctx1 = "\n".join(h["text"] for h in first)
    follow_up, _, _ = await run_delegate_text(
        make_delegate(temperature=0.0),
        "Context:\n" + ctx1 + "\n\nQuestion: " + question +
        "\n\nWrite ONE short search query for the fact still missing to answer "
        "the question. If nothing is missing, reply exactly NONE.",
    )
    if follow_up.strip().upper() == "NONE":
        return ctx1
    second = store.search(await generate_embedding(follow_up), top_k=top_k)
    seen = {h["text"] for h in first}
    return ctx1 + "\n" + "\n".join(h["text"] for h in second if h["text"] not in seen)

Document summaries (hierarchical retrieval)

Index one LLM-written summary per document alongside its chunks. Broad questions ("what is this report about?") match summaries; narrow ones match chunks. A common two-stage form retrieves the best documents by summary, then searches only those documents' chunks — metadata filtering again, with the filter chosen by the first stage.

Solution 
q7 = query_embeddings[7]
unfiltered = {h["metadata"]["doc_id"] for h in store.search(q7, top_k=5)}
filtered = {h["metadata"]["doc_id"] for h in filtered_search(q7, set(doc_ids[:5]), top_k=5)}
print("source in unfiltered top-5:", doc_ids[7] in unfiltered)
print("source in filtered top-5:  ", doc_ids[7] in filtered)   # always False
assert filtered <= set(doc_ids[:5])

The filtered result can only contain allowed documents, so the second line prints False whatever the embeddings are; the first line is True whenever dense retrieval finds the source at all. The filter enforces the boundary in code — the LLM never sees the excluded text.

Common Mistakes

⚠ Mistake 1: Chunks too big

A 2000-token chunk is easy to index but produces coarse retrieval — the user's question matches one sentence out of 2000 tokens and you pollute the prompt with 1999 irrelevant tokens. Start at 500, go smaller only if the system keeps retrieving almost-relevant chunks.

⚠ Mistake 2: Pure dense retrieval on rare-term queries

"What does MAS Notice 626 say about data governance?" The exact string "MAS Notice 626" is rare and specific. Dense embeddings compress it into a generic "regulatory document" signal and the retriever pulls the wrong notices. Always run hybrid on technical corpora.

⚠ Mistake 3: No RAGAS evaluation

Without faithfulness and context relevance scores you cannot diagnose which stage is failing. A full retrieval-augmented pipeline has 5+ failure modes, and "the answer is wrong" tells you nothing about which one.

⚠ Mistake 4: Stale index

You indexed a regulator's guidelines a month ago. Last week the regulator published a revision. Your RAG system confidently cites the old version. Set up an index refresh schedule and include a "last indexed" timestamp in your answer's footer.

🎯 Exercise B — RRF versus a weighted blend

Implement the weighted blend (min–max normalise the dense and BM25 scores over all chunks, then $\alpha \cdot \text{dense} + (1 - \alpha) \cdot \text{BM25}$) as a fourth retriever and sweep $\alpha \in \{0.0, 0.3, 0.6, 0.9, 1.0\}$. $\alpha = 0$ is pure BM25, $\alpha = 1$ is pure dense. Does any $\alpha$ beat RRF's MRR, and is the best $\alpha$ closer to dense or sparse on this corpus?

Solution 
def normalise(scores: list[float]) -> list[float]:
    lo, hi = min(scores), max(scores)
    return [0.0] * len(scores) if hi - lo < 1e-9 else [(s - lo) / (hi - lo) for s in scores]


def blend_search(query: str, q_emb: list[float], alpha: float, top_k: int = 20) -> list[dict]:
    dense = {h["metadata"]["chunk_idx"]: h["score"] for h in store.search(q_emb, top_k=len(chunk_texts))}
    d = normalise([dense[i] for i in range(len(chunk_texts))])
    b = normalise([bm25.score(query, i) for i in range(len(chunk_texts))])
    mixed = sorted(range(len(chunk_texts)), key=lambda i: alpha * d[i] + (1 - alpha) * b[i], reverse=True)
    return [{"text": chunk_texts[i], "score": alpha * d[i] + (1 - alpha) * b[i], "chunk_idx": i}
            for i in mixed[:top_k]]


for alpha in (0.0, 0.3, 0.6, 0.9, 1.0):
    retrievers[f"blend_{alpha}"] = (
        lambda q, k, a=alpha: to_docs(blend_search(q, q_emb_of[q], a))[:k]
    )
print(obs.retrieval.compare_retrievers(retrievers, eval_set, k=5).select("retriever", "recall_at_k", "mrr"))

The a=alpha default argument freezes each value of $\alpha$ inside its lambda (without it every lambda would see the last $\alpha$). The leaderboard is sorted by MRR; the blend_0.0 and blend_1.0 rows should match (up to tie-breaking) the bm25 and dense rows, which is a useful sanity check. If the best blend only ties RRF, RRF wins on simplicity — it has nothing to tune.

Reflection

You now have a complete RAG pipeline: sentence-aligned chunks, BM25 for exact match, dense embeddings for semantics, Reciprocal Rank Fusion to combine them, a re-ranker, hit@k and MRR to measure retrieval, and the four RAGAS metrics to tell you which stage is failing. Production systems use the same architecture with a real vector database, a dedicated cross-encoder and a much larger evaluation set.

More importantly, you now know the axis where RAG wins and where it loses. It wins on fresh knowledge, private corpora, and attribution — the three things fine-tuning cannot give you. It loses on style, tone, and task shape — which is exactly where Lessons 6.2 and 6.3 came in. A serious production stack uses both: fine-tune for behaviour, RAG for facts.
